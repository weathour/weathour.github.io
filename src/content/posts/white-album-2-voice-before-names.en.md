Haruki thought he was playing one last song.

The light music club had fallen apart. He had never been among the members due to perform onstage; now even his small part in organizing things and practising on his own was coming to an end. He would play a favourite, “WHITE ALBUM,” as a farewell. The piano next door joined in as usual. The player, whose identity he had yet to discover, was remarkably skilled, yet willing to accompany his clumsy guitar. Whether this was kindness, teasing or sheer boredom, Haruki could not say.[^voice]

This time, a singing voice joined them.

Haruki ran to the rooftop and found Setsuna Ogiso. Embarrassed at first, she asked him to forget what he had heard; as they talked, she could not help admitting how much she loved the song. Haruki had almost said goodbye. He had assembled every reason not to invite her: she disliked attracting attention, so why would she agree to sing onstage? In the end, he turned back and asked whether she would join the club.[^rooftop]

A song meant to finish things could also begin them again.

In *WHITE ALBUM 2*'s *Introductory Chapter*, hereafter IC, Haruki Kitahara, Setsuna Ogiso and Kazusa Touma grow closer through playing together, until they reach a point where they can no longer go on as before. Read backwards from the airport, every early approach can look like a portent of disaster. Yet at the time, they were doing things: learning to play, trying to sing, finding a way to make an impossible performance happen. The life they would long for acquired its substance in these activities. What they later lost becomes visible here, too.[^source]

## Someone Through the Wall Who Will Wait

Haruki first noticed the accompaniment next door about two months earlier. The other members had gone, and he stayed to practise alone, stumbling along. Once something resembling a tune emerged, the piano would arrive. Sometimes it sounded like instruction, sometimes a challenge. Later came bass, drums and saxophone. What astonished him was that the other player never made a mistake. He concluded that this person must have plenty of spare time.[^voice]

He could hear the skill. Before seeing the work behind those sounds, however, he had already found a word for the person making them: “idle.”

The official digital novel turns to Kazusa's side of those days. She had taught Haruki guitar face to face and had initially told him he was not trying hard enough. What she could learn in a night still troubled him after long practice. As she taught him, though, she began to realize how many years of experience with instruments lay beneath her “one night.” What Haruki could grasp in his mind, his fingers might not manage; what Kazusa's fingers did naturally, she might struggle to explain. Each had a kind of proficiency, and each a clumsiness that became apparent only in the other's presence.[^teaching]

The problem of teaching changed. Blaming him for being unable to play would not make him able; demonstrating something once did not mean he could reproduce it. Kazusa began to consider how this particular person might learn enough to perform in the time remaining. She moved equipment into the second music room. When the guitar sounded next door, she met it with her own playing. If he rushed, she pulled him back; if he lagged, she waited; if a note strayed, she guided him towards where it belonged. She began with the familiar piano, then switched to other band instruments so that he could gradually get used to them.[^teaching]

There was already something precise in this understanding. Kazusa could hear how he went wrong, and Haruki could adjust his playing within her accompaniment. But the understanding concerned rhythm, pitch and coordination. He did not know how the person next door had worried over teaching him, much less how much this waiting mattered in her days. A later account lets the reader see that dimension. It does not let Haruki see it at the time.

In the “Zhiyin” chapter of *The Literary Mind and the Carving of Dragons*, Liu Xie writes that one understands music only after practising a thousand tunes. He begins with the work of discernment: listen only according to your preferences, and what you hear will still be yourself. Liu Xie goes on to trust that a reader can pass through a work into its author's feelings, until even what is hidden becomes apparent. Between Kazusa and Haruki, that confidence carries us only part of the way. They can understand how the next notes should follow. Why the other is so patient, or why they themselves look forward to the next session, remains difficult to express and sometimes has yet to become clear even to them.[^zhiyin]

This understanding has not reached the whole of their feelings, but it already lets them do something together. Neither person across the wall can give a complete account of the other. The guitar nevertheless sounds better than before.

A duet after their examinations captures this particularly well. Hearing Haruki play “WHITE ALBUM,” Kazusa notices his improvement since before the exams: the notes hold together, and his hands show the results of solid practice. Ordinarily she would correct his overexcited tempo. That day, she follows it. She plays faster, and so does he; she speeds up again, and he struggles to keep pace. Eventually the song is too fast and too heavy, with little of its original character left. Kazusa is delighted.[^joy]

Her pleasure has gone beyond teaching a student well. Someone she is accustomed to leading has begun to draw her forward. She, too, sets aside how the piece ought to be played for a while, allowing their present excitement to change the performance. The earlier work has mattered: Haruki's growing skill sustains the chase. As Kazusa follows his enthusiasm, she also hears him finding the right notes in the accelerating rhythm. In this exchange, each becomes able to do a little more.

Kazusa's own days change as well. Someone who does not want to be seen playing has spent considerable time considering how another person might stand before an audience. Of course there is love, and a need she can hardly express. Here, however, that love takes the form of preparing equipment, devising ways to teach, hearing an improvement, and allowing herself to join him in making a mess of a good song. Call all of this concealment, and we erase the most living part of their relationship.

## The Third Person Has a History of Her Own

When Setsuna joins, she encounters a bond with a past that has yet to be clearly spoken of. Haruki enthusiastically tells her how Kazusa teaches him. Suddenly she asks: when he is with one girl, does he always talk about another? Haruki feels unfairly accused. She asked, and he answered.[^women]

He has answered what she asked about. He has not yet heard how she is asking.

Setsuna soon seeks Kazusa out herself. She accompanies her shopping, sees her making a dinner of hamburgers, and advises her to add a salad. Kazusa complains that she sounds exactly like Haruki. When men approach them in the street, each says it is the other they are interested in. Setsuna even compares how often they have been approached, leaving Kazusa momentarily speechless. Through these small quarrels, they gradually become people who can speak directly to one another, beyond being someone the other knows through Haruki.[^women]

Naturally, they also talk about him. Setsuna notices how thoroughly Kazusa knows the club's situation and takes the opportunity to invite her to join. By now Setsuna can say that there is one more person working hard—herself—and that she wants to add another. She makes the invitation in her own right, placing herself within the activity already underway. While Haruki is still planning his next attempt at persuasion, Kazusa and Setsuna have agreed on a condition: if Setsuna can persuade her parents, Kazusa is willing to join too.[^women]

Haruki brought things together, but not everything begins with him. The two women can change each other's decisions in his absence. Their later care, probing, jealousy and acknowledgment of friendship all have this direct bond behind them. Their pain, too, comes from more than loving the same person.

Kazusa says that she and Setsuna will probably never share the same values: they might become mortal enemies or lifelong friends. Blunt and exaggerated as this is, it comes closer to the surprise of their acquaintance than saying they already understand each other perfectly. How can someone so different come so close? In accepting the invitation, she even makes their inability to understand each other a reason why they might as well spend time together. What Haruki hears is that she has finally agreed to stay.[^women]

The trio's connection thus has three histories. Haruki and Kazusa have teaching and responses through a wall; Haruki and Setsuna have an invitation, being heard and being encouraged; Setsuna and Kazusa have dealings they have established themselves. As these three bonds overlap, each person acquires a place they did not have before. Contact brings the prospect of meeting again. Rehearsal soon gives that prospect different meanings.

## Making a Song Together

Once rehearsal begins in earnest, “the three of us together” does not always mean three people in the same room.

Kazusa first wants to test Setsuna's voice and sends Haruki next door to practise on his own. He assumes that, with everyone assembled, they will naturally play together. Kazusa tells him that at his level he would only get in the way. The organizer suddenly becomes the person in need of extra lessons. A few days later, Setsuna hears him making fewer mistakes and wants to call him back. Kazusa still objects to his abrupt slowing at difficult passages. The plan has not reached that stage, she says—while her hands have already begun unconsciously accompanying the guitar next door. Setsuna catches her doing it.[^rehearsal]

Someone who knows how to teach can also be impatient to play together. The spoken plan and the eager hands belong to the same person.

Later, Kazusa takes Haruki to the basement studio at her house. The instruments, recording equipment and control room behind glass astonish him. Behind this hastily assembled student performance stand the space and equipment her family can supply, the skills she has developed over years, and the nights both of them can give. Here Haruki comes to understand how much Kazusa accommodated him through the wall. Now that he must keep to a fixed rhythm, she cannot wait for him at every turn. Even after his mistakes are pointed out, he asks to start again. Kazusa agrees: as many times as he needs.[^studio]

Their relationship acquires substance through working together. One provides the conditions; another works on the skills still lacking. The person who first proposed the project must follow someone else's instructions; the person who plays by instinct must think through another's difficulties. These changes make the performance possible. They also give “reliable,” “useful” and “worth waiting for” a meaning beyond descriptions of individual character. Such qualities gain weight through what they do for someone else.

Yet the same effort can leave an uninformed person feeling excluded. Once Setsuna learns that the two have secretly practised through the night, she cannot carry on as usual. The technical need for extra practice cannot answer, on her behalf, why she was kept in the dark. Haruki later calls and admits the concealment. She tells him how a confession of love in middle school cost her her friends. She likes Haruki and Kazusa alike. She ought to be happy to see them close, but the thought of being outside that closeness hurts.[^phone]

Her pain cannot all be attributed to the past. There really is something these two know together that she does not. Earlier exclusion makes today's concealment especially painful; today's concealment makes that earlier exclusion more than a memory.

As the performance approaches, the three also make a new song. Haruki has lyrics written earlier. Kazusa composes the music and prepares the backing track, adjusting the difficulty of the guitar part and taking Setsuna's vocal range into account. Presented with the new song, Setsuna again first thinks she has been left out. Kazusa explains the secrecy as a surprise: Haruki wrote the words, she composed the music, and the song is for Setsuna to sing. Haruki immediately adds that the original lyrics predate her joining; he changed their wording and imagery afterwards.[^song]

That detail matters. A song given to her to sing contains changes made for her, but also a history in which she took no part. Setsuna takes it up, Kazusa brings it into being, and the small thing Haruki had hoped for a chance to leave behind finally acquires a voice as a song. Their wishes remain different, yet here they become a work made together.

Romantic closeness helps bring the song into existence, and the song gives that closeness new substance. Its melody and words, whose voice it suits and who can finally play it, have all been shaped by their particular abilities and dealings with one another. Replace one person and the activity might continue, but the work and the experience would not remain the same. Their relationship is distinctive partly because they have made something that could grow only out of this particular exchange.

Before the show, Kazusa tells the others to listen to her if they lose their way. Setsuna says the last number belongs to the three of them and that she wants to sing it with abandon. Onstage, she can already joke with the audience, introduce the songs and her companions, and even put in a word for herself in the school vote. Beside her, Haruki is surprised that she has so much composure to spare. Takeya, in the audience, is surprised as well: when did Haruki learn to play so well?[^stage]

The people onstage have changed, and those below can see it. They have made happen a scene they once scarcely dared imagine.

## The Weight of Happiness

The opera in Lu Xun's “Village Opera” is not particularly satisfying. The roles the children want to see take forever to appear. Once the old-woman character sits down, she seems to have no intention of getting up again. The children wait and yawn, finally agreeing to leave. They launch the boat as eagerly as when they set out. Yet by the story's end, that night's opera remains something they will scarcely encounter again.[^shexi]

To point out that memory has beautified the facts would be hasty. The facts of that night also include Shuangxi finding a boat and reassuring the adults, the companions taking turns at the oars, waiting in disappointment, leaving together, then lighting a fire, shelling beans and sharing them on the way home. The performance onstage is only part of the experience; going and returning belong to it as well. The narrator eats beans again the next day, but they taste different. Beans can be cooked again. A night's companionship cannot be put in a bowl on its own.

Nor does “Village Opera” flatten the countryside's differences. The children ordinarily have work to do and are allowed to do less while entertaining their guest from afar. On hearing the praise, Old Liu Yi concludes that educated people from the city have better judgment. Closeness can arise amid such differences, and the happiness really did occur. Looking back at the school festival from here, we can see the unequal equipment and talent, the quarrels and hurt during rehearsal, and the pleasure of the performance itself. The former do not cancel the latter.

A shared life often first takes shape through something small that can be done and looked forward to. Someone will come tomorrow; what cannot be played well today can be practised again. Someone takes your lyrics seriously; someone is willing to wait for your clumsy hands. At first, they organize their days around the performance. Later, those days themselves become something to wish for.

A person can discover within a relationship that they are capable of living this way, and only afterwards ask what to call it. Through the festival, Haruki gains a work he has helped make beyond his grey routine. Kazusa's talent has particular people it can reach. Setsuna has companions with whom she can sing, make a fool of herself or quarrel, and still want to return. By the time the work is done, those days have made parting difficult.

In the final session of *Seminar XX*, Lacan brings together the contingency of an encounter and love's demand for continuation: once an encounter has happened, a person may wish it never to stop, as though it should go on inscribing itself in their destiny. Here lie both love's force and its drama.[^encore] The wishes following the school festival can also be understood in this light. A success gives them a reason to go on seeing one another, but it does not arrange the form that continued life will take. “Afterwards” thus carries both hope and a demand.

The question after the stage is therefore severe: how can this life, which has already changed them, continue? They do not yet have the same answer.

## “Together” Needs Asking Again

During the phone call in the rehearsal period, Setsuna says she wants to remain with both of them after the festival. She does not want to return to her old routine, still less to be left behind. Haruki hastens to promise that he will not leave. When she asks what exactly he means, he explains that as long as she does not break off their friendship, he will always be her friend. She accepts that promise.[^phone]

Words have clarified one meaning, while the heart may still ask another of them. Haruki is speaking of lasting friendship. Setsuna's question also concerns how much she matters to him. She understands “friend,” and therefore knows what the word leaves open.

This is the dimension Žižek addresses in his discussion of fantasy in *The Plague of Fantasies*: fantasy arranges positions for desire, allowing a person to understand what they are in the other's desire. Its concern is not merely what one has yet to obtain. It also concerns what is worth wanting, and how receiving it would count as being loved.[^fantasy]

Identification deserves a longer pause. Haruki's ability to do things for his companions is a practical capacity. His wish to see himself as someone who will never abandon another person is a way of understanding himself. The two connect, but do not automatically coincide. On the phone, Setsuna both mocks his tendency to play safe and asks him to promise not to leave. He connects that promise to the kind of person he has always been: she should know that he is a meddler who does not readily break off a friendship. Another person's expectations make this reliable image seem worth upholding; the image, in turn, helps determine his answer.[^phone]

What a reliable person should actually do, however, depends on what the person before him needs. When organizing a show, keeping someone involved may help it succeed. When someone wants to end a painful form of closeness, continuing to treat staying as proof of sincerity may preserve his image of himself while failing to meet her request. The difficulty of identification thus concerns more than accurate self-knowledge. It also concerns whether a person will answer others only from the position he has made his own.

Desire acquires direction through these exchanges as well. Being needed by someone gradually links “What can I give you?” to “Where do you want to place me?” A request can be met without settling that place: I have a part in rehearsal, but will there still be a place for me after the show? Technical decisions can assign the former. Each of the three must answer for the latter.

Seen through this distinction, playing together does not require the three to share a single fantasy about the performance. Haruki hopes that if he organizes things well, they can stay together. Setsuna hopes to gain a place from which she will no longer be left outside. Kazusa has long given her approach to Haruki the form of teaching and accompaniment, while wanting him to respond to her in turn. Their dealings make these wishes concrete and allow them to affect one another. The same pleasure in playing together can carry different futures.

Around the confession, these differences become impossible to leave vague.

After the performance, Io advises Setsuna to act soon if she is serious. Setsuna says she likes their present relationship best: today's stage was so much fun, and she wants the happiness to continue. Later, Kazusa kisses the sleeping Haruki in the music room. Setsuna sees it.[^afterstage]

Between these scenes, the time Setsuna treasures with the other two has not suddenly become false. What she sees is that the present arrangement she wants to preserve already contains someone else's movement towards a relationship for two. Keeping things as they are requires someone to be willing to stay where they are. That willingness is not hers to decide.

The digital novel *祭りの後* follows her subsequent wavering. She blames herself while arranging opportunities to draw close. She wants Haruki while imagining that, if Kazusa becomes his girlfriend first, Kazusa's distress might gradually cause Setsuna herself to be kept at a distance. Finally, she entrusts her action to a reason she cannot be sure of: to keep the three together, she must first come to Haruki's side.[^matsuri]

There is pursuit here, and fear; attachment to a friend, and desire for a lover's place. The novel does not put these feelings into a settled order. Setsuna herself cannot be certain which reason comes first or weighs most. Choosing one “true motive” for her and discarding the rest would make the reading simpler and the person thinner.

After Haruki wakes, she asks to stay together from now on. She talks about university and whether they might enter the same faculty. Haruki says they will still see each other at the same university, and he wants the three of them to do something again. Setsuna pauses, then asks him once more to be with her.[^confession]

The two instances of “together” finally reveal that they contain different numbers of people.

Inwardly, Haruki asks whether it cannot be the three of them. The thought has real things to hold on to: the music room, rehearsals, the song they finally completed. Yet the three-person relationship he cherishes no longer suffices as an answer to the person before him. Setsuna wants him to choose her as his girlfriend; Haruki must decide whether to accept. In the end, he initiates a kiss. The novel reveals how Setsuna brings this choice about, and the game clearly shows how Haruki makes it himself. The actions on both sides count.[^confession]

Here lies the force of the confession. It gives a wish to another person, asks for an answer, and on that basis begins a relationship in which something can be expected. Haruki and Setsuna now owe each other fidelity. “Afterwards” is no longer merely an excited word after a successful show. Meanwhile, the earlier teaching, care and time alone acquire new meanings: some approaches that could once continue under the name of friendship now conflict with a romantic commitment.

A name can give some aspects of a life a settled form; it cannot settle every desire. It supplies a reason for a relationship to continue and makes its participants responsible for how they go on. What they have agreed to must be answered again in the next approach, concealment or departure.

## When True Feelings Arrive

Setsuna later speaks directly to Kazusa about the romance. She even calls herself an intruder and says Haruki no longer belongs to Kazusa. These are Setsuna's words, carrying guilt and provocation; Haruki had made no prior romantic commitment to Kazusa. Kazusa acknowledges the promise between her two friends and asks Setsuna to call her by her given name.[^recognition]

These remarks do different things. Recognizing Haruki and Setsuna as a couple establishes between the women that the relationship has changed. Inviting Setsuna to use her given name also recognizes her as a friend who can come close. Kazusa has not thereby erased her own love. While that love remains, she acknowledges the decision the other two have made.

Keeping the three together “as before” consequently demands ever more effort. Haruki wants both to remain faithful to Setsuna and to keep Kazusa beside him. Kazusa accepts the fact of the couple, yet must face the person she loves continually approaching her as a friend. For Haruki, “not leaving” still looks like sincerity. For Kazusa, “only being beside him” has become painful. The same arrangement has different consequences for people in different positions.

When Haruki learns that Kazusa will go to Europe, the arrangement finally breaks open. He conceals from Setsuna that he is going to collect Kazusa at the airport, then insists that Kazusa accompany him on the pretext of taking her to Setsuna's birthday party. In their later argument, his questions gradually shift from why she has not told Setsuna to why she is leaving him. Kazusa replies that these are not words her best friend's boyfriend should be saying to her.[^departure]

A single designation places together the two relationships Haruki does not want to bear at once. He really does have a deep bond with Kazusa, and he really is Setsuna's boyfriend. The former gives his pain a history. The latter means that pain alone cannot justify his demand.

Hearing Kazusa's feelings, Haruki still wants to approach, embrace her and make her stay. She tells him not to follow and retreats towards the railing. He nevertheless advances and kisses her. She pushes him away and slaps him. Her jealousy of Setsuna cannot cancel this explicit refusal. Her earlier love cannot give consent on behalf of her body now.[^refusal]

True feeling can explain where an action comes from. The action must still answer to the person towards whom it goes. Romantic intensity too easily obscures this distinction.

Around graduation, Kazusa also says directly that she does want to go to Europe: she wants to play the piano better and live with her mother. The Kazusa who loves Haruki and the Kazusa who wants to leave both have actual wishes. While searching for her, Haruki has also realized how large their familiar surroundings are. She has always been nearby because she herself chose to be there.[^leaving]

The realization comes very late. Her earlier responses through the wall, her teaching at night, the repeated ease of finding her—all can become, through habit, “she will always be there.” Only when he cannot find her does each earlier presence reveal the time and choice another person gave.

## After the Show

There are still three people at the airport.

After learning of Haruki's betrayal, Setsuna nevertheless takes him to look for Kazusa, wanting them to meet once more. At one point he urges Setsuna to give up. On seeing Kazusa, however, he runs to her first. Kazusa reminds him that Setsuna is right behind him; he embraces her anyway. Kazusa then apologizes to Setsuna and returns his kiss. This time, a transgression that might once have been kept between two people enters the third person's sight.[^airport]

After Kazusa leaves, Setsuna asks Haruki to go home, then urges him at least to come inside for hot coffee. He agrees in words but does not move. She embraces him from behind, telling him he can pull away if he does not want it. Haruki neither turns nor leaves. In his thoughts, he insists that since he has already committed the lowest betrayal, he must go on being vile.[^airport]

![Outside the airport in the snow, Setsuna embraces Haruki from behind while he faces away with his hands at his sides.](./white-album-2-voice-before-names/ic-airport-aftermath-setsuna-embraces-haruki.jpg)

*After Kazusa leaves, Setsuna embraces Haruki from behind; he does not turn around. Game image: ©2012 AQUAPLUS / via 4Gamer.net.*

The shadow of the next three years is already here. Once someone fixes himself as unforgivable, the request of the person before him struggles to get through. She fears he will freeze; he thinks of his guilt. She asks him to go home; he will not step out of that guilt. Self-reproach occupies the place of an answer. The warmth Setsuna actually offers is still there. It has not set the relationship in motion.

All three have regrets, and regret does not point them in the same direction. Setsuna wants to bring Haruki back into the life before him. Haruki still faces the person who has left. Kazusa has embarked on a journey she herself wants to make. Acknowledging these differences lets us see how the parting happens. “They all suffer” cannot yet tell us what each is doing.

From the snow, IC returns to the school festival. Setsuna again introduces the last original song, “Todokanai Koi.” She says it has only just been completed and they have had little time to practise, but she wants to sing joyfully, from her heart, to the end.[^airport] After everything that follows, hearing these words again is sorrowful. Her happiness in singing them then remains a fact.

A song cannot decide whom the three will belong with. It has nevertheless changed their days. Haruki once played well enough to astonish an old companion. Kazusa once reconsidered how to teach and how to wait because of another person's clumsiness. Setsuna once offered her voice before a hall full of people as something for them to complete together. Later injury calls for questions about responsibility and promises. These changes that have already occurred deserve to remain as well.

At first, Haruki meant “WHITE ALBUM” to be the last song, and a voice called him back. Now the final number really is over, and the people who made it together must each live the next day. The music can sound again. The old days will not return by themselves.
