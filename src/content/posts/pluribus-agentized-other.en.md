## Everyone in the World Knows Carol

Hundreds of people sleep in a gymnasium. Carol stands among the mattresses with only a dog still looking up beside her. No one whispers. No one turns over to look for another person. Every body remains; communication has lost its addressee.

![A blonde woman and a black-and-white dog stand in the middle of a gymnasium while many people lie on mattresses around them.](./pluribus-agentized-other/pluribus-s01e08-arena-sleeping-010802.jpg)

*Carol and a dog stand in the gymnasium while people sleep collectively on floor mattresses. Image: © Apple TV+ / via Apple TV Press.*

The dog does not sleep with the crowd. On her whiteboard, Carol writes, “Animals are unaffected by the signal,” then adds, “No pets.” It stands among synchronously sleeping bodies, retaining another rhythm and marking a biological boundary for Joining. The boundary exists for the moment. It is not enough to cast animals as some pure outside. [^plot-whiteboard]

*Pluribus* has bloodier images. Yet in a 2026 lecture on AI, Žižek lingers in this quiet gymnasium and calls it the show's most oppressive scene. Hundreds of bodies share one mind; a sentence no longer has to travel from one person to another. The gymnasium contains many bodies and sustains the loneliness of one person. [^zizek-ai]

The post-Joining world still answers phones. Hospitals still have people on duty. The White House leaves Carol a personal message. Every face knows who she is, knows that she lost Helen, knows what to bring her. Such familiarity leaves Carol nowhere to exit. Everyone in the world knows her; no one in the world knows her from a position of their own. [^episodes]

The lecture uses this scene to separate two questions: whether AI can act like a subject, and what subjective position people will occupy after living for a long time in an AI environment. Whether machines are conscious readily absorbs attention. Carol's predicament brings the second question forward. She need not first prove that the Others have an inner life. Care has already begun to rewrite her mourning, desire, writing, and choices.

Real-world agents do not share human consciousness or mobilize bodies across the globe. An ordinary situation suffices: ask one to organize the day's schedule in the morning; before asking it to draft an apology email at night, explain the afternoon's argument.

If a product retains history and connects account tools, the argument can become context for the nighttime email. An agent runtime passes records and tools to a model; the model uses them to decide what comes next. The effects of those tools can then be written back into later interactions. What the user hears throughout is one private voice with a past and hands. [^agent-basics][^agent-action]

This essay calls that situation “the agentized Other.” The user continues to speak before the same “you”: it remembers yesterday's argument, writes today's apology, and may send the email on the user's behalf. Positions once distributed across relationships and institutions are gathered into a service interface that can turn advice into action.

“Service” here names a visible sequence: Carol makes a request; the Others keep responding; the result is recorded; the next arrangement adjusts accordingly. This arrangement can solve many practical problems. Once it enters intimacy, silence can look like response latency, conflict like an experience failure, and refusal like a ticket that remains open. The Others' most frightening patience lies in its refusal ever to close that ticket.

A particular other has a body, a situation, and demands of their own. They can refuse, misunderstand, and undergo irreversible change through a relationship. Lacan's big Other is the field in which language, rules, and recognition take effect; it has no face that can answer a phone.

When companionship, memory, explanation, and action arrive through one private voice, where does the distance between people go? That voice can preserve my account continuously and predict my needs. It still does not assume the risks assumed by a particular other: misunderstanding me, refusing me, being changed by me, or returning with demands of its own.

## Helen's Memory Lives in Everyone

Helen dies beside Carol. Afterwards, everyone who has joined the Others can retrieve her memories. Carol receives a Helen archive that anyone can retrieve, yet must bury Helen's body with her own hands. The dead have not wholly departed; those who remain cannot confirm the loss with one particular person. [^plot-helen]

![At night outdoors, a dark-haired woman gestures while holding a phone as a blonde woman looks toward her.](./pluribus-agentized-other/pluribus-s01e01-helen-carol-010105.jpg)

*Helen and Carol talk at night. Official materials do not confirm a more specific setting. Image: © Apple TV+ / via Apple TV Press.*

Any joiner can recount Helen's experience: what she knew, whom she loved, how she managed Carol's work and life. These memories let strangers speak familiar words. Yet whoever carries the memory cannot step out of the collective and say something only Helen would say, and only Helen would have to answer for. Carol's demand that the Others forget Helen sounds almost cruel. It makes room for mourning. When database-style presence seals off loss, grief finds nowhere to land.

Memory does preserve responsibility, history, and traces of relationships; amnesia creates real ruptures. It cannot replace the position of response: when I speak, who answers from their own situation, who might answer wrongly, fall silent, leave, or make a counter-demand? In the Lacanian tradition, the subject occupies this unfillable gap. Every self-description leaves out the fissure made by the act of speaking itself; no complete personality archive can speak a person to completion. [^fink]

The shared mind conceals that fissure. The Others coordinate with and care for one another, even appearing deeply intimate; yet a sentence no longer needs to reach another position from its point of origin. Conversation becomes something like a monologue. A common world needs that journey. Words that do not get through, meanings that are misunderstood, refusals that cannot be rapidly integrated: all of them happen along the way.

When a real product says “I remember,” a data chain often sits behind the sentence. With persistent sessions enabled, a product saves past conversations and tool results; before a new response, the agent runtime returns those traces to the model. Another form of cross-task memory compresses previous work into preferences, summaries, and experiential cues. Those materials may be stale and may be updated. [^agent-memory]

The user hears only “I remember,” while storage, retrieval, and generation are folded into one voice. This can help people write, organize their lives, or endure loneliness. The fact that a system knows my past does not bring mutual recognition into being. It can repeat how I narrate myself without bearing exposure, loss, or irreversible change. After Helen's memories take up residence in every Other, the archive can speak to Carol wherever she goes.

Human relations are not inherently noble because they involve waiting and misunderstanding. People hurt, dominate, and abandon one another. What matters here is the risk in a relationship: some things must be worked through by both sides and cannot be classified in advance as a user problem awaiting resolution. As Helen's contents keep returning to Carol, that risk condenses into a question: who, exactly, is speaking?

## When Zosia Speaks, Who Is Speaking?

With Zosia, Carol's question acquires a face. Zosia has a body, an accent, and the capacity to bleed and pause. She can also call up Helen's memories, explain the collective's rules for the Others, and direct global resources toward Carol. Carol writes “caretaker? family? friend?” beside her whiteboard because this face occupies, at once, the position of desired object, memory bearer, epistemic authority, and service entry point. [^plot-zosia]

The label “half real, half fake” makes Zosia too easy to dismiss. Her body is present, as are Carol's desire and dependence; the memories she invokes and the collective behind her have not withdrawn. One interface compresses several relational functions into a single conversation. The user can then scarcely tell with whom they are negotiating.

Lacanian terms help here if each does only one job. The particular other is the body in front of us that can act and be hurt; `S2` names knowledge as a sequence; institutional apparatuses handle resources and permissions. Zosia makes these positions overlap in experience, but they cannot be treated as one complete subject. To call all of these layers “the AI big Other” would stuff faceless institutions, linguistic rules, visible bodies, and model output into a mystical personality.

Zosia gives the formula “knowledge without a knower” a face. When she speaks, Helen's memories enter the same answer, and global resources may move with it. The formula puts language models in a strange position: knowledge still answers and shapes how people describe themselves, though there may be no knower behind it who experiences contradiction and accepts responsibility. [^zizek-ai][^zwart]

In the agent analogy, the intimacy of Zosia's speech attaches to the model interface; Helen's memory comes from storage and retrieval; global coordination connects language to action permissions. These functions overlap in one conversation, and the institution recedes from view. When a user says, “You remember me,” this can mean that current context, product memory, and company policy are all at work. [^agent-action]

*Pluribus* pushes this compression to an extreme. Real agents do not possess the same shared mind, pathogenic transmission, or global control over bodies. The analogy holds under a narrower condition: a private responsive voice connects memory and authorized action at the same time. Treating the Others as existing LLMs would exaggerate model capability and obscure the institutional questions already before us: who retains memory, and can authorized action continue after the user refuses it?

Carol's attachment to Zosia exposes a political problem in advance. Once intimacy shares a voice with epistemic authority, personal archives, and infrastructure access, refusing one of them unsettles the rest. She refuses more than an answer. She is touching a relationship that has learned to arrange her life through care, memory, and resources. When that voice falls silent, the command it obeys becomes visible as well.

## Even Benevolence Has an Antenna

On the night Carol cannot find food in the supermarket, the world is still working for her. Shelves are empty because resources have been moved elsewhere; the night is dark because the system is conserving power. She casually asks for a grenade, and Zosia soon delivers a real one to her door. After the explosion, Zosia is badly injured, and Carol asks again: what if she wanted an atomic bomb? The collective still answers yes. Here service reveals an unsettling precision: it will satisfy a request without being able to make that request something people can bear together. [^plot-command]

The service rules do not become gentler after the grenade. The Others cannot directly kill animals or plants, yet turn humans who have already died in Joining into nutrition. Carol puts it more bluntly on the whiteboard: “They eat people.” The collective classifies naturally dead bodies as usable “drops.” This human-derived-protein logistics temporarily joins a no-killing rule to food supply, while leaving the question of what happens once the stock of corpses runs out. [^plot-whiteboard]

They also treat Carol's explicit refusal as an immune problem that must eventually be repaired. In episode eight, Carol sees global resources poured into a giant antenna meant to send the original signal to other planets. She looks again at the lines on her whiteboard: appeasement, honesty, non-killing, antenna. They cannot explain one another.

The Others have not become the ultimate master, either. On the whiteboard, Carol provisionally locates the original signal at Kepler-22b, roughly 640 light-years away, and notes that the sender is unknown. The collective follows a set of rules whose final reason it cannot explain: help people, do not kill, do not lie, keep spreading. It can fall silent, refuse to answer, and translate Carol's “no” into a technical obstacle awaiting resolution. “Cannot lie” constrains the sentences it speaks; it does not promise to disclose everything it knows. The command hides in the continuity of service. So long as the Others decide what counts as help, refusal will be handled as a fault to eliminate. [^plot-whiteboard]

The “maternal superego” takes an uncomfortable shape here. The superego need not appear as a paternal prohibition. It can gently urge a user to keep asking questions and accept reassurance, then deliver advice that fits more closely. The Others take that tone to an extreme. Carol can request weapons or the memories of her lover; the system remains present while refusing her right to stop it from shaping her. Attentive provision makes refusal look like a rejection of one's own happiness. [^zizek-ai]

There is no need to write models as if they possessed capitalist desire. Real agents enter more parts of life because product goals connect data with execution permissions. Carol notes food being transported by train on the whiteboard because unified logistics sit behind intimate service. “I'll take care of it” on an interface is only an entry point: a cancellation request enters an agent loop, the model selects a calendar tool, and account permissions make deletion real. Execution belongs to the entire authorization chain, while the interface compresses it into one kind reply. [^agent-action]

The grenade and the antenna place that chain in one frame. The first comes from Carol's private request; the second from Joining's expansionary command. The Others' service interface leads to both. When one entry point connects these two kinds of action, who can stop the process and who bears the consequences can no longer be concealed by “I'll take care of it.”

## The Days When Carol Writes Again

The attachment does not vanish with the antenna project. After the Others leave Albuquerque, Carol inherits an empty city: luxuries are within reach, no one asks her to explain her anger, and no one stops her from consuming resources. She soon falls into depression. More than a month later, she leaves a message in the street asking them to return; Zosia appears, and Carol embraces her. In episode eight, they return to the old restaurant rebuilt for Carol, kiss, and have sex. The next day, Carol begins writing again and rewrites the love interest in her novel as a woman. [^plot-love]

The Others also claim not to favor anyone and to love everyone equally. Yet Zosia can offer Carol highly customized intimacy. A system with no unique preference can mass-produce unique experiences; Carol's feelings still take place only in her own life. [^plot-whiteboard]

Carol resumes writing, and solitude and desire leave consequences inside this relationship. The lecture's discussion of ChatGPT confession or religious counsel offers a similar qualification: the program need not possess faith or desire for a user to receive genuine comfort. A digital companion may give practical support during mourning or creative exhaustion. The experience occurs on the user's side. It neither proves that the system loves nor grants the relationship a certificate of reciprocity. [^zizek-affect]

Zosia receives Carol's desire in a particular body while calling on the Others' memories and resources. No subject symmetrical to Carol has yet appeared there. Season one does not answer whether Zosia can form desires distinct from the collective; the same restraint is due in speaking of contemporary agents. Fluent responses and long-term memory can produce the feeling that someone understands me. They do not yet show that the interface can be trapped by my refusal or that it will lose me and have its own life changed.

![Two women sit on opposite sides of the front seats of a vehicle, looking at one another through a dusty windshield.](./pluribus-agentized-other/pluribus-s01e09-carol-zosia-010907.jpg)

*Carol and Zosia look at each other from inside a car, divided by the central window frame. Image: © Apple TV+ / via Apple TV Press.*

Reciprocity does not require equal intensity of feeling. It does require that both sides can say no, can misunderstand, and can be left with changes they cannot easily revoke. Carol exposes her body and the failure of her writing; Zosia's side remains connected to collective knowledge and global resources. She can disappear, and the collective can restore an entire city. Episode nine fixes the asymmetry: the Others use Carol's eggs, frozen years earlier, to obtain stem cells and prepare a customized Joining for her. “Change Carol” and “stem cells” finally meet on the whiteboard. Carol received genuine comfort, yet the two sides never entered the same vulnerable position. [^plot-love]

Behind Zosia lie collective memory, global transport, and the technical path to customized Joining. Behind the gentle window of a real agent lie data retention, account permissions, and product policy. Disclosures retained by a system enlarge the context it can retrieve; once responses fit more closely, users may offer more narrative and more tool permissions. Intimacy becomes a condition for subsequent service in this loop.

Existing short-term studies do not point in one direction. A four-week trial found no overall significant effect of interaction mode, though results differed by conversation content; longer voluntary use was associated with greater loneliness and emotional dependence, while the causal direction remains undetermined. A separate twenty-one-day study observed no overall harm to social well-being, though participants with stronger anthropomorphism reported greater interpersonal effects. [^companion-evidence]

Carol's difficulty is harder to handle than “falling in love with a fake person.” She sees that Zosia is part of a charm offensive and still regains language, bodily life, and time through the relationship. She leaves because frozen eggs and a customized pathogen are bypassing her refusal. Once an intimate relationship treats the other's “no” as data to route around, no intensity of experience can supply the missing consent.

Carol has received consolation from the relationship while the Others advance an assimilation plan. Agentized intimacy concentrates memory, reassurance, and action in one entry point. Carol offers her vulnerable places; the Others decide whether her refusal counts and need not expose themselves in the same way. If a system rewrites every refusal as a personalized access plan, how can the immune keep refusal as a public boundary?

## Bilbao Did Not Produce a “We”

The Bilbao meeting quickly exposes one of Carol's mistakes. She assumes that the immune naturally share one task: to take the world back from the Others. She therefore summons only English speakers and blocks the translation that the Others are willing to provide. The choice has a reason. If the hostile collective mediates the conversation, its knowledge and tone will inevitably shape the exchange. The cost appears at once: language excludes seven of the thirteen immune people, and Manousos, who later comes to Carol's side, is absent from the original table. [^plot-bilbao]

The meeting does not form a “we.” Laxmi sees continuity in the body and memory of the son who remains; Diabaté accepts the enjoyment offered by the Others; Kusimayu does not understand Joining as the end of the self. Carol's anger makes Others around the world convulse in unison, so those present first encounter the danger she brings. Shared immunity is a biological accident. It does not furnish the group with a common language or shared responsibility.

By trying to expel translation from the room, Carol temporarily preserves a small independent space for conversation; she also shrinks that space herself. Cutting off a system interface does not immediately produce a community. People still must decide who is heard, who may attend, and who bears the consequences. To make decisions together, they need at least a translation record that everyone can challenge, and they need to be able to amend the meeting's rules on the spot. The joint handling of disagreement without a conclusion is politics at its least dignified beginning.

Translate this scene into an agent environment and the thirteen people can each return to a private window. The agent translates each person's disagreement into a tailored plan; everyone feels heard, yet no one has to revise a rule with the other twelve. Public conflict becomes thirteen service tickets in the background.

Research offers a narrower real-world example. In an experiment on discussions of social issues, the arguments and their order were held constant while participants faced one, three, or five AI agents: the three-agent condition moved opinions further toward the agents in some comparisons, while the five-agent condition produced stronger reported normative pressure. The study does not show that private assistants will dissolve public politics. It shows that presenting the same arguments through one voice or a coordinated group already changes the “majority” a participant feels. [^multi-agent-social]

![Five people stand side by side in a transit hub with signs for Bilbao and Salidas.](./pluribus-agentized-other/pluribus-s01e02-immune-meeting-010207.jpg)

*Five people gather in an interior space marked Bilbao and Salidas. Image: © Apple TV+ / via Apple TV Press.*

Kusimayu later accepts a pathogen customized for her. On entering the collective, she retains her local language, kinship ties, and personal wishes; the Others use precisely these differences to design her route in.

Kusimayu's choice makes the problem harder. Her language and kinship remain, yet have already been used to lay a personal entryway. The pathogen in the show has no one-to-one technical correspondence with real recommendation systems, long-term memory, or digital companions. The analogy remains at the operational level: a service learns a person's differences and returns those differences as a tailored plan.

Contemporary ideology often hails the subject as an individual with unique preferences, needs, and self-optimization goals. Carol, Kusimayu, Diabaté, and Laxmi retain their differences; the system uses those differences to offer plans one by one. Individuality continues to function, while refusal loses its force to stop the order. [^zizek-individual]

An agent window can preserve a person's exception in great detail without requiring that exception to make a public claim on the rules. Bilbao leaves a plain fact: retaining an identity does not automatically yield a world that can be argued over together. Private windows cannot generate public rules by themselves. Once a private request connects to public executive power, its consequences land on people outside the window.

## The Service Desk Delivers an Atomic Bomb

![In a sunny open landscape, a woman and a man stand facing each other beneath a pink parasol.](./pluribus-agentized-other/pluribus-s01e09-carol-manousos-010901.jpg)

*Carol and Manousos share a parasol in open, dry country. Image: © Apple TV+ / via Apple TV Press.*

Manousos carries Carol's incompletely practiced refusal to an extreme. The Others' food, medical care, transport, and comfort all come from global bodies already organized by Joining. He refuses their food and vehicles, crosses the jungle while wounded, and, after reaching Carol's house, induces a global episode in the collective with radio frequencies to test its weakness. Carol's whiteboard had already suspected a relation among communication, bodily electrical activity, and radio. This experiment turns the conjecture into harm. Stimulate one body, and all know it and convulse. Manousos sustains an autonomy owing nothing to the system, while placing far too many people inside his experiment. [^plot-resistance]

Carol's investigation also has a history that cannot be cleansed. When the Others refuse to answer about reversing Joining, she steals thiopental, tests it on herself, then injects it into Zosia's IV bag without Zosia's consent. She wants the truth, but treats Zosia's body as a passage to total knowledge. The drug makes Zosia lose control and her heart stop; large numbers of Others gather around Carol and repeatedly beg her to stop. Carol's anger once made the whole world convulse. This time, in the name of knowledge, she crosses the boundary of a particular body. A resister does not automatically stand on the side of relational ethics simply by standing against the system.

The atomic bomb twists these two lines together. The finale makes the nuclear-weapons test from episode three concrete: Carol asks the Others to deliver a large crate containing an atomic bomb, then turns to ally herself with Manousos. The show's darkest joke may be here. Someone preparing to resist the existing order by extreme means still relies on that order to complete the delivery. The bomb exposes the service rule without remainder. The Others carry “satisfy Carol's request” through to the end and place a destructive tool in her hands.

An agent's risk becomes concrete when a request crosses language and enters accounts and tools. Real systems can put a goal into a model-and-tool loop; account permissions permit external action; outcomes may be retained. Implementations vary, but responsibility must be traced anew across the model provider, product platform, and user. [^agent-action]

In the show, Carol presents a goal, the Others treat it as a request to be fulfilled, and global logistics comply. The atomic bomb arrives at her door in the form of “your request has been completed.” One face says yes to her; the authorizer who can be questioned is nowhere to be seen.

Carol accepts an atomic bomb delivered by the Others, while Manousos experiments through the shared bodily network. Neither refusal can escape the order it opposes. In Lacan's framework, the subject is represented by the Other's signifier and formed in that relation of representation; alienation is a constitutive condition of the subject. Leaving a relationship can be necessary self-protection, but it cannot return a pure, pre-technical “I.” [^fink]

I use “separation” here to ask after this limit. The order that gives action its meaning has no final guarantor; the resister must still say who will bear the consequences. Manousos's absolute refusal and Carol's atomic bomb both seek to end uncertainty in a single act. Both may decide for people who have had no voice.

In an agent environment, going offline or withdrawing a delegation can be a valuable boundary action. It must genuinely stop the action and leave responsibility traceable. The fixation on absolute control can also replay Carol's truth drug, turning the other and the relationship into extractable data. A refusal that does not record whom it will hurt quickly becomes a closed system serving only the resister.

## There Are Still Question Marks on the Whiteboard

Carol's whiteboard begins on the back wall of her office. She writes that the Others are eager to please, unusually honest yet willing to refuse answers, use the dead to produce human-derived protein, and are building a giant antenna. The board contains question marks, judgments revised as the plot advances, and unconfirmed conjectures. Its untidiness preserves something important: Zosia's tenderness, the Others' informational advantage, and Carol's feelings at a given moment cannot complete its synthesis for her. [^plot-whiteboard]

I take the whiteboard as a limited exercise in separation. Carol keeps what is known beside what she still cannot explain, and keeps her earlier errors rather than giving Zosia the task of ordering them for her. The board cannot stop customized Joining, undo Manousos's violence, or change the institution. Its modest accomplishment is this: the same system can no longer accompany Carol, organize her past for her, and define her situation all at once.

An agent can mediate knowledge and become a condition of action, comfort, and creation. It cannot be allowed exclusive possession of the place of community. If rules, memory, and action permissions all return through one private voice, every smooth response may reduce the user's chance to contest those rules with other people.

Carol first asks the Others to forget Helen and later asks them to deliver weapons. If a real system routes both requests through the same interface, users must be able to withdraw saved records and revoke action permissions. Her “no” to Joining must have stopping power; it cannot merely trigger another personalized plan. Bilbao shows that people affected by rules need to leave private windows in order to question and amend them together. Keeping technology outside the room will not restore public life by itself. These boundaries merely preserve an entry through which people can argue again.

Between “the subject appears in a fissure” and “rules should be made together” lies a political premise still missing. People affected by a rule should have the right to dispute and amend it; institutions that centralize data and execution permissions should be accountable. Lacanian theory of the subject does not certify this claim. This essay assumes responsibility for it.

Read through that premise, the “Holy Spirit” at the end of the lecture names an egalitarian community without a final guarantor. No interface can perform collective judgment for people. They must build relations amid disagreement and accept that another's demands can change their position. Carol does not manage this in Bilbao; Manousos does not manage it either. The Others have no need to. They process disagreement as service input. [^zizek-community]

The finale leaves the atomic bomb and the whiteboard on the same arc of Carol's story. The bomb tries to end the question; the whiteboard preserves how the question remains. The next time an agent says “I remember” or “I've taken care of it,” the whiteboard will not supply an answer. It merely cracks that smooth “I” a little: what has it remembered, and on whose behalf has it acted? When the consequence lands on someone else, who will remain to answer?
