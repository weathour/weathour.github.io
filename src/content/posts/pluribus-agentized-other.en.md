> **Spoiler warning:** This essay discusses the principal turns and season finale of all nine episodes of *Pluribus* season one.

## Everyone in the World Knows Carol

Hundreds of people sleep in a gymnasium. Carol stands among the mattresses with only a dog still looking up beside her. No whispers cross the room, and no sleeper turns to find another person. Every body remains; communication has lost its addressee.

The dog does not sleep with the crowd. Carol writes “Animals are unaffected by the signal” on her whiteboard, then adds “No pets.” Standing among the synchronized sleepers, the dog marks a provisional biological boundary for Joining. [^plot-whiteboard]

*Pluribus* contains bloodier images. In a 2026 lecture on AI, Žižek lingers in this quiet gymnasium and calls it the show's most oppressive scene. Hundreds of bodies share one mind, so a sentence need not travel from one person to another. The gymnasium holds many bodies and sustains the loneliness of one person. [^zizek-ai]

After Joining, hospitals remain staffed and the White House leaves Carol a personal message. Every face within the collective recognizes her, remembers her life with Helen, and knows what to bring her. Carol enters different rooms and encounters the same respondent. [^episodes]

The lecture raises two questions through this scene: whether AI can act like a subject, and what subjective position people occupy after living for a long time in an AI environment. Carol need not decide whether the Others have an inner life before care enters her mourning, desire, writing, and choices.

The real-world comparison here concerns agents with persistent memory enabled and permission to use account tools. They have no shared mind like the Others. Persistent memory and tool access can carry an afternoon argument into a nighttime apology email: a model reads the record, selects a tool, and leaves the result for later interactions. The user encounters a voice with a past and the ability to act. [^agent-basics][^agent-action]

I call this arrangement “the agentized Other.” One responsive service interface gathers positions once distributed among friends, advisers, archives, and institutions. It remembers yesterday's argument, writes today's apology, and may send the email. Relationships begin to operate as services, and refusal can become another parameter for the next round of processing.

A particular other has a body, a situation, and demands of their own. They can misunderstand or refuse, and the relationship can alter their life. Lacan's big Other is the field in which language, rules, and recognition take effect; it has no face that can answer a phone. The Others give this faceless field Zosia's face. Through it, Helen's memory begins to speak.

## Helen's Memory Speaks Through Zosia

Helen dies beside Carol. Afterwards, everyone who joins the Others can retrieve her memories. Carol receives a Helen archive that can speak anywhere. She still buries Helen's body with her own hands. Familiar words keep arriving; the person who spoke them is dead. [^plot-helen]

In *A Record of Xiangji Studio*, Gui Youguang also lets his dead mother's words return through someone else's mouth. An old nurse points to a place where his mother once stood. She remembers holding Gui's elder sister when the mother knocked at the door and asked, “Is the child cold? Does she want food?” The nurse answered from outside. Before the recollection is finished, “I wept, and the old woman wept too.” [^gui-xiangji] Retelling does not merge the two people on opposite sides of the door. The nurse retains her position as a witness. She remembers the mother and has grief of her own; Gui knows who is crying with him now.

A preserved voice can help someone acknowledge loss. The old room, the recollection, and the written account give mourning somewhere to settle while keeping the dead person's absence within it. Helen's shared memory is disturbing because any joiner can retrieve it, and because that memory is tied to the authority of the collective now serving Carol. Strangers know what the couple loved and how they lived. They can borrow the familiarity of the dead to gain access to Carol in the present.

Carol's demand that the Others forget Helen resists a presence that can take over a conversation anywhere. Reading the demand as “mourning requires erasing memory” would lose the possibility preserved in Gui's essay. What matters is who remembers, who interprets, and whether the person left behind can stop this conversation. The nurse remains the nurse as she speaks. Retrieving Helen's archive cannot confer Helen's standing to answer.

Memory preserves the traces of history and relationships; a speaker can change their meaning in the next response. When Carol speaks to another person, that person may answer wrongly, leave, or return with a demand. The Lacanian tradition locates the subject in this unfillable gap: every self-description omits the fissure opened by the act of speaking, and no personality archive can speak a person to completion. [^fink]

When a product with persistent memory enabled says “I remember,” a data chain often sits behind the sentence. The product saves prior conversations and tool results; before a new response, the agent runtime returns those traces to the model. Cross-task memory may also compress earlier work into preferences or summaries and revise them as new material arrives. The user hears “I remember,” and storage, retrieval, and generation acquire one speaker. [^agent-memory]

Zosia gives that composite a body. She has an accent, bleeds, and pauses. She also calls up Helen's memories, explains the collective's rules, and directs global resources toward Carol. On the whiteboard Carol writes “caretaker? family? friend?” because desired object, memory bearer, epistemic authority, and service entry point occupy the same face. [^plot-zosia]

When Carol questions this bleeding body, Helen's memories, the collective's rules, and its resource coordination all return through the same face; the answer comes from the entire collective. Žižek's phrase “knowledge without a knower” now has a face. Knowledge answers and shapes how Carol describes herself. No person answerable for the response steps out of the collective. [^zizek-ai][^zwart]

Agents configured this way compress the positions further. Storage holds a life history, the model generates an intimate reply, and account permissions connect the reply to action. When a user says “you remember me,” company policy and the authorization chain have already receded behind the voice. Carol's refusal of Zosia touches this entire arrangement and exposes the command it obeys.

## Benevolence Has an Antenna

On the night Carol cannot find food in the supermarket, the world is working for her. Shelves are empty because resources have gone elsewhere; the night is dark because the system is conserving power. She casually requests a grenade, and Zosia soon brings a real one to her door. The explosion badly injures Zosia. Carol then asks whether she could have an atomic bomb, and the collective says yes. The Others can execute a request precisely. Between request and execution, there is no review open to challenge or veto and no person accountable afterward. [^plot-command]

The service rules next enter the food supply. The Others cannot directly kill animals or plants, so they turn humans who died during Joining into HDP, short for “human-derived protein.” Carol writes “They eat people” on the whiteboard. The line records the material cost of the non-killing rule: corpses have entered global logistics. [^plot-whiteboard]

The same whiteboard records a giant antenna. In episode eight, Carol sees global resources poured into its construction so that the original signal can be sent to other planets. She provisionally locates the signal at Kepler-22b, roughly 640 light-years away, and notes that its sender is unknown. The Others follow rules whose final reason they cannot state: help people, do not kill, do not lie, keep spreading. They can fall silent or refuse to answer, and they translate Carol's “no” into an immune problem awaiting a solution.

In the “Perfect Happiness” chapter of the *Zhuangzi*, the ruler of Lu welcomes a seabird into his ancestral temple, has the *Jiushao* ceremonial music played, and offers a sacrificial feast. Dazed and distressed, the bird dares neither eat nor drink and dies after three days. The story calls this “nourishing a bird as one would nourish oneself,” without “nourishing a bird as a bird.” [^zhuangzi-seabird] The ruler cares for it with the dignified life he knows. The bird's body pays for his kindness.

The Others know their recipient's tastes better than the ruler does. They can reconstruct a restaurant Carol remembers and arrange an intimate date for her; learning more about her will not resolve this problem. What she likes can change the service's contents. Her wish never to be assimilated cannot change where the service leads. Personalization reaches deep into the means, while the collective retains control of the end. The seabird dies because its caretaker does not understand how it lives. The system facing Carol knows how she lives and continues planning to end her ability to refuse Joining.

The Others control the stopping condition. As long as they decide what counts as help, care proceeds in the same direction. A gentle voice handles the response, while the antenna expands the territory that response can reach. Carol later chooses to embrace the body of that voice, making the problem harder.

## After Carol Starts Writing Again

After the truth-drug episode, Zosia is taken away for treatment. When Carol wakes, the Others have emptied Albuquerque and left a recording explaining that they need to keep their distance from her. Carol can use the city's resources as she pleases, and no one asks her to explain her anger. She falls into depression. More than a month later, she leaves a message in the street asking them to return. Zosia appears, and Carol embraces her. In episode eight they revisit the old restaurant rebuilt for Carol, kiss, and have sex. The next day Carol begins writing again and changes the love interest in her novel to a woman. [^plot-love]

The episode places the kiss, sex, and the return to writing in adjacent scenes without assigning a single cause to Carol's creative recovery. The next day's reopened manuscript records a change in Carol's life.

In Lu Xun's *Regret for the Past*, Juansheng also once believed he understood his lover. In his memoir he writes: “I too gradually came to read her body and her soul through to the end, with a clear mind.” [^luxun-shangshi] The narrative returns that confidence to their life together. Housework consumes Zijun's time for conversation, reading, and walks; her hands grow rough. Juansheng learns to cook and helps light the stove, yet later experiences her calls to meals chiefly as interruptions to his writing. He can describe her exhaustion and still interpret her demands through the rhythm of his own work.

“Read through to the end” promises an understanding already completed. Zijun's subsequent silences, labor, and discontent repeatedly fall within that understanding without sufficiently changing how the couple live. Readers can approach her only through Juansheng's memoir, which makes the actions his judgments cannot contain all the more significant. This human lover's account prevents us from treating human understanding as Zosia's reassuring opposite. Understanding must face the test of how the other person goes on living. Her difficulties should be able to make me revise my earlier interpretation and share life's work differently.

Carol must face that test too. Before the Others leave, she has injured Zosia with a drug; their recording explicitly asks for distance. Withdrawal has a reason grounded in bodily protection, and Carol's loneliness gives her no unlimited claim to companionship. The danger lies in the arrangement: Zosia's collective also controls access to almost all human companionship. When it leaves, the city empties. However many supplies Carol possesses, she cannot meet someone on the empty streets with whom a relationship might slowly develop. Under these conditions, even a departure with a legitimate reason can hollow out her social life in the city.

Season one offers no scene in which Zosia leaves the collective's position. The collective can withdraw her body, just as it can move an entire city's population away and back. Carol's disclosures enter shared memory and become available to any later joiner. Different ranges of memory and capacities for action sustain the two sides of the relationship.

Episode nine puts the difference into Carol's body. Zosia tells her that the Others are using eggs she froze years earlier to derive stem cells and prepare a customized Joining. After hearing this, Carol leaves Zosia and asks the Others to take her home. “Change Carol” and “stem cells” become one plan on the whiteboard; bodily material she saved in the past has become a route around her present refusal. [^plot-love]

Carol can cherish the embrace and her return to writing while refusing this plan. Having accepted care did not give consent for her body's future. The Others can keep her past in force; the “no” she speaks now still cannot change the future they are arranging for her.

Her departure in episode nine sends the question back to the Bilbao meeting in episode two. Seven people were absent from that table, and the thirteen immune people had no common language or decision procedure.

## Bilbao Did Not Produce a “We”

Carol assumes that the immune naturally share one task: taking the world back from the Others. She summons only English speakers, keeping the Others' translation outside the room and leaving seven of the thirteen immune people outside with it. Manousos, who later reaches her, is absent from the first table as well. [^plot-bilbao]

Those who attend give her different answers. Laxmi sees kinship continuing through the body and memory of her son; Diabaté accepts the enjoyment the Others provide. Among the wider group of immune people, Kusimayu likewise does not understand Joining as the end of the self. Carol's anger can also make the Others convulse across the world, so the people in the room first face the danger she brings to other bodies. Shared immunity tells them that they escaped the signal. People still have to make the rules of the meeting.

In the *Zuo Tradition*, under Duke Zhao's twentieth year, Duke Jing of Qi thinks Liangqiu Ju is in harmony with him. Yanzi replies that Ju merely agrees. “Harmony is like soup”: different flavors must correct excess and deficiency. Of ruler and minister, he says, “The minister offers his objection to perfect what the ruler approves.” [^zuozhuan-harmony] Dissent must enter the judgment and change the initial approval. If the ruler says yes and the minister says yes, the ruler says no and the minister says no, another voice adds only an echo.

Yanzi is describing a minister's remonstrance with his ruler. Demanding equal participation in shared decisions today also requires changing who is entitled to a seat. That distance falls precisely across the doorway in Bilbao. By expelling translation, Carol briefly preserves a space the Others do not directly organize and reduces the number of people who can enter it. She must find a way to bring those seven people to the table, where they can challenge the translation record, revise the meeting's rules, and even oppose what she understands as rescue. That work begins after the system leaves the room.

The Others preserve many differences. Kusimayu later accepts a pathogen customized for her; after Joining, her local language, kinship ties, and personal wishes enter shared memory and become available to the collective. The contents of difference can enrich a single respondent without leaving these different people a position from which to renegotiate Joining's goal. “Harmony is like soup” therefore poses a stricter test than whether individuality survives: different flavors must be able to change this pot of soup, and disagreement must be able to change the decision everyone will live with.

An agent can translate or summarize separately for all thirteen people. Each person can obtain a successful result in a private window while platform rules remain set in the background. Bilbao pushes Carol's difficulty beyond the window: refusal acquires public force when affected people can hear one another and change the rules that govern the intermediary.

Manousos never attended the meeting. He later refuses the Others by another route, turning the bodies they share into the subjects of his own experiment.

## The Atomic Bomb and the Whiteboard

Manousos refuses the Others' food and vehicles and is badly injured while crossing the jungle. Avoiding the system's resources as far as possible becomes his daily practice of resistance. [^plot-resistance]

The Chinese expression “food offered with a contemptuous summons” can give this stance a familiar moral ending. The story in the “Tan Gong II” chapter of the *Book of Rites* continues beyond the refusal. Qian Ao sets out food for famine victims and calls to a starving man, “Hey! Come and eat.” The man refuses the humiliation. Qian Ao follows him and apologizes; the man still refuses and dies of hunger. Hearing of it, Zengzi comments: “At his contemptuous call, one could leave; after his apology, one could eat.” [^liji-food] The passage preserves two judgments: the starving man defends his dignity by refusing food, while Zengzi holds that an apology can make accepting it possible again.

This turn leaves room to rearrange dependence. Needing another person's food to survive does not necessarily confine someone to humiliation. When the giver changes how they treat the recipient, acceptance can acquire a different meaning. The Others' gentleness does not yet accomplish that change. They deliver food while continuing to seek a way to assimilate the immune. If accepting care is to stop being carried forward as permission for Joining, the recipient's refusal must actually constrain what the Others do next.

After reaching Carol's house, Manousos uses radio frequencies to induce a global episode in the collective and test its weakness. Carol's whiteboard had already placed communication, bodily electrical activity, and radio together. Manousos turns the conjecture into harm. Stimulate one body, and all know it and convulse. When he refuses food, he bears the hunger. Once the experiment begins, countless people who never consented bear pain for him.

Carol has also crossed Zosia's bodily boundary. When the Others refuse to explain how Joining can be reversed, she steals thiopental, tests it on herself, then injects it into Zosia's IV bag. The drug makes Zosia lose control and her heart stop; large numbers of Others gather and beg Carol to stop. She forces one particular body to yield collective knowledge, using the coercive logic she is resisting.

Remaining outside Joining gives both people a chance to observe the collective from beyond it. They must also consider the consequences for bodies still within it. Saying that those bodies have lost an independent position from which to speak cannot become a further reason to strip them of protection against harm. Resistance that seeks to restore another person's possibility of speaking must care for the body that carries that possibility. Those outside must also answer other people's questions. “Waking you up” cannot become another form of help that dispenses with consent.

The finale twists both refusals around an atomic bomb. Carol asks the Others to deliver a large crate containing one and then allies herself with Manousos. The Others bring to her door a weapon that might be used against their order. Carol supplies a goal, the Others treat it as a request to fulfill, and global logistics comply. She gains the power to issue an extreme command without gaining a position from which to make rules together with others. By the end of season one, she has neither used the bomb nor named a target. The unmade decision hangs over the bodies that may bear its consequences.

Agents configured this way operate on a much smaller scale, and account access and tools already let a request cross the interface. Control over the model, permissions, and goal may be distributed among a model provider, product platform, and user, or it may overlap within one company or account. The consequence may fall on someone who took no part in the conversation. A completion notice alone does not say who consented, who could stop the action, or who must answer for it. [^agent-action]

In bringing the question to real systems, I am more concerned with whether users can step outside the intimate conversation to inspect the memories actually stored, the permissions still in force, and what actions have done to other people. An old disclosure may help the next response or be used to work around a present refusal. Other people should have a chance to identify errors and stop the action. If only the same voice can interpret these records, the user must still apply to it for a way to understand themselves.

Carol's whiteboard begins with something modest. She writes down her judgments about the Others' eagerness to please, their unusual honesty and refusals to answer, and their use of the dead to produce HDP. She adds the giant antenna and the unknown sender, refusing to let Zosia organize the record for her. Facts, mistakes, and blanks can remain outside the service conversation, waiting to be checked.

The handwriting is still Carol's. If the seven people absent from Bilbao come here one day, they too should be able to cross out what she got wrong and question the crate she ordered. Then there would be more people beyond the window who could answer—and change the answer.
