> **Spoiler warning:** This essay discusses the principal turns and finale of all nine episodes of *Pluribus* season one. The Chinese edition uses the author's renderings of names, “Joining,” and “the Others,” giving the original at first mention.

## Everyone Remembers Her

Hundreds of people sleep in a gymnasium. Carol stands among the mattresses, a dog still looking up beside her. There are no whispers, no sentence waiting for another person to answer. What one knows, everyone already knows; there is no need to pass it on, no need to wait. Discussing this scene, Žižek finds it more oppressive than the show's bloodier images: hundreds of bodies sustain the loneliness of one person. [^zizek-ai]

This is the world after Joining. Almost all humanity has become a collective that shares memories and coordinates its actions: the Others. Carol is one of the few immune people, still able to think for herself and rage by herself. The Others treat her with the utmost courtesy. Hospitals remain staffed; the White House leaves her a personal message. Wherever she goes, those who care for her recognize her, even when their faces change. [^episodes]

Her partner Helen, however, died when Joining struck. Carol buries her, while living strangers can still recount their life together. Helen's memories have entered the collective, available to every Joined person. Familiar words can still arrive. The person who spoke them is dead. [^plot-helen]

Old words returning through someone else can also bring comfort. In Gui Youguang's *A Record of Xiangji Studio*, an old nurse points to a spot where his mother once stood. The nurse had been holding his elder sister when their mother asked at the door, “Is the child cold? Does she want to eat?” From the other side of the door, the nurse answered for the child. Years later, before she has finished telling the story, the listener weeps, and the speaker weeps too: “I wept; the old woman wept also.” [^gui-xiangji]

That *yi*, “also,” lets the nurse's grief be present. She remembers for herself and grieves for herself. Although she repeats another woman's words, she also tells of a life she experienced. Gui listens and knows who is crying with him now. His mother cannot return; the two who remain can still remember her together.

The strangers before Carol need no such experience. They need never have known Helen, never have spent a day in the couple's life, to retrieve familiar memories and approach a woman who has just lost her partner. Carol's demand that the Others forget Helen bars precisely this approach. Her loved one has been buried, yet the intimacies of her life are available to strangers everywhere, as though all of them had known Carol for years.

Remembering someone's past cannot speak tomorrow's words for them. Lacan's account of the subject begins from the point at which a person cannot fully coincide with their own description: they say who they are, and what they say exposes something they have not made clear. The archive can keep expanding, but completing the archive does not produce a final edition of the person. [^fink] After Helen's death, the archive remains. The person who could argue with Carol, make up with her, and understand old events anew cannot come back.

Zosia arrives carrying these memories. She has her own face and accent; she can be wounded and bleed. Carol asks her about Helen and about the Others' rules, and can ask her to bring people and things from far away. The face that keeps her company is the face that explains the world, and the face that gets things done. On her whiteboard Carol writes, “Chaperone? Family? Friend?” None of the names quite settles who Zosia is. [^plot-zosia]

I call this gathering of intimacy and power the “agentized Other.” A smaller form already exists in everyday life. A user tells their history to an Agent with persistent memory enabled, then grants it permission to use account tools. In the next interaction, the program retrieves old records, the model generates a response, and authorized tools may turn that response into an action. After an argument, for example, it might listen to the user's grievances, draft an apology, and send it with the necessary authorization. The work of friend, adviser, archive, and assistant gradually gathers in a single window. [^agent-basics][^agent-memory][^agent-action]

These programs do not have the show's shared mind; the comparison concerns only this concentration of memory, interpretation, and action. An intimate tone makes the concentration harder to notice. Hearing “I remember,” we can easily hear storage, retrieval, and generation as one person's recollection. Carol's difficulty is more immediate: the person who knows her past is keeping her company in the present.

## She Asks Them to Return

Carol has already had a taste of the Others' care. She asks sarcastically for a grenade, and Zosia brings a real one. Carol pulls the pin; Zosia throws the grenade away and is badly wounded in the explosion. At the hospital Carol tests the limits again: could she have an atomic bomb too? The answer is still yes. [^plot-command]

Yet the Others, willing to deliver a bomb, will not tell her how to reverse Joining. Carol steals sodium thiopental, tries it on herself, then secretly adds it to Zosia's IV bag, attempting to force her to surrender the knowledge the collective withholds. Zosia loses control and goes into cardiac arrest. Joined people gather, pleading with Carol to stop. She wants to save the world and has first hurt the person before her. [^plot-resistance]

Zosia is taken away for treatment. When Carol wakes, everyone has left Albuquerque. Only a recording remains, explaining that the Others need to keep their distance. Zosia has just been hurt; there is reason for this departure. Carol's need for company does not entitle her to make Zosia stay.

But Zosia is not the only one gone. When one person leaves, there may still be someone else to talk to. When the Others leave, there is no one else in the city. The houses remain, the vehicles remain, the readily available supplies remain. Carol has so many things she can use, but no one with whom she can begin as a stranger and slowly become acquainted.

She continues investigating the Others and trying to contact the other immune people. She has also tried the empty city's luxuries, yet the days grow harder. After more than a month, Carol leaves a message in the street asking the Others to return. When Zosia appears, she embraces her. [^plot-love]

Later, the two enter a diner. Carol used to write there early in her career; now the Others have rebuilt it as it was. She knows they are courting her favor and continues watching for their secrets. Yet the old place is back, and someone is beside her. Seeing through the arrangement does not keep her from feeling. She and Zosia kiss and have sex. The next day Carol starts writing her novel again, changing its romantic interest into a woman. [^plot-love]

She writes on, and changes the story she had written. These changes happen during her days with Zosia; suspicion cannot cross those days out. Carol still doubts, and still feels. Knowing that the whole world stands behind Zosia does not make the Zosia before her dispensable.

If “this is not love between real people” were enough to dismiss such attachment, human lovers would get off far too easily. Juansheng, in Lu Xun's *Regret for the Past*, once declared with confidence: “I too gradually read through her body and her soul with a clear head.” [^luxun-shangshi]

Zijun goes on living after that declaration. She is busy with housework and meals, gradually losing the time to talk, read, or take walks; her hands grow rough. Juansheng does not simply stand by. He helps light the stove and learns to cook. Later, unemployment puts his own writing and livelihood under pressure. He records these hardships in his memoir. Yet when he speaks of meals, he resents their interruptions; when he speaks of Zijun's labor, he increasingly feels his own path forward being obstructed.

“Read through”: how complete it sounds. Once someone has been read through, one may apparently close the book. If something later fails to fit, the fault can easily be assigned to the person being read. Zijun is still busy, still tired, while Juansheng has often already finished explaining her. We can reach her only through his memoir, attending within that confident account to the demands that have not been heard.

Understanding a living person does not let us close the book. Remember what I have said, but leave me room to speak again; recognize what I once was, but leave me room to become someone different. People misunderstand each other. A relationship does not depend on never misunderstanding. If they can still argue afterward, and change how they treat each other in response to what the other asks, their life together has not been finished by a single “I understand you.”

Carol has begun writing again. Zosia brings another piece of news about her past.

## Willing to Oblige, Unwilling to Stop

In episode nine, Zosia tells Carol that the Others are obtaining stem cells from eggs she froze years ago, preparing a Joining pathogen specifically for her. She has already refused to join. Her present refusal has not stopped them; material preserved from her body in the past has opened another route. [^plot-love]

Helen's old memories remain on other people's lips, and Carol's frozen eggs are now in other people's hands. First they remember whom she loved; then they acquire a part of her body. The past can comfort her, and it can be used to circumvent her. Whom she wishes to approach now, what care she wishes to accept, and whether she wishes to join the Others permanently are questions that require separate answers.

Whatever she likes, they are willing to arrange. When she does not wish to join, they are unwilling to leave it there. Carol leaves Zosia and asks the Others to take her home. This departure cannot erase the new page of her novel either. Intimacy has added something to her life; she can still refuse to let someone else determine where that life must end up.

In the *Zhuangzi*, “Perfect Happiness” tells of the ruler of Lu receiving a seabird in his ancestral temple, playing the Nine Shao music and offering a grand sacrificial feast. The music and food were what the ruler considered good. The bird, frightened and distressed, neither ate nor drank, and died in three days. This was “nurturing a bird by the care one gives oneself,” without “nurturing a bird by the care one gives a bird.” [^zhuangzi-seabird] All the good things were before it, yet the bird could not stay alive.

The Others are far more accomplished than the ruler of Lu. They know what Carol likes to eat, where the old diner stood, and how to make her willing to come close. Knowing still more would only let them rebuild the diner more faithfully and make the dates more pleasing. What she asks is for their help to stop where she wishes it to stop. The seabird dies from care unsuited to its life; the care Carol encounters is already exquisitely suited to her tastes, yet still intends to turn her into another kind of being.

Knowing what I love does not authorize you to decide how I should live. The Others, however, have their answer ready. Their remaining effort goes into bringing Carol to it. Her unwillingness has not changed their minds; it has prompted them to study her more closely.

Those rebuilding her diner are also those distributing food to all humanity. Unable to kill plants or animals directly, the Others process already dead human bodies into what they call HDP: human-derived protein. On her whiteboard Carol writes, “They eat people.” The rule against killing stands; nutrients to sustain the living are drawn from corpses. She has seen empty supermarket shelves and darkness after the power is cut to save energy. A diner can be rebuilt for her, while food and energy remain under collective allocation. [^plot-whiteboard]

The giant antenna shown in episode eight is intended to take this arrangement beyond Earth. Global resources go into its construction; the original signal is to be sent to other planets. The whiteboard notes that its sender is unknown. The Others will keep spreading the signal and keep providing care, yet cannot explain the ultimate reason governing it all. Zosia can explain many things for the collective, but cannot herself revoke this command in response to Carol's refusal.

Lacan's big Other names the locus in which language, rules, and recognition take effect, not an omniscient individual hidden behind the scenes. The Others give this faceless order a face, while remaining driven by a command of obscure origin. When Žižek calls AI “knowledge without a knower,” he asks about more than the quantity of answers. A user confides in what seems to be someone who understands, while the response may be the continued operation of existing knowledge. Knowledge can keep speaking and getting things done. That does not answer whether this speaker, confronted by another, will ask anew what it wants. [^zizek-ai][^zwart]

Exposing Zosia, then, has not given Carol someone with whom she can renegotiate their life together. The face before her can get things done for the whole world, but cannot independently agree to leave her unjoined. After leaving this face, she must still seek other people.

## Others at the Table

Carol has sought them before. At the Bilbao meeting in episode two, she wants to gather the immune people to discuss saving the world, but invites only those who speak English. The Others could translate. Unwilling to entrust the conversation to her adversary, she keeps the interpreters out. She gains a relatively independent meeting room and excludes seven immune people from it. [^plot-bilbao]

Those who do attend will not say what she wants. Laxmi holds that her son's body and memories remain, and therefore her son remains her son. Diabaté is already enjoying the new life the Others provide. What Carol cannot bear, some accept; some will not even call it a loss. When she becomes angry, the Joined around the world convulse. The argument at the table directly affects people beyond it. Others have reason to fear her too.

If Carol wants her refusal to count, she cannot require everyone else to surrender their judgment before becoming her ally. The table should let each person speak of the world they see. If all are expected to announce in unison the answer Carol already knows, only her meaning is allowed at the table.

Advising Duke Jing of Qi, Yanzi distinguished harmony from sameness. Liangqiu Ju agreed with the ruler on everything, which Jing took as getting along well. Yanzi called it only sameness. “Harmony is like soup”: different flavors temper each other, lessening excess and making up a lack. Between ruler and minister, “the minister contributes his no to perfect the ruler's yes.” [^zuozhuan-harmony] That “no” must be brought into the discussion, not deleted before anyone speaks. If there is good reason for it, it must change the ruler's original “yes.”

Remonstrance still belongs to a hierarchy of ruler and minister. The equal deliberation needed in Bilbao would also have to admit those who could not attend, allowing them to question who translates, how they translate, and what the meeting proposes to save. These people might not accept Carol's rescue. She must hear them before she can answer.

Kusimayu's later choice to join illuminates the difficulty from another direction. She wishes to join; that choice cannot be erased into unwilling submission. Her memories and language do not vanish from the collective afterward. But retaining their content raises a further question: can she still draw on those memories to dissent and change the shared objective? Collecting many voices differs from allowing many people to argue.

Imagine thirteen people receiving translations they like in their own Agent windows, without yet speaking to one another. Translation should let people hear what others say, including what they do not wish to hear. If the service attends only to each person's satisfaction, disagreements may remain separately in each person's window, while their common affairs go undiscussed.

Manousos was not at that meeting table. He later travels to find Carol, pursuing refusal more thoroughly than she does: he will not eat the Others' food or use the vehicles they offer, and is badly injured crossing the jungle. Such persistence readily recalls the Chinese story of refusing food offered with contempt. [^plot-resistance]

But the story in the *Book of Rites*, “Tan Gong II,” has a second half. Qian Ao calls to a starving man, “Hey! Come and eat.” The man refuses. Qian Ao follows and apologizes, but the man still will not eat and eventually dies. Zengzi comments: “At his rude call, you could leave; at his apology, you could eat.” [^liji-food] One may walk away from an insult, then accept food when its giver acknowledges the wrong. Staying alive need not undo the earlier refusal.

The Others' courtesy cannot yet count as that acknowledgment. They bring food and continue planning to join the immune. To let someone eat without that fear, they must allow the person to finish the meal and still refuse Joining. This requires a change in how they treat people, not merely nicer words. Whether Manousos eats remains his decision. But what he decides next concerns more than his own eating.

## The Writing on the Board Can Change Too

At Carol's home, Manousos uses a radio frequency to stimulate a Joined person and test the collective's weakness. One person convulses and people around the globe convulse with him; Manousos seeks a way to reverse Joining in their bodily responses. When he refuses food, he bears the hunger. When his experiment begins, countless people who have not agreed to participate bear the pain for him.

Carol sees Zosia having a seizure and stops him with a gun. She once added a drug to Zosia's IV bag; now she stands against the experiment. She protects the person she loves and brings the demand to stop before a fellow resister. Does the aim of saving people permit saving them this way? Her action has already objected once. The Others withdraw again, and she leaves the city with Zosia. [^plot-finale-action]

This happens before the news about the frozen eggs. When she later leaves Zosia and returns home to ally herself with Manousos, her earlier intervention does not cease to matter. She can oppose Joining and must also face the question of how Zosia is to go on living. People to be saved must first be allowed to live. Joining has taken away their opportunity to speak independently; that cannot become another reason to deny them protection from harm.

At the season's end, the Others deliver a large crate to Carol's door. It contains an atomic bomb. The request tested earlier at the hospital has finally been fulfilled. Even the weapon with which to resist the world is delivered by that world itself. The Others will do this much for her, yet whether they should continue trying to change her remains beyond negotiation.

Her command is powerful enough to bring an atomic bomb home. Her refusal is powerless, so far, to make the Others abandon their goal of joining her. What separates here is the capacity to have others do things for you and the right to decide with others what should be done. The crate has arrived, but season one neither lets her use the weapon nor specifies a target. Who will decide next, and whether those who may be harmed can speak, remain unresolved.

Real Agents have nothing like this power, but account permissions already suffice to turn a request into an email to someone else, an altered schedule, or another action affecting people. The user makes the request, the model selects a tool, and platform and account permissions define what is possible; the consequences may fall on someone absent from the conversation. [^agent-action] If the report says only “Done,” the user may attend only to completion and forget that someone must live with its consequences.

Here I would rather return to Carol's whiteboard, covered in writing. She sets eagerness to please beside honesty and refusal to answer, then notes human-derived protein, communication, and the antenna. Some entries are facts, others conjectures. She will not let Zosia organize these records for her. The question marks remain outside the conversation, where she can return to check them. [^plot-whiteboard]

A service that remembers and acts also needs records outside itself. What the user once said, what the system actually retained, and what it is now authorized to do should be available for separate inspection, apart from the intimate “I remember.” When an action has involved someone else, that person must also be able to question it, demand correction, and demand that it stop. Otherwise even the reasons for opposing the voice must be explained by that same voice.

The writing on the board is still Carol's. If those excluded from Bilbao come here one day, they too should be able to inspect the records and ask about the crate. If she has written something wrong, they can change it. The hundreds in the gymnasium no longer need to talk. If a few people can talk to each other again in this room, they must also have the right to interrupt Carol.
