This essay discusses the complete first season, including its ending.

Carol shows Zosia a new chapter of her novel. On this morning together, the story's love interest has become a woman. How the characters meet, how to make the premise work: these are things worth talking through. After death, fury, experiments, and an empty city, life once again contains something that need not immediately be put to the task of saving the world.

This small thing makes the problem of *Pluribus* harder. Carol Sturka is a bestselling novelist and one of the very few people who have not undergone the Joining. A signal from space has changed the Earth: countless people, biologically connected, share memories and knowledge and act as a single “we.” Her partner Helen dies in the upheaval. The survivors treat Carol kindly, offering to care for her and meet many of her demands, while hoping that she too will eventually join. She wants to bring back humanity as it was. Yet Zosia, who comes to her from this new world, gradually becomes her lover.

If the new world did nothing but deceive, and Carol were merely taken in during a moment of weakness, the story would be much easier. We would only have to wait for her vigilance to return, for her to recognize all that tenderness as an elaborate fraud. But the new chapter in episode eight resists this explanation. Before the kiss, Carol has already claimed to be writing again. After they become intimate, writing, sharing, and revision do become part of their life together. There is more than one way for creative work to return to a life. The political danger of this happiness does not strip either the writing or their time together of its own significance.[^e08]

What she receives is real. What she refuses does not cease to matter because of it.

This is harder to disentangle than “trading freedom for happiness.” That phrase seems to have weighed the two things in advance: comforts to be enjoyed on one side, a self that must not be sold on the other. Yet the ability to rest, write, and love is already part of freedom. And the Carol who angrily declares herself free of domination has also exercised power over someone else's body. What most deserves our attention in *Pluribus* is how love makes resistance difficult—and how it makes the old way of resisting untenable. To fall in love with the world one wants to resist need not mean simply surrendering. It may mean finally becoming unable to regard that world as an obstacle to be demolished regardless of the cost.

## A Person Can Rest

In losing Helen, Carol loses far more than someone who would listen. Someone had shared the work of sustaining her routines, her career, her habits; someone knew how she both depended on and despised her own books, and knew the parts of her life she could not comfortably show the public. Death breaks these relations without making the houses, roads, and books disappear with them. The familiar world remains. Her life within it can no longer continue as it did.

The Others, however, retain Helen's memories. Strangers can answer Carol out of a dead woman's past, something close to comfort and close to an affront. Remembering someone does not bring her back. Even an exact account of everything that happened may offer no one who can receive the words Carol is only now about to speak. Helen's absence is therefore not a piece of missing data awaiting completion. It unfolds in time: whatever happens next, Carol will never experience it together with this person again.

It is into this time, still moving forward, that Zosia gradually enters. They have days to spend together, conversation, games, closeness, and a manuscript. Carol need not hold her identity as a resister aloft every day to prove that she has not been subdued. When someone has spent a long time exhausting herself in the effort to hold herself together, finally being able to stop keeping such a grip on everything can be good in its own right.

Opposition to the Joining can quietly borrow an ascetic standard here: the more miserable you are, the more certain it is that you have not been assimilated; the lonelier you are, the more certain it is that something inside remains beyond purchase. By this measure, anything that helps a person settle into life becomes suspect. Good food, hot water, friendship, and love must first prove that they will not weaken resistance. A life becomes a task of vigilance that never ends, and even rest has to account for itself to the watchkeeper.

This is not a good enough freedom. Hunger can occupy a person's whole mind; illness can narrow the range of possible action; prolonged fear can leave someone unable even to finish saying what they mean. Easing these burdens may allow people to feel, make, think, and decide more fully. We have no reason to require those receiving help to declare that they do not love it before we will recognize their judgment.

Qing the woodworker, who carves a wooden bell stand in the “Mastering Life” chapter of the *Zhuangzi*, offers another account of such an experience. The finished object astonishes people, and the ruler of Lu asks what art he possesses. Qing begins instead with how he stills his mind. First he stops thinking of rewards and rank, then forgets praise and blame, skill and clumsiness, and finally forgets even his own body. Isolate the word “forget,” and it might seem that all mental activity is to dissolve here. But the passage goes on to describe him entering the forest and examining the timber. Only when the wood and the object he intends to make are suited to one another does he begin: “Otherwise, I leave it be.”[^zhuangzi]

Forgetting himself has not made the wood irrelevant. On the contrary, he can see what is before him more clearly, knowing when to work and when to refrain. What he has set down is the tangle of rewards, penalties, and self-assessment that catches him before he starts. He has not set down his discernment about the work.

The passage that follows describes Dongye Ji driving a team of horses. His advances, retreats, and turns all conform to the rules; the performance is splendid, and Duke Zhuang orders him to keep circling. Yan He, however, sees that the horses' strength is running out. Trouble duly follows. The movements can remain orderly after the bodies can no longer bear them; the better they fulfill a demand, the more likely the demand is to continue. Read together, the two stories deny smooth performance any meaning on its own. We must ask whom that smoothness enables to act, and whom it makes less able to stop.

Carol's new chapter deserves to be understood through this distinction. An author who stops constantly proving whether she deserves to be an author can begin to attend seriously to her fictional characters. Once she makes a little room away from her reputation, failures, and self-disgust, the work can become more than a report card on its maker. This capacity is something the new world gives her. It will also become part of how she judges that world.

The novel has a dignity of its own. It can please readers, work through desires its author still does not fully understand, or give an experience a different arrangement in which to keep growing. It need not oppose the Joining on every page for Carol to be speaking in her own voice. If we count only investigative records, reversal plans, and protest slogans as meaningful writing, we have already decided for her the sole purpose a writer may serve.

## The Voices That Remain

The Others have not remade everyone's experience into the same experience.

When Carol's novels come up in episode four, Larry can draw on a reader named Moira's experience to explain what those books once did for a particular person. Carol presses further and hears Helen's different judgments of her published work and another manuscript. The judgments do not align. Nor has the collective revised them into a compromise review. It can express appreciation and report a lack of appreciation; it remembers the help a book gave one person, and remembers that someone close to its author did not therefore consider it a good book.[^e04]

This detail tests our understanding more severely than a row of faces turning at once. The easiest horror to imagine gives everyone the same expression and the same sentence to repeat, deleting names, tastes, and histories one by one. *Pluribus* allows for a richer possibility. Many voices remain, and opinions of the same book can still differ. Unified action need not come at the cost of impoverished content.

That richness matters to Carol. An author can spend years trapped between two judgments: the market calls you a success, while a harsher gaze says you amount to little. Both center the author. Both rule on the question “What sort of person am I?” Moira's experience introduces a different question: once the book reached someone else, what kind of life did it enter? The meaning of the work no longer entirely coincides with its author's standing. Carol need not first establish which rank of writer she belongs to in order to have a reason to go on writing.

Yet preserving an opinion is still different from its holder remaining able to go on speaking. An archive can record that two people disagreed; two people in a living relationship can alter that disagreement through their next conversation. An accurate restatement of a past judgment is one kind of continuity. The person who made it remaining able to hesitate over new events, depart from who they have been, and take responsibility for a choice not arranged in advance is another.

Ordinary people say “we” too. Living together does not require every sentence to list every dissent. A plural pronoun alone tells us little about how those who use it may go on sharing a life. The questions must be asked of this particular world: how does the biological connection work, how does its common purpose persist, and could one person's new experience change what everyone is doing? The first season shows us many differences being drawn upon. It has not yet shown an internal dispute capable of changing the goal of the Joining. This makes the danger discernible. It does not give the viewer omniscience, or warrant a declaration that no subjects remain within it.

To understand the danger further, we need to distinguish what a person retains from how a person becomes who they are. In his account of Lacan, Bruce Fink takes care to distinguish the subject from the individual, the conscious ego, and the “I” in a sentence.[^fink] Being able to recount one's biography in full does not mean being wholly at one with oneself. Being temporarily unable to explain why one is this way does not mean having lost oneself.

The names we borrow, the language we learn, and the expectations we inherit from others precede some of our apparently most private wishes. A writer wants to become a “real writer,” but “real” already contains a whole set of other people's measures. A lover asking “Is it me you love?” is not requesting a report in which physical features and personality traits match perfectly. They want to know how the other person has become involved in this relationship beyond these articulable reasons, and who they themselves have become within it.

The subject is not a little piece of original material hidden behind every relation. Strip away dependence layer by layer, and the ego that thinks it can finally exist on its own will also lose the language and experience through which it took shape. People form within these relations and begin to ask questions where they do not quite coincide with them. Sometimes a sentence used for years to explain oneself suddenly stops working. Sometimes a wish one has already endorsed, once fulfilled, can no longer offer a place to settle. A few more pieces of information will not necessarily resolve this inconsistency.

Nor is this a celebration of dissatisfaction. The fact that desire cannot be exhausted by a ready-made list does not mean people ought never to obtain what they want, eat their fill, or find love returned. Artificially manufacturing scarcity only adds avoidable suffering to desire. What matters here is a person's remaining able to enter into a new relation with their own wishes: I did want this—do I still want it now? You have changed me; why must that change still lead to the original ending?

The unsettling aspect of the Joining can now be stated more precisely. It need not delete differences. It may be extraordinarily good at preserving, understanding, and providing for them, while making them primarily into content that a shared goal can use. You like this food, miss that city, remember a childhood no one else lived: all of this can remain. As for where it must ultimately lead, the service has not made that predetermined direction disappear.

## A Lowercase You

Carol asks Zosia what preferences are her own. She is looking for a lowercase “you,” not the “we” that can speak for all humanity every time it opens its mouth. Zosia talks about the place of her childhood, and about mango ice cream. Her answer contains somewhere a body once lived and a taste that can be named. For a moment, they give this “you” a recognizable outline.[^e08]

The question is moving, and can easily send criticism astray. It is tempting to think that finding a few unique attributes in Zosia would prove that true love survives, while discovering that others can recount the same memories would expose the love as counterfeit. Love then begins to resemble the custody of an unrepeatable collector's item, its most important quality being that it exists nowhere else.

Sharing something does not automatically cancel intimacy. Two people can like the same song without its losing its meaning for them; many people understanding a novel does not make a particular reading unreal. Closeness also depends on time actually spent together: when something was said, who was distressed at the time, what the listener did next. Knowledge can be passed on. Time already shared cannot be made, retrospectively, never to have been shared.

The changes a shared mind makes to a relationship plainly reach far beyond two people liking the same song. But this at least stops one conclusion from arriving too quickly: other bodies' access to Zosia's memories does not prove that nothing has happened between Carol and Zosia. Whether their experience together is real and how the original Zosia continues to exist are related but distinct questions.

What really happens between them is not confined to Carol's feelings, either. Zosia's body can be injured, and the Others can ask to save her. Caring for a body should not have to wait until we settle the philosophy of consciousness. If Carol were to say that so many others remain connected to Zosia that hurting her would only mean sending a replacement, shared memory would become a convenient reason to deny a particular injury. Here the urgency of not treating a body as disposable exceeds the question of whether its personality data are unique.

As for whether Zosia loves Carol out of her own desire, the first season provides no test that we could take elsewhere and apply. Her loving words may be sincere, and may at the same time serve the collective's goal of bringing Carol into the Joining. The two need not even exclude each other. Someone can care earnestly for another person while firmly believing that the other must ultimately accept a way of life they regard as good. Sincerity does not automatically undo that insistence.

The unknown between lovers should therefore not be understood as a few secrets that must be preserved to keep a relationship interesting. If information is missing, one can ask; fewer misunderstandings are usually a good thing. The deeper unknown is that neither person yet knows what the relationship will make of them, so neither can possess its answer in advance. Even if I know every part of your past, I must still face the possibility that what you say now will change me. Otherwise, what I know is an increasingly accurate version of you, rather than a you who can join me in changing the life we share.

If there is something in Carol's lowercase “you” that information cannot satisfy, it lies here. She wants more than to extract a file called Zosia from the dictionary of “we.” She is asking: can this time together make a claim on you? Now that we have loved, can something that previously had to happen no longer have to happen?

The question also turns back upon her. Can she allow the Zosia before her to be more than a stage she must pass through on the way to restoring humanity? If Zosia's good life differs from the world Carol wants to restore, is she willing to take that difference seriously? Love cannot settle the dispute in advance. It can make it increasingly difficult to say that the other person is merely part of a world that is wrong.

## Whose Body Can Wait

In episode four, seeking answers about the Joining, Carol drugs Zosia and prevents others from intervening. She loudly defends her agency, her power to act. Zosia's heart later stops, and the Others must even ask permission to resuscitate her.[^e04]

It is very hard to take a comfortable side in this conflict. Carol is not asking about something trivial: what has happened to humanity, and can it be reversed? The collective's goodwill cannot stand in for an answer. But the importance of her questions does not turn the body under the drug's influence into an unclaimed object. As Carol defends her own agency, someone else's survival waits for her permission.

“Respect my choice” has reached an embarrassing point. A person's ability to issue commands, and the seriousness with which everyone around them obeys, do not guarantee a free relationship. If it becomes harder and harder for others to interrupt her actions, what she gains may be a position of domination. In the name of protecting the individual, other people's bodies become secondary.

The Others subsequently withdraw, leaving Carol an empty city. Though she still has supplies and a house, she gradually finds no place for herself in the days. Loneliness eventually makes her desperate for their return, but the withdrawal first has a reason in self-protection: Zosia needs to get away from a danger that has already brought her close to death. Their needs conflict here. The suffering Carol endures cannot alone explain why this happened.

The same action can have a legitimate protective reason and produce an enormous disparity of power. When an ordinary person leaves, it remains one person's departure. When the other party is connected to an entire city's population, communications, and services, keeping a distance can change your whole living environment. The power to leave a relationship is so different in the two parties' hands. Carol's need for connection is real, and the Others' need for safety is real. The former cannot require the latter to remain forever present. Nor can the latter make the consequences of such immense power cease to be a problem simply by having no ill intent.

By episode nine, the direction has changed. Manousos continues looking for a way to undo the Joining, and his radio experiment puts Zosia in danger. Carol stops the experiment, then leaves with her. This happens before their travels and the later news about Carol's frozen eggs.[^e09] What makes her interrupt the experiment at this moment is the harm being done to the person before her.

She sees a particular body suffering for a goal decided by others. Earlier, she herself made this body wait for an answer. Now she will not wait any longer. This change need not yet amount to a principle she applies fairly to everyone. The injured person is the one she loves; private attachment plainly plays its part. Were the person someone she was not close to, the first season cannot guarantee that she would be equally willing to stop the experiment.

But recognizing an injury often begins with “this person cannot bear any more.” We cannot demand that concern withdraw merely because it remains partial, so that a more abstract humanity can carry on advancing. Private feeling sometimes makes us disregard others; sometimes it brings before us the others previously hidden behind a grand purpose. We must look at which actions it stops and which it allows to continue.

Manousos's persistence matters. He refuses to settle comfortably into a world in which the original people have been changed, and he does not abandon the search for a remedy just because most people around him express satisfaction. But persistence does not automatically confer a right to experiment. Someone convinced that he is saving everyone still has to explain who will bear the risk, why it falls on this person, how the experiment can be stopped, and whether those he would save want something else. If every present injury is deferred for discussion until after victory, resistance reproduces the structure it opposes: the destination is fixed, and particular people are left to pay the costs along the way.

Carol's insistence that the experiment stop can therefore be a political change brought about by love. It does not give her a way to save the world. It begins to make “saving the world” answerable to the life before her. This deserves more serious attention than whether she maintains her hostility without a moment's lapse.

Later, the news about her frozen eggs reveals another violation. She has already refused the collective permission to obtain the stem cells it needs through invasive sampling in order to develop a way of bringing her into the Joining. Now she is told that it is trying to use eggs preserved in the past.[^e06][^e09] She has protected her body in the present, but material she saved for a different kind of life has become a route around her refusal.

What is appropriated is both bodily material and a past choice. That choice did not authorize every future use at once. Preserving eggs for one possible life does not mean consenting to others' use of them to arrange another form of existence. Material being technically available and permission being given within a relationship are two different things. Behind her refusal of a sampling procedure is a more direct statement: I do not want to join. Avoiding the sampling does not answer it.

Traveling with Zosia, choosing to be close to her, handing her a new chapter: none becomes a substitute signature consenting to the Joining. The danger is that care can continue, love can continue to happen, and the plan can still proceed in its original direction. The relationship has entered Carol's life. That destination has not yet changed in response to her.

So she chooses resistance again. But this does not cancel all the love that came before. If she can regain the right to refuse only by admitting that she never truly loved, the other side's power acquires another layer: even her understanding of her own past must submit to that destination.

## Someone Else's Life in the Old Diner

Episode eight also stages an exquisitely attentive act of nostalgia. Carol recalls a diner from her youth. She worked at night and wrote in the mornings then; life was hard, but she remembers those days as good. The Others rebuild the diner and bring her old surroundings back before her. Even a waitress named Bri returns.

But Bri has since moved to Miami to become an esthetician, and is newly married. For the sake of Carol's memory, she has been summoned back to resume being the diner's waitress. Once Carol learns this, she challenges the arrangement.[^e08]

The past suddenly acquires another layer. For Carol, this is a diner bound up with her early writing. For Bri, it is part of a life she subsequently left behind. A place in which two people once coexisted occupies a different position in each of their lives. The power rebuilding it now gives priority to one person's longing, returning the other to where that longing requires her to be.

One may wish an old shop were still there, or hope to walk the paths of one's youth again. Such longing has a tenderness of its own. Once a wish can actually direct other people, however, the nature of what happens is no longer determined solely by the feelings of the person remembering. Carol wants her youth back. Bri must return to a job she left long ago.

The accuracy this service pursues may even obstruct what it hopes to restore. The hardships of youth need not be remembered as good days because Carol loved poverty. Perhaps she was then making a life that had not yet settled into shape. What her work would become, who she would become, had not yet been fixed by success and self-disgust. The diner can be restored, the menu made the same. Someone who has already lived everything that came afterward cannot, through these things, return to a time before she knew what came next.

Bri especially cannot. Her life has unfolded elsewhere, and a reconstruction that succeeds too well draws that later life into its materials as well. The richer the preserved memories, the more exact the arrangement can be; the easier it becomes to call up someone else's history as the scenery of my own. The Joining's power here does not lie in ignorance of human differences. It lies in knowing a great deal about them and being able to make them serve a reenactment.

Carol's objection matters. She does not remain comfortably enthroned as the recipient of this magnificent service. She begins to see that a supporting character in her memory has also left that stage and lived days that did not continue for her sake. Intimacy makes one body irreplaceable; the diner brings another person's later life into view. Her ethics need not arise entirely from holding fast to her old self. Some of it emerges precisely as she receives a gift and objects to the way it has been arranged.

This also makes “love costs people their independence” seem too crude. In Lu Xun's *Regret for the Past*, Zijun firmly declares that she belongs to herself and that neither her father nor her paternal uncle has the right to interfere. As she says this, she is moving toward a shared life. Love gives her the strength to oppose the old relationships. Independence does not wait until she has escaped love to begin.[^shangshi]

What Lu Xun writes next, however, concerns housing, housework, unemployment, food, and the gradual loss of time for reading and conversation. Two people may begin a new life in love, but the courage of that beginning cannot spare them the conditions the life will continue to require. Zijun works day and night. Juansheng sees her exhaustion while increasingly experiencing the tasks that keep them alive as impediments to his own work. Love has not settled, by its own power, how labor will be shared.

Juansheng later imagines a way forward unencumbered by the relationship. He seeks to escape falsehood by telling Zijun he no longer loves her; he also imagines it will free her to act without ties. Yet who speaks a truth, and the position in which it leaves the other person, cannot be guaranteed by the word “truth.” Only after Zijun leaves does he face more closely where the burden he has put down has gone. The story unfolds through his remorse and self-defense, refusing to let an exalted declaration of independence wash this transfer away.

In *Regret for the Past*, the pressures of making a living gradually render the couple's shared life unsustainable. In *Pluribus*, Carol receives far more than she ever had before. These circumstances are very different, yet they reveal the same question that love can easily hide: how our capacity to live is supported by relationships and sustained by what others contribute. Carol's ability to write, travel, and rest cannot simply be counted as her finally having time to express her inner life. Where that time comes from, and who keeps daily life going for her, also belong to the substance of this freedom.

She need not feel ashamed of accepting care. What needs asking is whether that care can become a condition for people to change their lives together, or whether only its recipient may enjoy it while the caregiver is continually returned to an assigned place. Condemning all dependence as weakness obscures the question; merely celebrating lovers' newfound need for one another obscures it too. Sometimes the most private freedom between two people lies in who can put down the work in their hands, and who still has a future of their own.

## Who Sustains the Good Life

The world Carol receives and the life being lived by all the bodies of the Others are not the same picture.

The explanation of HDP in episode six makes the difference visible. Bound by a prohibition on killing or harming life, the collective faces difficulties supplying food. Its existing stores will eventually run out, so it extracts edible nutrients from the bodies of the dead. At the same time, it promises to reserve better food from the remaining supplies for Carol. The other immune people know that the collective faces the danger of famine, and discuss possible solutions such as robotic farming.[^e06]

“Eating people” is shocking enough, but it may not be the phrase that best explains what matters. The Others use bodies whose deaths have already occurred, and the people threatened by famine are also looking for ways to stay alive. The dead nourish the living, postponing the exhaustion of stored food while bringing the principle of non-harm up against its own difficulty: how can a community that professes not to harm life sustain life itself?

Being alive requires nutrition, labor, and continual changes to the external world. Delivering a meal also entails growing, harvesting, processing, transporting, and the energy all these consume. Coordinating the activities with extraordinary smoothness may reduce waste; it cannot make the activities cease to be necessary. The food placed before Carol does not escape the world's conditions of survival simply because it comes without a price or a bill.

Keeping some supplies in reserve to ensure abundance for a few and nourishing every body over the long term are therefore matters on different scales. Greater attentiveness to the former does not necessarily secure the latter. Carol can demand many things; she can even obtain weapons. The force of these private demands cannot directly be equated with a right to decide how the community will arrange its future.

A peculiar combination appears: in matters of enjoyment, the individual is almost sovereign, yet may have no corresponding standing when it comes to common purposes. The vast collective apparatus has not abolished personal particularity. It can attend to it in minute detail. You can insist on your tastes, keep your name, enjoy a journey arranged especially for you. These choices are real. They do not automatically extend to why everyone continues to be joined, or why those outside the Joining must still be brought in.

Opposing this world cannot therefore end with a demand for a few more individual choices. There are already many choices. The question is how far they reach. Someone whose wishes are assiduously satisfied may still be able to decide only the details within a direction given by others. Even if they remain skeptical and withhold inward assent, the common apparatus continues to work as usual, arranging their next meal and next journey.

Collective production, shared knowledge, and centralized coordination can relieve some of everyone's toil. Medical care, transport, and caregiving cannot all be undertaken by isolated individuals. If shared life must first be broken into mutually suspicious fragments before the individual can become free, that freedom will be available only to those who can afford isolation.

The distinction worth making is between jointly controlling the conditions of life and jointly accepting a single destination. The former allows people to change together what they cannot change alone. The latter sends everyone's abilities in a direction already decided. Both may employ similar technologies of logistics, information, and collaboration, but that does not make them the same political relationship. “Everyone is satisfied” especially cannot substitute for “How does everyone decide what life to lead?”

The Others' prohibition on harm takes this difficulty a step further. They have immense knowledge and can search for ways around obstacles. If the rule cannot change, their ingenuity is directed first toward finding ways to obey it while barely keeping bodies alive. Testing whether robots can perform some operations is a meaningful attempt. But even if a solution is found, an engineering success will not settle once and for all which actions count as harm, or why harm is defined in that way.

Any society will encounter conflicts between principles. Recognizing that life deserves protection does not automatically tell people how to farm. Recognizing that individuals deserve respect does not automatically tell people how to act in the face of experiments and shared dangers. Only through their consequences in lived life do rules disclose whom they protect and what they leave outside that protection. If consequences can only spur those carrying out a rule to obey it more cleverly, without in turn changing the rule, immense knowledge may be kept busy sustaining a necessity it cannot reconsider.

These difficulties do not yet constitute a complete economic explanation. They have already changed the meaning of the seemingly omnipotent service Carol receives. Attentiveness and scarcity coexist: an individual can be cared for very well while life as a whole still faces material limits. She sees a world that knows how to look after her. That world is also trying to keep itself alive.

This recognition should change the object of resistance. What needs changing is neither a group of people who do not deserve happiness nor a cold machine unrelated to oneself. The order being resisted is made up of bodies that can hunger and be hurt, bodies that once each had a life. Making it stop doing something must be distinguished from leaving those bodies unable to survive. Here political judgment begins to carry weight, beyond declaring which side one has chosen.

## Even the Whole World May Not Know

In his discussion of *Pluribus*, Žižek offers a conjecture worth retaining and disputing: perhaps the Others' happiness in Carol's presence has something to do with finally encountering a mind outside themselves. A whole that integrates countless experiences might have a loneliness of its own.[^zizek]

The loneliness of the whole can coexist with the happiness of companionship. Needing another mind because one is lonely may even lend that happiness greater significance. What this conjecture unsettles is the picture of a Joining that has absorbed every outside and no longer needs to ask anyone anything. A whole gathering countless experiences may also depend on something beyond itself.

In the same lecture, Žižek preserves another distinction. The show's Others do not directly equal the Lacanian Other, and cannot simply be declared an apparatus of pure functioning from which subjects have vanished. They are materially connected, yet may still face an Other of their own: the demand transmitted by the signal, a mission they can execute without necessarily being able to give a final account of its origin or reason.[^zizek]

The Other is not simply a brain bigger than mine. It concerns the order on which language, rules, and recognition depend: why must I do this, before whom does my speech count, who seems to know what I should become? No omniscient being need actually sit behind this position. People often act according to a demand held to be inviolable even when no one possesses all the answers.

The Joining gathers innumerable people's knowledge without thereby proving that the world finally knows itself. It may be able to answer how to execute a task and still be unable to answer why this particular purpose must be served. Who sent the signal, and within what relations the senders themselves exist, are questions the first season does not sufficiently answer. Filling this uncertainty at once with an alien master merely rewrites an unresolved question as a villain who has yet to appear.

Even “they have questions too” needs careful distinctions. Lacking a piece of knowledge is not the same as reflecting on one's mission. Encountering a contradiction while obeying rules is not the same as actively changing them. Žižek's grounds for leaving open the possibility of subjectivity deserve further thought; they cannot be treated as proof already supplied by the plot. What matters is that the Others may not occupy the position of absolute master either. The destination they impose on Carol may also govern them.

This makes the relation between goodwill and domination harder to resolve. We are accustomed to looking for someone who knows he is doing wrong, as though finding him would locate the source of power. But a relationship can also be sustained by people who carry out its demands sincerely. Everyone is helping, everyone is looking for the least painful way, and still no one can decide the destination anew. The absence of malice does not make the problem disappear. It may only mean that those acting have no position outside the action they perform.

Looking back from this at present-day imaginings of intelligent agents, the useful comparison is not who most resembles a person. Imagine an agent that arranges someone's travel. It really does save the time spent searching, contacting people, and changing reservations, finally enabling the person to go. But understanding a wish, obtaining authority to act on someone's behalf, selecting available channels, and deciding what counts as a successful trip remain different things. The convenience can be entirely real while some decisions remain beyond the control of the person expressing the wish.

This hypothetical concerns only the relationship between a person and an apparatus that carries out tasks. Actual software is not the biologically joined humanity of the show. Training material is not the memory everyone is currently sharing, and tool permissions are not an irresistible biological command. Erasing these differences would make it impossible to judge what can be changed, what requires consent, and which organization should answer for the consequences. A voice that sounds attentive does not establish that the entire relationship is attentive. Equally, a voice produced by a machine does not prove that the time and capacities it provides are illusory.

*Pluribus* gives these questions a weight beyond the software comparison, because what stands before Carol is not a dialogue box she can close at any time. There is Zosia's body, Bri's life unfolding elsewhere, and many people facing food shortages. Carol wants to change the common purpose, and has already become entangled with the world sustaining it. Clear awareness cannot dissolve those ties on its own.

## After Love

At the end of the first season, Kusimayu chooses to join. What terrifies Carol means, to her, a life worth moving toward. This disagreement should be allowed to stand. Her willingness cannot sign anyone else's consent, but neither can we preserve the justice of resistance by declaring in advance that she does not understand what she wants.

Carol, too, gains no privilege to determine everyone's destination simply by being the one whose boundaries have been violated. She resumes working with Manousos and brings back a nuclear weapon. The finale does not tell us how it will be used, against whom, or with what consequences.[^e09] Its presence makes it difficult to tidy her earlier intervention in the experiment into a story of moral growth. If one particular body must not be subjected to experiments at will, how is this greater power of destruction to be restrained? Can the life she sees in her lover also prevent distant people she does not love from becoming the cost?

The season leaves the question here for more than the suspense a second season needs. Carol can no longer simply return to the person she was at the beginning. She has received care, written a new chapter, and spent time with Zosia that she cannot subsequently treat as never having happened. She has also harmed her, and others have had to leave Carol to find temporary safety. Love has not made her a person who is naturally right. It has made “I am here to save you” increasingly unable to supply the whole reason for her actions.

Lu Xun's *Village Opera* also contains a happiness in which one almost forgets oneself. A boat moves over the water; flute music mingles with the night air; a boy's heart grows still, then seems about to diffuse into the surroundings. If every loss of self is read as danger, this night too will be put on trial without cause. Yet its happiness cancels neither the children's bodies nor their ability to change their minds. They borrow a boat, take turns rowing, tire of watching the opera, and agree to return. Hungry on the way, they find beans, shell them, cook them, and share them.[^shexi]

That night does not consist of every wish being neatly fulfilled by someone else. The opera has its disappointments; the beans involve someone else's field; salt, firewood, and boats have owners; and the visiting boy receives special treatment. Lu Xun has not written a village without distinctions or unequal privileges. Even the praise at the end retains the favorable treatment accorded to an educated person. Yet the happiness does occur in time when a group do things together and change their direction together. The self recedes for a while without that time being taken out of their hands.

This lets us expect something of Carol both more generous and more exacting than “always remain clear-eyed.” Generous, because she may be happy, dependent, and temporarily forget that she is a resister. She need not prove through permanent loneliness that she has sold nothing away. Exacting, because the life she receives must no longer leave other people's bodies, labor, and futures out of sight. Nor can the world she seeks to change merely transfer the power to prescribe the destination from “we” to “I.”

Equally, if the shared life Zosia represents is to answer Carol, it must do more than prove it can make her happy. It must give the words spoken within a relationship a chance to change that relationship's direction. Saying she is willing to love you does not mean saying she is willing to become you. Accepting the life you give does not mean allowing you to decide how she must live from then on. What is truly hard to bear is not that something remains unknown between two people. It is that one already knows where the relationship must end up, and treats every unsettled wish of the other as a winding route to that destination.

Between love and resistance, Carol cannot simply choose one side to preserve her innocence. What love obliges her to is not obedience, but a more concrete responsibility for shared life. What resistance asks her to seek is also more than a self forever unchanged by anyone else. She has already been changed. What she must still ask is whether this change can lead to a future that neither has possessed in advance, and neither can bring about alone.
