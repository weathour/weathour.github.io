At a table in Osaka, Haruki has to tell Setsuna why he came to see her. The screen offers two answers: "I only wanted to see you" and "Tell her the truth about Kazusa."

Each answer contains part of the truth. Haruki did want to see Setsuna, and he had left Tokyo to avoid Kazusa's concert. The first choice lets the first fact stand for the whole reason. Everything that follows goes smoothly: their hands meet across the table, Haruki has already booked a double room, and the two prepare to treat the past few weeks as if they never happened. The second answer begins, "I've been seeing Touma again. Every day." He goes on to describe the neighboring rooms, two weeks of daily care, his concealment, and the happiness he felt with Kazusa. Setsuna does not fall into his arms. She says first that she already knows. When he has finished, she tells him, "Even so, I won't forgive you."[^xii]

Her slap is so light that it can scarcely hurt Haruki, yet it changes where everyone will go that night. Setsuna makes him return and face Kazusa. She refuses to become his place of escape and says that, for the time being, she will not contact him first. They part without a kiss, without holding hands, without even touching fingertips. The script renders the difference in physical detail. The same man reaches the same city, but the amount he discloses determines whether the other person can stop an embrace, set a distance, and send him back to the relation he has yet to settle.

The last two menus come later. Kazusa proposes a "false love" that will last only until the end of winter, and the screen first asks the player to "Let go" or "Turn away." Haruki is gripping both her wrists. Letting go first gives Kazusa the use of her hands; she cups his face and tests the distance between them. Haruki then turns her tentative approach into a real kiss and pulls her into his arms, entering the Affair route. Turning away is the bodily refusal that night, and only then does the next question appear: "I love Setsuna" or "I can't lie to Kazusa." The same declaration, "I love Setsuna," leads either to Setsuna True or Coda Normal depending on the Osaka disclosure and the other route conditions accumulated before it.[^menus]

The three menus perform three tasks in only two physical settings. Question XII determines how much Haruki tells Setsuna in Osaka. Questions XIII and XIV follow one another in the same room: first they settle the bodily boundary between Haruki and Kazusa in that moment, then Kazusa hears his answer containing Setsuna's name. Setsuna is absent from the last statement, just as Kazusa was absent from Haruki's disclosure in Osaka. The game gives the trio no single final table at which they can choose together. It asks the player to perform three acts across two encounters between pairs, and facts disclosed in the earlier setting can close off buttons in the later one.

"I can't lie to Kazusa" does not make Haruki choose her on the spot. He goes on to say that he still cannot accept her and has not yet decided. The reciprocal demands come five days later, after Kazusa turns back from the airport. Walkthroughs reconstruct this button as the route toward Kazusa True; Question XIV itself has not yet completed the characters' final choice.[^menus]

These menus stretch a "choice" into a history. The weight of the final line depends on who learned what beforehand, who spoke first, who received time to answer, and who turned words into housing, work, and another meeting. [The previous essay, "The Sinner's Guilt, Other People's Lives"](/en/posts/white-album-2-guilt-and-the-days-after/) followed the money, illness, and interrupted harm that remain after confession. This essay restores that account to sequence. Once someone chooses, who still governs everyone else's days?

Soseki's three novels place the question at three different moments. *Kokoro* shows a decision completed before an answer can arrive, followed by a long letter that seals off every question. *Sorekara* lets a delayed truth reach the people involved while they are alive, so objection and limits begin at once, though income and housing remain unresolved. *The Gate* advances the relation by six years: wages, household expenses, and care have taken form, while the third person's own account remains outside. Read beside *WHITE ALBUM 2*, the novels reveal a relation that recurs across Japanese literature. Several lives have already altered one another, but decision, answer, and daily life rest in different hands.[^comparison]

## An Engagement Settled in Fifteen Minutes

In *Kokoro*, K slides open the partition between two rooms and tells Sensei that he loves the young lady. Sensei later writes that he scarcely heard the particulars of the confession. Once his body loosened from its shock, one thought rose first: "He has beaten me to it."

That sentence does not make Sensei a schemer who calculated everything from the beginning. K's confession does stun him into silence. By that afternoon, however, he feels that his chance has passed, as though declarations of love obeyed a rule of precedence: after K has spoken, the same words from him would appear "unnatural." The first paralysis and the later urge to compete both belong to him. Sensei did not write a deception in his mind and then carry it out. He moved first through panic, shame, and jealousy, and only later began to use that move against K with growing awareness.

Nor does an outsider suddenly intrude upon a household that Sensei already possesses. After K breaks with his adoptive family and loses his means of support, Sensei insists on bringing him into the widow's house and asks the mother and daughter to talk with him more often. Shizu enters K's room to talk, walks home beside him after they happen to meet in town, and helps him during the card game. None of this proves that she fell in love with K. It does establish that she was not an empty place waiting to appear when Sensei proposed: K's feelings and Sensei's jealousy both grow within an arrangement of care that Sensei built and Shizu actively inhabited.[^kokoro]

A few days later, K asks him to judge whether he should advance or retreat. Sensei throws back K's own old maxim: "Anyone without spiritual aspiration is a fool." The phrase once demanded that both men restrain desire and pursue a life of the spirit. Sensei now uses it to drive K into retreat. K finally says that he is "not entirely without resolve." The novel never settles whether this resolve means continuing to pursue the young lady, renouncing her, or preparing for something darker. Sensei's account, written years afterward, covers the ambiguous line with the shadow of K's death. That shadow cannot turn it backward into a clear suicide note.[^kokoro]

Sensei confirms one more fact: K has told no one else. The desires of both men, K's hesitation, and the young lady's daily life with K have now converged in Sensei's hands. K believes he is asking a fair-minded friend for judgment. Sensei knows that no second person can correct the version he controls.

The partition builds this asymmetry of knowledge into the house. K first opens it, crosses the threshold, and confesses. After Sensei misses his chance to answer, he waits all day for K to open it again from the other side but never walks over himself. K does not speak again as Sensei expects. On the night of the suicide, Sensei wakes to find the partition ajar and K lying in the next room; only then does the blood come into view. Sensei keeps waiting for the other person to open the boundary. Once the relation cannot be repaired, he makes "too late" the center of the life that remains.

Sensei waits several more days because he cannot find a moment when K and Shizu leave the house together. About a week later, he claims to be ill, stays home until they have gone out, and approaches the widow. He first tests whether K has said anything recently. When the widow asks whether K has confided in him, Sensei says no and immediately asks to marry her daughter. The discussion takes less than fifteen minutes. The widow says that they need not ask her daughter first because she would never give the young woman to a man she did not wish to marry. Sensei protests that the daughter herself should answer first; the mother still supplies that answer in her place. Back in his room, he feels that "the course of my future has been settled."

Their marriage later contains more than deception. Shizu loves Sensei. The couple share a long life; he cares for his mother-in-law, visits K's grave, and sincerely wants to protect his wife. Nor is marriage the end of Shizu's agency: she asks why her husband has grown so gloomy, urges him to stop drinking, and proposes that they visit K's grave together. What she lacks is the causal history behind these acts. Living out that marriage cannot retroactively supply the answer missing fifteen minutes before the engagement. The mother knows her daughter well and may have judged her wishes correctly. Her certainty still does not mean that Shizu has heard what passed between the two men or consented in her own voice to all the conditions under which the engagement arose.

*Kokoro* therefore gives Coda a severe test: decisiveness alone does not tell us how a decision was made. Sensei can propose first because he knows more than K and Shizu. He then lets the widow speak the answer Shizu might have given.

The Osaka conversation turns this problem around. Before Haruki speaks, Setsuna has already inferred from Tomo's sightings that Kazusa appeared outside his home and at the performance. Her knowledge does not release Haruki from the duty to explain. Inference tells Setsuna that the two have been meeting. Haruki's disclosure tells her how he accepted the interview, how Youko arranged for Kazusa to live next door, how he cooked for her every day and shielded her from the press, and how he felt happy with her while feeling guilty toward Setsuna. Setsuna can now cite a shared set of facts, challenge him, and alter the course of the night.

She can also rename his escape. Haruki says that he does not want to betray Setsuna again. She asks why that should allow him to betray Kazusa instead. Setsuna admits the malice she does not want to voice and the possibility that Haruki may never return. She still sends him back to Kazusa after the concert. Knowledge does not make Setsuna selfless. Once jealousy, desire, and principle can all enter the conversation, she becomes someone who changes the process of choice rather than someone waiting to be chosen.

Guessing correctly and receiving the facts from the person who acted are two different things. A guess leaves its risk of error with the person forced to make it. A disclosure lets that person refer to the same facts, object, refuse, and set another boundary. Question XII affects eligibility for Setsuna True because the game gives this difference a formal consequence: the earlier disclosure has already allowed Setsuna to take part in the choice that follows.

## A Long Letter That Leaves His Wife Outside

K leaves a short note after his suicide. He writes that he lacks will, sees no future, thanks Sensei, entrusts the aftermath to him, and asks him to apologize to the widow. The young lady alone goes unmentioned. Sensei first checks the note for anything that could damage his reputation. Only afterward does he see the blood on the partition.

The novel does not reduce K's death to disappointed love. His poverty after breaking with his adoptive family, ascetic convictions, long habit of self-coercion, and mental state have all appeared earlier. The note says little about any concrete cause. Sensei later gathers his life into a history of guilt in which he got ahead of K and K therefore died. His guilt has a basis in events, but it also absorbs all the reasons that no one can ask K to disentangle anymore.

Years later, Sensei writes a much fuller account for the young narrator. He begins with an uncle who stole his inheritance, explains why he distrusts others, recalls bringing K into the widow's house, and admits using K's moral language out of jealousy. The letter runs at great length and seems to exhaust every cause he can name. Shizu is expressly "the sole exception." Sensei says that he cannot put a drop of black into his wife's white memory. He wants her to believe that he died suddenly, even if she must think him mad. The young narrator must keep the letter secret and never show it to Shizu while she lives.[^letter]

Sincerity and control inhabit the same act. Sensei explains his concealment as protection. He says he is not trying to preserve his moral image before his wife; he cannot bear to make Shizu endure the truth. Because he describes this protection as heartfelt, a decision that belongs to Shizu can readily appear to be care that her husband must provide on her behalf: which past belongs to her, why her husband has lived in gloom, what K's grave means inside their marriage, and whether she wants to preserve a "white" past after Sensei's death.

The letter's length also acts on the living. When the young narrator receives it, his own father is dying. He reaches the last page and immediately leaves for Tokyo. The novel never returns to Shizu or tells us his father's fate. The dead man's past fills Part Three, and Sensei's voice fills all the pages that remain. The novel leaves neither living person a line of reply.

Sensei also closes off the ten or more days in which he writes. He encourages Shizu to nurse her ill aunt and hides the manuscript whenever she returns home. He arranges for the letter to reach the young narrator only after his own death. The recipient, time of delivery, and means of checking the account are all fixed in advance: who may read it, when they may read it, and whom they can still question afterward. The testament cuts question from answer and preserves only the writer's half in full.

The letter also borrows an ending from public time. Sensei has repeatedly contemplated death. After the Meiji emperor dies, he calls himself a man left behind by the spirit of Meiji. When Shizu jokes about following one's lord in death, Sensei replies that if he is to do so, it will be for the spirit of Meiji. A month later, the funeral cannon sounds and General Nogi and his wife take their own lives. Only two or three days after that does Sensei decide to die. The emperor's death and Nogi's act are not the single cause of his suicide. They give an existing wish for death a date, a vocabulary, and a public script that can be imitated. Sensei thereby absorbs Shizu's joke into a decision she will have no chance to withdraw.[^letter]

The completeness of a confession and the affected person's ability to answer must be judged separately. Someone can tell the whole history of his life while choosing the audience, order, deadline, and conditions of secrecy. The more complete his account sounds, the easier it becomes to mistake the person denied a voice for someone already cared for.

Kazusa True goes further in another direction. Haruki does not leave his decision in words. He personally breaks off the engagement, submits his resignation, finishes the proofreading, arranges the handover of his remaining work, and tells the Ogiso family that he will move to Vienna. The route's greatest force lies in his willingness to go out and do each of these things.

Yet when he and Youko discuss her illness, Haruki still filters what Setsuna will learn and assumes that family, friends, and work will help her bear the result. Setsuna first answers his proposed breakup and sacrifice. Only afterward does Haruki break off the engagement, resign, and prepare to move his life to Vienna. When Setsuna answers, she has still heard only the portion he chose to give her.[^kazusatrue]

No rule of "tell absolutely everything" can settle this problem. K speaks of his desire only to a rival, with disastrous consequences. Sensei writes nearly his whole life and then bars his wife from answering by dying. Haruki would likewise harm the people involved if he used Youko's illness as leverage to force Setsuna to accept his choice. The proper measure of disclosure is what it leaves the listener able to do: ask, wait, restrict who may know, or say that she does not want to hear it now.

Question XII matters because the disclosure occurs before the ending. Setsuna neither forgives at once nor has to deliver a final ruling. She deals only with that night: no embrace, no refuge from Kazusa, no contact initiated by her for the time being. Haruki may come to her if he needs her, but she will not listen if he comes only to run away. The truth does not solve the trio. It first gives Setsuna back a small interval in which events can still be changed.

## Three Years Late, the Truth Finally Meets an Answer

Daisuke takes three years to tell Michiyo that he loves her in *Sorekara*. Three years earlier, Hiraoka tells him that he wants to marry Michiyo, and Daisuke interprets his own silence and withdrawal as friendship and self-sacrifice. The jealousy that breaks out after their marriage and his anxiety while exchanging letters already show the desire suppressed by this account. Only in the final confrontation does Daisuke admit that he himself arranged the match. He sacrificed his own future, invoked a friend's duty and the chivalrous spirit of *gikyo*, and answered on Michiyo's behalf. The earlier recollection establishes how he understood his withdrawal, not the word *gikyo* itself; Hiraoka's later questions finally force the arrangement into a conversation between the two men.[^sorekara]

Those three years of inaction are not freedom without support. Daisuke receives a monthly allowance from his father, has an old debt paid by his brother, receives covert help from his sister-in-law Umeko's private funds, and leaves the household work to Kadono and an elderly maid. He can therefore describe life without a profession or family obligation as obedience to "nature" because family money and hired labor keep that life running.

Hiraoka loses his job, and an old debt obstructs his search for another; Michiyo has to ask Daisuke for help with that debt. She also brings the clothes left by her dead child, while heart disease following childbirth worsens amid poverty. The debt, the child's death, and the illness are not all effects caused by Daisuke. They do force the arrangement he had aestheticized as sacrifice back into the present: his withdrawal did not give Michiyo a life free of consequences.

On the day of the confession, he first confirms that Hiraoka is at the newspaper office. He buys a great armful of white lilies in the rain and summons Michiyo to his house. The rain seals them off from Kadono, the maid, and the society outside; the smell of lilies fills the room. Daisuke tells himself that he has at last returned to the "natural" past. Yet he has arranged every condition of this supposedly spontaneous return: the husband's absence, the carriage, the purchased lilies, and a house where they can be alone. Rain gives the man indoors seclusion and beauty. Elsewhere in the novel, Michiyo says the matter plainly. She has come through the rain in sandals and does not find it "good rain" at all.[^sorekara]

Daisuke begins, "My existence needs you." Michiyo's first reply through tears is, "Why did you abandon me?" He admits that he should have spoken three years earlier and presents his continued bachelorhood as the punishment he has endured. Michiyo answers at once: was that not his own choice?

Her answer changes the confession. Daisuke offers three years without marriage as proof of constancy and atonement; Michiyo returns the subject of those three years to him. She neither denies his pain nor makes the old silence harmless. He can no longer describe his decision not to marry as the revenge Michiyo imposed on him.

Michiyo then says, "Now that we have come this far, let us make up our minds." She moves the relation forward herself, without accepting a ready-made life from Daisuke. She remains married, has heart disease, lives amid debt, and possesses no independent income. Once his father cuts him off, Daisuke understands that what he calls "taking responsibility" remains an aim, not a completed fact. Money stands in the path of their life together. All he has in mind is the word "employment," with no particular job attached.

Michiyo does not let the confession remain between her and Daisuke. After she falls ill, Hiraoka takes leave from the newspaper, calls a doctor, and nurses his wife. On the second night of care, she asks him to question Daisuke; on the third day, she asks again. All three people therefore speak in sequence, though they never confront one another in the same room. Hiraoka listens to Daisuke for more than an hour, asks whether he advanced step by step while knowing he was wrong, ends their friendship, and bars Daisuke from his home. He speaks of "giving" Michiyo to Daisuke, in the grammar of a woman transferred between men. At the same time, he refuses to give her over while she is ill because he remains responsible for her care as her husband.

Hiraoka cannot be reduced to a husband who merely obstructs true love. He has been hurt and has also made Michiyo suffer in their marriage. He speaks the language of transfer between men, while calling a doctor, leaving work to care for her, and setting a boundary. His intervention does not make the situation fair. It does force Daisuke's long account of "nature" to face questions from another affected person.

At the novel's end, the family has disowned Daisuke, Michiyo remains ill, and no divorce, shared home, or common income has been secured. Daisuke addresses "Mr. Kadono" with sudden formality, says that he is going to find work, and rushes into the midsummer street. A tram starts moving. The red of postboxes, signs, balloons, and flags enters his sight until the world becomes a spinning mass of red. He has only set out to look for work; their shared life has yet to begin.[^sorekara-life]

The title *Sorekara* leaves that gap to the reader. The novel gives no report of success or failure after the confession. It places "what comes after" on its cover and stops when Daisuke has only begun to seek work. Earlier he could explain civilization, nature, and freedom at length. The ending leaves him with motion, a tram, and color. Michiyo's short sentences, Hiraoka's questions, his father's withdrawal of money, and the red of the street strip away his leisure to keep the world at the distance of an aesthetic object.

*Sorekara* loosens half of the impasse left by *Kokoro*. The truth arrives while those involved remain alive. Michiyo can challenge it, Hiraoka can impose a boundary, and Daisuke's self-explanation can be interrupted. The other half remains beyond the final page: who provides income, how Michiyo receives treatment, how the marriage ends, and whether the three can later revise what they have said today.

Setsuna True carries a related process further. The Osaka disclosure lets Setsuna learn of the crisis early. Later, Haruki asks her to "save Kazusa." Setsuna hears that he has placed Kazusa before her, yet agrees to think about the problem seriously with him and continues to intervene. She enters a direct conflict with Kazusa, who distinguishes Haruki's choice of romantic partner from her own place in his life. The route then advances farther than the ending of *Sorekara*: the trio play together again, the editorial work and recording follow, and another shared project has begun. The pressure remains. Setsuna arranges much of the mediation and renewed contact. She first reveals her recent unsent replies and the jealousy, suspicion, and anger within them, but Haruki still presses her for an answer on the spot. Setsuna asks for five minutes, yet in the same request promises that afterward she will become a woman who "understands everything" and his "home." The narration takes this as her acceptance at once. The five minutes have not yet elapsed when the answer becomes settled.[^setsunatrue]

Direct conflict matters because someone can at last answer the protagonist's private version. It remains an entrance into daily life. Who arranges the next meeting after the quarrel, and whether today's answer may alter later arrangements, determine whether conflict opens a new relation or supplies a more elegant form of closure.

## Six Years Later, Yasui's Voice Remains Outside the Book

*The Gate* does not narrate the decisive event among Sosuke, Oyone, and Yasui. It tells us only that something happened between the end of winter and late spring, like a great wind that knocked two people down. They rose covered in sand without knowing when they had fallen. The novel does not say who first confessed, how Oyone answered, or when Yasui learned what had happened. We know only that Sosuke and Oyone later became a couple and left school and their friends. The outcome does not establish the legal status of Oyone's former relation to Yasui. Soseki removes the romantic climax and gives the entire novel to what comes afterward.[^mon]

Across six years, Sosuke and Oyone scarcely quarrel. They buy cloth and sew clothes, cook rice, and live on his government salary. The novel likens them to two drops of oil on water, pressed together by the water's rejection until they can no longer separate. The image holds two facts at once. Their intimacy is real and deep; social exclusion also helped give it this shape. They left their friends, or their friends and school cast them out. Action and injury keep revising each other in the same sentence.

Oyone has no surviving line in which she chooses Sosuke, but she repeatedly decides the shape of their life six years later. When Sosuke worries about paying his brother Koroku's school fees, Oyone proposes giving up the six-mat room that contains her dressing table, providing him room and board, and splitting the remaining costs between the two households. She also takes a Hooitsu screen, recovered from Sosuke's father's home but useless in their cramped house, to be appraised. The dealer first offers six yen. Oyone gathers the nerve to identify the artist, and the couple later wait until they can sell it for thirty-five. At the Sakai house above the cliff, the same screen has cost eighty yen. Its value changes with the house and owner. Oyone's budgeting, sacrifice of space, and bargaining turn "suffering for love" into a room someone can inhabit and clothes someone can wear.

Their home stands at the foot of a cliff, while the Sakai family live above. The houses are close, but visiting requires a detour. Sunlight, children, books, servants, and a room in which to appreciate the screen collect at the top. Below, Sosuke and Oyone count shoes, rice, and salary. The screen travels uphill and rises in price. Its movement mocks the couple's ignorance of the market and makes visible the material limits within which they sustain their relationship.

When Oyone falls ill, several people care for her. Sosuke holds her aching shoulder. Koroku borrows a telephone and collects the medicine; Kiyo changes the water and applies compresses; a doctor examines her and leaves instructions. Sosuke cares for his wife in fear, while Oyone often smiles first through pain to keep him from worrying. Their love does not become counterfeit because an old wrong stands behind it. The reality of their days is exactly why "they have suffered for six years" cannot supply the reply that the novel never records from Yasui.

"It can't be helped" is often a kindness in this marriage. When income falls short or an old object will not sell, the couple use the phrase to stop blaming one another. It can also end a negotiation that still has practical parts to resolve. When Koroku asks about his tuition and future, Sosuke first says that it cannot be helped, then gathers the problem into the judgment that he has been wrong in everything from the beginning. A concrete question about where his brother will live and who will pay stops inside a total confession of fault. Acknowledging responsibility has not yet divided it into the next actions.[^mon]

Oyone also privately links three losses in pregnancy and childbirth into a chain of consequence. The first pregnancy ends in miscarriage at five months; the second child is born prematurely and lives for a week; the third is born at term but never breathes. She calls the three losses a "thread of causation," and a diviner tells her that she cannot raise a child because of an old wrong. Oyone keeps this account from Sosuke until the present conversation, when he dismisses the divination as absurd. What matters here is that Oyone once absorbed the losses into a history of punishment, while husband and wife did not always possess the same facts or explanation. Their intimacy resembles both two drops of oil pressed together by water and "honey on the tip of a whip": the sweetness is real, and so are the lash and the reading of suffering as atonement.[^mon]

Yasui later returns to Tokyo from Manchuria and nearly joins Sosuke at dinner in the Sakai home. That evening Sosuke tells Oyone only that he feels unwell. She asks twice. He retreats beneath the covers, at first wanting to call her over and face the news together. When she comes to his bedside, he instead says, "Bring me a cup of hot water." The narration immediately says that he has lost the courage to finish and lied his way out.

The lie spares Oyone the shock that night and turns their shared past into a crisis managed by Sosuke alone. He wants to see Yasui in secret and read in the man's appearance some assurance that he did not ruin him, but he does not want Yasui to look back. Sosuke later questions Sakai indirectly and learns only that Yasui has left Tokyo. He gains Yasui's whereabouts, not an answer. The novel never gives Yasui his own account of leaving school, illness, or travel, nor does it show whether he would choose to meet, refuse a meeting, or speak through an intermediary.

Sosuke even goes alone to a Zen temple in search of peace. Thinking of Yasui at night, he says explicitly that he cannot now concern himself with the cause of his pain and wants only to save his present self. A crisis in the relationship becomes a private illness: he hides its occasion from Oyone, reports himself ill to the office, and seeks personal salvation at the temple. The concrete alternative he imagines there is still to move house before Oyone learns that Yasui has returned. He never brings the event that prompted his departure into a marital negotiation, and the temple does not open the gate for him.[^mon-return]

Ten days later he returns home. The immediate danger recedes because Yasui has left, and the couple's life grows calm again. At the end, Oyone looks at the spring light and says that spring has finally come. Sosuke trims his nails and replies that winter will come again soon. Spring is present, and he has received a real interval of peace. The winter he foretells leaves open the possibility that the old matter may return with another message, person, or season. The novel does not go on to narrate that winter.[^mon-return]

*The Gate* presses Coda from the direction opposite to *Sorekara*. *Sorekara* contains answers but no shared life yet. *The Gate* has sustained a shared life for six years, while leaving Yasui's answer outside the text. Income, housework, care during illness, and years of love cannot speak for him. If his account were ever to enter the relation, it still could not erase the couple's six years of work. Both facts have to remain.

Its first point of comparison with Coda Normal is narrative order. Normal makes the engagement one year later, contact with the family, and Haruki's editorial work visible, but leaves outside the time shown how Setsuna hears the final decision and how Kazusa formally says goodbye. *The Gate* likewise fills its pages with the shared life while hollowing out the answers through which the relationship first took shape. Neither omission can be filled in as "it never happened." Nor can the visible later life provide the content of the absent answer.

Kazusa True makes departure much more public than Sosuke does. Setsuna opposes the breakup in her own voice; Haruki then breaks off the engagement, resigns, finishes the proofreading, and arranges the handover of his work. None of this is equivalent to Sosuke's concealment. Yet Haruki still filters the truth about Youko's illness, and relocation temporarily removes the immediate conditions for further face-to-face negotiation with Setsuna. The comparison is limited to one mechanism: when a relation cannot bear a third person's gaze in its present setting, geographical movement reduces who can meet and answer afterward.

## Restoring a Choice to Its History

After *Kokoro*, *Sorekara*, and *The Gate*, Coda's four endings can no longer be ordered only by whom Haruki chooses last. A relation approaching its ending demands at least eight questions: who first knows the facts, who initiates the next move, when the affected people answer, how family and work enter the negotiation, who puts the decision into practice, how the old arrangement ends, who handles the aftermath, and whether anything can be renegotiated later. These questions are not eight virtues, nor do they require everyone to speak in one room. Refusing contact, answering through an intermediary, changing a boundary, and choosing silence can all be answers when the affected person chooses them.

The eight moments often overlap. Disclosing what one hid may itself advance the relation. Care can handle an aftermath while beginning another negotiation. Ending a secret may open a public life. Separating the moments prevents the loudest act from swallowing the rest: resignation cannot answer for Setsuna, open conflict cannot supply housing, and six years of marriage cannot turn a report of Yasui's movements into his own account.

![Eight consecutive cards read Knowledge, Initiation, Answer, Negotiation, Implementation, Ending, Aftermath, and Renegotiation. Each card asks one concrete question, and the note below says to record both what has been completed and what remains for later where needed.](./white-album-2-who-lives-after-choice/choice-history-eight-stages.en.svg)

*The eight questions show when action changes hands. They do not score the four routes or claim to supply a complete model of relationships. Original analytical figure.*

Coda Normal begins with a bodily refusal. Haruki avoids crossing the boundary that night and tells Kazusa, "I love Setsuna." The shared non-Affair passage leading toward the ending then changes hands again. To protect Kazusa's performances, Haruki and Youko conceal the illness from her. Kazusa senses the secret, slips away from Haruki's watch while he sleeps, and goes directly to Youko. Their arrangement collapses when Youko falls in front of her.

This carries Haruki to the point at which he says that he must decide before seeing Kazusa again; Kazusa does not command him to choose in person. In the snow, he then thinks, "Kazusa cannot decide, so I will," and takes the final binary choice back into his own hands. The line does not prove that Kazusa generally lacks the capacity to decide. His inward choice of Setsuna still carries an afterthought that cannot fully say goodbye to Kazusa, and the script immediately jumps to the engagement, family contact, and editorial work one year later. A public life has formed, but the route does not show how Setsuna heard the final decision or how Kazusa completed the farewell.

In the Affair route, Kazusa first proposes a time-limited relation "only until the end of winter." Haruki accepts with a kiss and embrace, then turns it into several days of secret life together. The engagement, the families, and the workplace take no part in negotiating its beginning. Kazusa later says that she has deceived Haruki all along and that even the marriage promise was a game, then admits that this account of having lied is itself untrue. She still wanted to run away with him the night before and decides to send him back only after seeing him smile that morning. Ending the relation was not a plan she had controlled from the start. It is an action she acquires at the last moment.

Haruki afterward tells Setsuna, who has already sensed the affair, about the trip and their sexual relation. When she asks whether he thought of her during those days, he says that he had "forgotten Setsuna all along." His inward narration has already admitted that Setsuna's voice entered more than once. The false answer casts him as the most complete betrayer and lets him claim both the guilt and the sentence of lifelong punishment. Setsuna does not close the matter through forgiveness alone. She refuses to endorse his conclusion that he is unforgivable and must suffer alone, stops his monologue, and a year later asks him which would make her happy: his recovery alone or their recovery together.

Question XIV does not complete the choice on the spot in Kazusa True. Haruki first uses a fabricated farewell message hinting at suicide to force a conversation. Kazusa appears and exposes the lie before the hotel staff can open the door; the scene does not show Haruki rescuing her from an act of self-harm. During the conversation, Haruki calls his action an attempt to save "only the person most important" to him and proposes leaving Japan, yet he asks Kazusa to decide whether she can accept a man who will betray his partner, friends, and work.

Kazusa points out that he has handed her the cruelest part of the choice. She does not remain in the position of someone being saved. Five days later, she turns back from the airport, stops Haruki from going to Setsuna, and returns the named demand to him: "Betray Setsuna. Choose me." Only then does Haruki put the answer into a series of public acts: breaking off the engagement, resigning, finishing the proofreading, arranging the handover, and preparing to move. Of the four routes, he goes farthest here in making the decision public and concrete. What remains unresolved is not whether anyone acted, but that Setsuna hears only a filtered account of the illness and that departure temporarily removes the immediate conditions for further negotiation face to face.

Each of the three additional stories reopens one part of life after an ending. After the Affair route, a call between Kazusa and Setsuna reopens questions about Haruki's aftermath and each woman's responsibility. After Kazusa True, Youko restores the old house, while Haruki and Kazusa return to the school and hold a belated wedding there. Their limited visits to Japan resume, and Youko and Setsuna also come back into contact through the Ogiso family. After Setsuna True, husband and wife explain by phone why urgent work made each miss an appointment, revise the plan, and meet afterward.[^after] None of these openings brings all three people to one table to renegotiate the whole past, and none dictates a single future for its route.

The player's position also needs its own entry. A player who has read all four routes knows what each person said elsewhere. No character in the current route has that cross-route memory. Pressing a button makes one possible history current without giving the player's full knowledge to Setsuna, Kazusa, or Haruki. Readers can compare the routes, but they cannot use all the text they have seen to answer for someone who has not received the facts.

In *Kokoro*, the engagement precedes Shizu's answer. In *Sorekara*, the answers precede a shared life. *The Gate* fills six years with shared life while keeping Yasui's account outside its pages. Coda likewise distributes action among three people. Across different routes, Haruki refuses, discloses, breaks off an engagement, resigns, or makes a proposal. Kazusa proposes the time-limited relation and later decides that morning to end it. Setsuna stops Haruki's escape, forces him and Kazusa to speak to each other, and reconnects their music with work. Haruki's decisions always meet one of the women's approach, refusal, or repair. None of the four routes is made by the person who presses the last button alone.

Judging a choice requires following two lines past the end of the sentence. The first asks when the people affected hear what happened and whether they can refuse, ask for time, or change their answer. The second asks who provides income and housing, does the work and care, and handles the aftermath once the decision leaves the menu and becomes a life. The first keeps a fait accompli from passing as consent; the second keeps a beautiful answer from passing as responsibility already fulfilled.

The loudest decision completes only its opening move if it closes off replies and leaves other people to put the days afterward in order. Hesitation, jealousy, and refusal may be ugly, but they can preserve the chance to revise an arrangement. Wages, housing, care, and a proper handover carry no romance, yet they decide who ultimately bears the choice.
