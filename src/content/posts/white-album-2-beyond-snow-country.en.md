The body figures distinguish actions already taken from the outcome the characters wish the storm would produce, then place the public engagement, secret timetable, and later recovery on one timeline. As the train enters a tunnel, Kazusa falls asleep against Haruki's shoulder, still holding his left hand. Haruki does not wait for her to wake. He touches her with his right hand, asks whether she has read Yasunari Kawabata, and tells her to look out the window and count down with him.

Three, two, one. The train clears the tunnel. Sky, trees, and ground have all turned white.

This is how *Snow Country* enters the Affair route. The allusion is so beautiful that readers can easily follow Haruki's gaze and take the scene for a real border. Tokyo lies on one side of the tunnel and snow country on the other. Setsuna, work, the engagement, and all the words already spoken remain behind; the two lovers have reached a place of their own. Soon they wish for heavier snow, enough to stop roads and railways. Then they would not have decided to stay. They would be unable to leave.

Yet many actions precede the white landscape. Kazusa chose the destination, and Haruki did not object. They left Tokyo, changed to a local train, and Haruki woke her inside the tunnel, invoked Kawabata, and began the countdown. At the inn they still discuss taking the first train onward the next morning. The storm has not trapped them. They reach the place first, then wish that the weather would remove the return journey.

[The preceding essay](/en/posts/white-album-2-who-lives-after-choice/) asked who receives enough knowledge and time to answer a choice. This essay takes the question one step further. From whose engagement, rooms, and work does this world for two borrow its days, and how long does it defer Setsuna's chance to answer them face to face?

*Snow Country* supplies an entry through Shimamura's way of seeing. Komako and Yoko keep doing things, while he can turn their acts into scenery. Once another person's days become a view, her waiting and questions lose weight.

## A Face on the Glass, Days in the Ledger

*Snow Country* opens on Shimamura's second visit to the hot-spring town. Before the train crosses the prefectural border, an act of care is already under way. Yoko asks the stationmaster to look after her brother, then tends the sick Yukio throughout the journey: she covers him, gives him water, watches his breath and face. Shimamura observes from a short distance. After dark, the window becomes a mirror. Yoko's face floats on the glass and merges with the fields, lights, and dusk. A distant light passes through her pupil, producing the novel's famous opening image.[^yukiguni]

Care comes first; the vision follows. Yoko never poses for Shimamura and does not know that he is watching. Railway, station staff, carriage, and glass place them together. The window then turns tiring, physical work, work that also makes claims on other people, into beauty received by one spectator alone.

The same change becomes clearer with Komako. She can say that 199 days have passed since Shimamura's last visit. She has kept diaries for years and records characters and impressions from the novels she reads. Shimamura knows that she does not intend to publish them or show them to anyone, so he tells her that the effort is futile. Komako hears the insult. Yet her apparent futility moves him at once: a provincial woman labors over records, music, and reading with nowhere to send the result, and this makes the labor seem pure.

Futility is Shimamura's verdict, not Komako's. Her notes let her count the interval between visits. Practice from radio broadcasts and sheet music makes her able to work at banquets. She calculates principal, interest, living expenses, and the remaining years of debt, and limits her engagements to preserve her body. These acts may not open the life she wants, but they change the shape of each day. Once Shimamura calls them futile, he can keep the feeling and leave the questions behind: what happens after four years of debt, and how many times will he return next year?

One missed appointment and one unwritten letter put that difference into time. Shimamura fails to attend the bird-chasing festival as promised. Komako cuts short her care of her teacher so she can return to see him, then waits in vain. Later she refuses to write the kind of letter that would cause no trouble even if his wife saw it. Such a safe letter would have to remove the waiting, desire, and resentment, with each sentence adjusted for a reader in Tokyo. Shimamura wants affection that disturbs no life. Komako refuses to edit herself into its harmless version.

The novel gives Shimamura's wife almost no voice. Her one directly confirmed act is a reminder about mothproofing before he leaves home. Shimamura also imagines bringing his wife to snow country so that Komako might show her around and teach her to dance. Neither woman is consulted about this considerate arrangement. These few lines cannot support an invented account of the wife's housework, knowledge, or consent. Her silence still stands behind the blank letter: Shimamura can travel back and forth, while Komako must decide what his wife might do if she read what he receives.

Shimamura also supplies a love story for the three people in snow country. A masseuse reports a local rumor that Komako was once engaged to her teacher's son Yukio, and adds that she does not know the truth. Shimamura has already arranged Komako, Yukio, and Yoko into a tragic triangle. Komako denies both the engagement and the claim that she became a geisha to pay Yukio's medical expenses; Yoko later denies the engagement again. The rumor better suits Shimamura's snow country than the women's answers do. Engagement, sacrifice, and a sick man returning home make Komako's life look like a doomed romance. The triangle first takes shape in the spectator's reading and then conceals the participants' own history of action.

When the dying Yukio calls for Komako, she refuses to return and chooses to see Shimamura off. Yoko comes to the station to plead with her, and only then does Shimamura promise to take Komako back at once. After Komako calls him frank, he stops urging her. The novel never settles why Komako refuses. Self-protection and an inability to face the deathbed remain possibilities, not verdicts. At the fire, Komako is also the first to rush toward Yoko's fallen body. Her refusal and her later rescue are equally real; Shimamura does not keep the promise he made to Yoko at the station.

Shimamura knows that his dance research rests on books and photographs, and that he loves Western ballet without testing that love against a live performance. He can see his missed promises, evasions, and inability to remain. *Snow Country* is therefore far more complex than a story about an unfaithful man and a devoted geisha. Shimamura knows that “futility” is his own name for Komako's life, yet her records and music move him as futile all over again.

The quarrel in which he calls Komako a good woman has the contradictory speech familiar from *WHITE ALBUM 2*. Komako has just tended him while he is unwell, but cannot tell whether he is praising her or assessing an accommodating geisha. She presses him. Shimamura knows that she has heard another meaning and closes his eyes without answering. Komako later returns to apologize, invites him to bathe, and leaves first. On the way to the fire, “good woman” comes up again. The meaning Shimamura left unanswered has not faded with time.

The final fire tears that distance open. Yoko falls from the second floor of the silkworm warehouse. Shimamura first sees the line of the falling body, the flames, and the Milky Way across the night sky. The novel does not say why she fell or confirm that she died. Komako has already rushed to the scene and taken Yoko into her arms. One person is still watching a shape, one body has been injured, and another person is trying to save her. Kawabata does not abandon beauty here. His beautiful prose gives readers a share in Shimamura's position, then Komako's action forces them to notice what they have missed.

Komako and Yoko are more than two pictures arranged around Shimamura. They envy and detest each other, seek help, and make requests. Yoko asks Shimamura to treat Komako well; at the end Komako carries Yoko herself. The two women have a relation that does not depend on a man's explanation. Shimamura keeps absorbing that relation into his feeling until the fire gives him no time to rename it.

*Snow Country* places the first problem in Shimamura's eyes. He is moved in earnest, but gathers Komako's waiting, debts, and coming year into scenery. An explanation confined to a leisured spectator would still be too easy. A person skilled at getting things done can build the same kind of sealed world. *South of the Border, West of the Sun* gives us such a man.

## Who Gets to Call a Part of Life "Real"?

Shimamura stands aside and watches; Hajime knows how to arrange a life. He runs businesses, cares for his children, and can drive, book tickets, and find a doctor in a crisis. Before Shimamoto reappears, he meets Yukiko in the rain, falls in love, proposes three months later, and builds a family with her. They raise two daughters. Hajime likes his bars and has a talent for running them. He listens to employees, pays well, and knows how music, lighting, drink, and conversation can make a room people wish to visit again.

Shimamoto's return does not render this life counterfeit. Yukiko's love for Hajime is real, as is his love for his wife and daughters. The novel gives Hajime the same word, attraction, for more than one woman. The strength of desire cannot tell him which life is true and which is false.

During his early relationship with Izumi, Hajime thinks to himself that a relation accumulates through small, concrete facts rather than one promise. He later arranges a tryst with Izumi's cousin and tells Izumi more than a hundred lies. When she discovers the affair, he takes all the blame and insists that he would do the same thing if he lived his life again. That verdict compresses a sequence of alterable arrangements into “this is the person I am,” as though his later actions had ceased to be choices. At the end of the novel he again tells Yukiko that he might repeat his betrayal. She does not finish the judgment for him. She asks them to decide from this point whether they can continue.

Many people build the bars. Yukiko's father offers Hajime a unit in a new Aoyama building owned by his company, charges market rent and a deposit, lends startup funds as needed, and pays for the interior while bringing in the designer and contractors. Bartenders, musicians, cooks, and other staff keep the doors open each night. Hajime describes a bar as a garden suspended in the air, a carefully made place where paying customers can feel more intimate than they do outside. He understands how money and labor produce the sensation. Beside Shimamoto, however, he imagines a self untouched by those same things.

Shimamoto acts for herself. She walks into the bar and sets limits on what Hajime may ask. She proposes finding a river that flows to the sea so she can scatter the ashes of her infant daughter. She knows that Hajime must lie to his wife and initiates the journey anyway. At Hakone she demands all or nothing, refusing a permanently hidden place as his mistress. By morning she and the record are gone; Hajime can only infer that she seems to have taken it. His promise to abandon everything now has nowhere to be carried out.

Hajime nevertheless knows almost nothing about her adult life. He does not know where she lives, whether she is married, where her money comes from, or what happened to her in the intervening years. Shimamoto has reasons for refusing to tell him; her silence is not by itself a lie. Hajime can only guess why she leaves, and the result does not make her a noble rescuer of his family. The less he knows, the more readily he explains her according to his own need. Whenever she appears, the self divided among marriage, children, and business seems whole again.

Their journey to the river has already staged an escape through snow. Hajime invents a different itinerary for his wife and arranges a flight and rental car. When snow threatens the return flight, Shimamoto asks what he will do if he cannot get back and tells him not to worry about her. Hajime silently hopes the flight will be canceled; Shimamoto later says that she had wished for the same thing. They both want chance to cancel the return, but the flight takes off. At Hakone, Hajime finally says he will abandon his wife, daughters, and business. His words are clear, but they have not become a shared life. He has told his family nothing, the marriage remains intact, and Shimamoto's own relations are still unknown. Housing, income, and the children never enter the conversation.

The novel even places this exceptional space beside Hajime's business. His father-in-law asks him to lend his name to a shell company while also teaching him the rules of an affair that will not destroy a family: choose the partner carefully, return before two in the morning, do not provide her with a home, and do not use friends as an excuse. Both practices share one kind of ingenuity. Public rules need not change if the breach remains within limits the visible order can absorb. Hajime detests the illegality in the business arrangement and sincerely treats his desire as something exempt from shared rules. The two feelings coexist.

After Shimamoto disappears, Yukiko first asks whether he wants to separate. Hajime says that he does not know. She tells him to think and waits for two weeks. When she asks again, she refuses to let "I love you" replace yes or no. Nor will she allow "I am unworthy" or "I may do it again" to decide the ending in advance. Her most consequential sentence is that he has asked her nothing. Hajime repeatedly assumes that his wife knows nothing. In fact, he has never asked what she noticed, how she lived through those days, or whether she still wants the marriage.[^south]

Yukiko does not become an already forgiving wife at the end. She says that she too has lost dreams and may hurt him in the future. She agrees only that they will try to begin again. The novel stops in a morning when Hajime still cannot stand and wake his daughters. The hand that comes to rest on his back is not identified. The two have agreed to try again; the novel has not written how they will live the next day. The “small, concrete facts” that Hajime once recognized inwardly and later betrayed return as the test of whether they can make a life together.

The novel remains inside Hajime's retrospective first-person account. Its form gives his self-scrutiny unusual force and makes it harder for Shimamoto to escape his interpretation. A hundred thousand yen, a cigarette butt, glasses, photographs, and the record briefly work as exhibits proving that she was no apparition. The record is found missing the next morning; the money is not found missing until the following week. The chain of objects breaks in stages. The novel does not declare Shimamoto a ghost. It removes Hajime's and the reader's confidence that objects can settle the matter. We have to remain between what Shimamoto did and the motives Hajime supplies for her. Earnest self-scrutiny can still cover another person's account.

*South of the Border, West of the Sun* carries the problem in *Snow Country* one step further. Shimamura evades answers through watching, while Hajime can arrange each practical detail. Once he names the self beside Shimamoto as complete, the life maintained by his wife, daughters, employees, and father-in-law recedes into the background. Those days remain; his story has assigned them a lower rank.

*The Sting of Death* carries the hope that full disclosure will restore a life to its limit. The husband keeps hiding facts, shifting meanings, and delaying admissions, so the wife needs more and more truth. The door closes tighter during the pursuit.

## Truth Can Lock the Door Too

Toshio's diary has exposed a long affair, and Miho demands that he stop lying. He promises, then keeps shifting the meanings of words, omitting facts, and correcting himself late. Miho turns ten years of marriage into an account: what would cooking, laundry, childcare, and years of waiting alone be worth at a maid's wage? From the beginning, truth is bound to housework and stolen time.[^toge]

Toshio continues to hide photographs, letters, and records of his mistress's body. He calls these things the last support left to him in a transparent life. Miho already knows that hidden objects exist, so she is waiting for more than an inventory. She wants to see whether Toshio will speak without being prompted. An answer that comes late, or in words she finds imprecise, becomes more evidence that ordinary answers cannot be trusted. His long deception gives her questioning ample cause. That same cause makes it harder for the two to speak without an interrogation.

When questioning closes in, Toshio feigns madness and pushes the conflict toward self-harm or a plan to die together. Miho and the children then have to set the original question aside to chase him, take dangerous objects away, or keep him alive. His suffering and drive toward death are not wholly performances. Each crisis still changes his place in the scene, from the person being questioned to the person who must be rescued, and interrupts the earlier demand to account for what he did.

At the same time, Toshio does care for Miho and the children and handles moves, medical care, hospitalization, and negotiations with relatives. He savors the freedom of being alone outside the home and repeatedly returns. Care and evasion are acts of the same man. Toshio records his own disgrace, but he still orders the causes and assigns motives to Miho and the mistress.

As the facts accumulate, each begins to perform the same task: proving whether the other person will choose loyalty without prompting. One spouse knows an answer but has to wait for the other to volunteer it; a slight delay turns the fact into the next test. Truth retains its value, but the pair can no longer use it to handle the matter in front of them together.

The novel stages a finger-cutting in which no blood is drawn. Miho does not actually cut Toshio, but bandages his finger and tells the children that it has been cut and that this is no playacting. Toshio does not expose the lie and soon feels real pain. A few pages later, Miho has abdominal pain but still lights the fire, cooks, makes the bed, and prepares a hot-water bottle. Punishment, pain, deception, and care occupy the same night. The couple care for their children while pulling them into their ritual of punishment.

The two children speak and act. The son tells a classmate's mother what is happening at home because he thinks telling someone is better; his father declares the matter a secret for the four family members. Later the child says it plainly: he does not want this family life and wants a happy home. He does not decide which parent loves whom. He rejects the life they have made together.

When the mistress enters the house, all three adults finally occupy one room, but no disclosure follows. Miho stops the woman from leaving and forces her husband to answer whom he loves. She then orders him to block the exit and slap the mistress. Toshio complies, blocks the way, and prevents her from leaving. The mistress points out that Toshio created their shared predicament. A neighbor calls the police, and officers entering the house bring the violence to an end.

That night, Toshio calls the confrontation a kind of “shock therapy.” The next morning, he still hopes that the shock will stop Miho's attacks. The name remains his. Shimao follows it with compensation, the landlord terminating the lease, a move, and relatives caring for the children. What Toshio calls therapy means a call to the police, payment, lost housing, and another household taking care of their children. The mistress manages only a few sentences and needs the police in order to leave.

Medicine supplies no single answer either. Doctors disagree. Miho once fantasizes about forgetting through electric shock, then refuses when faced with the procedure; treatment later proceeds over her resistance. A hospital can separate immediate danger and provide medicine and beds. It can also reduce a relationship to the question of who should restrain whom. Near the end, Miho even suggests that Toshio pretend to resume the affair, recover every remaining letter, and then flee. A woman demanding complete truth now plans a new deception because it promises to complete the evidence.

Miho is finally admitted to a locked ward, where one bed lacks bedding. Toshio remains free to leave and collect it. Outside the hospital, he briefly feels released and sees his relatives and children living calmly without him. He then chooses to return with the bedding and stay beside her. The locked door directly confines Miho; Toshio can still come and go. The letters remain unrecovered, the children and work remain outside, and the couple have no answer for how they will live together. Bringing the bedding back is a small, real act of living. It gets them through the night.

Shimao's method refuses to make one quarrel the novel's only climax. Meals, moves, doctors, prayer, attacks, reconciliation, and another meal recur across the chapters. A different house, doctor, or vow gives the same problems a new site. The first-person narration places a violent episode beside the hour of dinner, who will fetch the children, or where the bedding goes. The atmosphere suffocates because the pot remains on the stove after each outburst and someone must still cook the next meal.

Relatives, police, landlords, and hospitals can stop one violent scene, arrange housing, and take the children. A locked ward may also move the problem into a tighter container. Daily life closes another door when it permits neither different answers nor bodily limits or exit.

The two situations are far apart. The comparison concerns only whether disclosure gives three people room to act again.

The three novels arrange the relations between women in three different shapes. In *Snow Country*, Komako and Yoko already ask, envy, and entrust things to each other, but Shimamura divides them into complementary scenery. In *South of the Border, West of the Sun*, Yukiko and Shimamoto never meet, while Hajime controls the information on both sides. *The Sting of Death* puts all three people in one room, where blocked exits and violence suppress the answer. A relation between the women, a face-to-face meeting, or a violent clash does not by itself give anyone a safe chance to ask, refuse, or leave.

## Living a Few Days as One Winter

Long before Coda, Closing Chapter puts “Go to Kazusa's concert” on the menu twice. The player can see the choice but cannot execute it. Fumiaki Maruto later confirmed that the option was deliberately placed on screen even though it can never be selected and its flag never becomes available.[^grey]

Haruki admits that claiming no interest would be a lie. Yet the preceding three years have become a life involving Setsuna, friends, coworkers, and work. The player can see another path. Haruki cannot press a button and remove those years from himself.

A visual novel also lets a player remember other buttons and endings, so the player knows more than anyone within a single route. The women in the Affair route can act only on what they hear, see, and do within this history. The player's cross-route knowledge does not belong to them.

Coda brings Kazusa into Haruki's working life as an interview subject. His magazine and Youko keep him responsible for the interview and concert coverage, while injury and performance schedules give continued contact a public name. Youko rents separate accommodation in Japan to avoid the press and signs the lease on the neighboring room. Love later turns that work arrangement into a sealed room.

Kazusa first gives the relation a limit: it will last until she leaves Japan or winter ends. She calls it one season of "false love" and leaves "true love" to Setsuna. Haruki kisses her while she is still hesitating and accepts the proposal in the street. Kazusa names the arrangement; Haruki makes it real. He then does most of the scheduling that decides when they meet and when he returns home.

“False love” does not rank the strength of their feelings. Kazusa uses the phrase for two opposed acts. She admits that she wants Haruki and promises that the desire will not rewrite his future with Setsuna. Once Haruki accepts her proposal, “until the end of winter” becomes a set of days that two people have to live.

He creates four numeric codes. Zero means that he will return home, one that he will be late, two that he will not return that night, and four that Kazusa must vacate the neighboring room so Setsuna can stay over. Four is neither a room number nor an address. With Setsuna beside him, Haruki disguises a message about "four o'clock" with a fictitious company and surname. Setsuna hears a business arrangement. Kazusa receives a timetable for access at night.[^winter]

They betray Setsuna while keeping her daily life running as usual. Calls continue and weekends remain together; even the couple's physical intimacy cannot cease without warning. Setsuna cooks every week, saves for their future, and prepares the visit to her parents. Haruki does love her. The more closely the engagement follows its usual rhythm, the less visible the secret becomes.

The codes cannot place the women in wholly separate times. Kazusa later admits that she never once obeyed code four. For weeks she stayed next door and listened while Haruki and Setsuna were together. Setsuna thinks the adjacent room is empty, but Tomo has grown suspicious; later, while Takeya is present, Tomo presses the neighboring doorbell. Across the same wall, Setsuna believes that she is alone with her fiancé, Kazusa trains herself to be present without making a sound, and Haruki trusts a code sheet to keep their meetings apart.

No arrangement removes their divided motives. Kazusa's "false love" tries to preserve Haruki and Setsuna while claiming her final span of time. She demands departure but never leaves the adjacent room. Haruki loves Setsuna and desires Kazusa; he hopes that separate schedules will keep the two loves from negating each other at the same table. Setsuna senses gaps too. She has checked the next room and heard spaces inside Haruki's explanations. Haruki and Tomo later judge that she may already suspect the betrayal and be pressing that suspicion back into "not knowing." Each person knows something and withholds something in order to protect a life she or he cannot yet bear to lose.

Valentine's Day illuminates the labor at the other end of this timetable. Kazusa learns about the engagement, congratulates Haruki, and urges him to return to Setsuna. Haruki is the one who turns back. He invents an interview and misses Setsuna's birthday and the visit to her parents. Setsuna and her mother still wait for him to come home for dinner. The script calls his excuse the worst kind of lie. It hides an affair and a meal prepared for the original hour.

Haruki later claims to have influenza and abandons editorial work he was meant to finish. Coworkers who trust him rearrange the job, and Kazusa stops practicing. The pair obtain several continuous days in what they call the "real world" because colleagues take Haruki's shift and Kazusa suspends her work.

Mutual desire cannot lend later consent backward to earlier silence and refusal. In one intimate encounter, Haruki says that if Kazusa remains silent, he will take that as consent. She is still wavering, and he does not stop. In another, Kazusa explicitly says no and tells him to stop several times, but Haruki again does not stop at once. Only later does she consent and show active desire of her own. Each encounter needs an answer at the time.

On public streets Kazusa wears glasses, changes her hair, and uses the alias Kazumi Higashino to avoid the press and people she knows. Haruki removes the disguise and declares that he is looking for Kazusa Touma. The two hide from public sight and also want someone to see that they are in love.

Setsuna's trace already exists in the inn. She booked it five years earlier, when all three stayed there together. The snowy view also recalls the night when the three performed *WHITE ALBUM*. The harder the Affair route tries to delete the third person, the more closely it follows her old reservation and their shared memory.

When an employee almost recognizes Kazusa, Haruki says that she only resembles the pianist and improvises that the two will marry soon. After they receive a blessing from someone who does not know the facts, Kazusa asks him to repeat the words. Haruki proposes. Kazusa imagines becoming “Kazusa Kitahara,” and the two treat the sex that night as a ceremony witnessed only by each other.

The private engagement carries their wish and exposes the problems it cannot carry. Haruki and Kazusa turn a wish that had long remained an “if” into one they acknowledge to each other in the present, giving their suppressed relation a continuous span of time. The work also lists what is absent: dress, formal clothes, rings, and an informed witness, as well as housing, income, work, and family after winter. The inn employee truly blesses them without knowing what the blessing witnesses.

*The proposed winter depends on the public engagement, real work, and a secret room. After the secret ends, more people have to take ordinary life back up. Original analytical figure.*

They call the limit one winter, but the secret life lasts less than a month. Haruki has not seen Setsuna for about two weeks; the uninterrupted cohabitation, withdrawal from work, and journey occupy the final several days, and the trip ends on its third morning. Calling it one winter makes the relation sound as though it will end when the season does. Yet people make every step: Kazusa proposes the deadline, Haruki designs the codes and excuses, Youko provides the room, the editorial staff inherits his work, and Setsuna and her family continue the engagement.

The lies do not erase either love. They distribute those loves across audiences and times. Haruki talks about work to Setsuna and sends codes to Kazusa. He lies to the inn employee that the two will marry, then turns the lie into a private engagement with Kazusa. Each sentence makes sense behind one closed door and omits its decisive condition in the next room. Setsuna continues to love him, cook, wait, and plan the wedding with her family. She does not know how the timetable works and therefore cannot stop it.

Interviews, wages, the neighboring room rented by Youko, and Setsuna's continuing engagement give the pair several uninterrupted days. Setsuna waits outside the door. Inside, Haruki and Kazusa can hear days they arranged together as fate.

## Who Brings This Winter to an End?

Kazusa first says that she intended to end the relation from the beginning. She soon admits that this was a lie about lying. Only on the morning of the return journey, when she sees Haruki smile on waking, does she decide to leave. The woman who wished to flee farther north yesterday prepares to give him back to Setsuna today. Both impulses belong to her; choosing one sentence as her true heart would erase the other.

The train to Tokyo passes through the tunnel again, leaving the white country behind the glass. They do not continue to Sapporo or abroad. The train they actually take returns to Tokyo.

Before the concert, Kazusa leaves the only vacant front-row seat beside Haruki for Setsuna. Her performance is a farewell to him and a professionally unassailable cover for the days of cohabitation. If the audience sees only a pianist completing her concert, the days behind the door can pass as though nothing happened. After the third piece ends and the interval begins, Haruki tells Setsuna about living together, running away, having sex, and still loving Kazusa.[^ending]

The separation of hands is colder than an explicit verdict. Kazusa asks Haruki to let go. He says that he is applying no force: she need only withdraw her hand and the two will separate. Kazusa admits that she wants him to fling her away and reject her, so that he will bear the final responsibility too. Haruki refuses to perform the act for her. The narration says only that their hands finally part. It does not identify who moves first.

That omission keeps the Affair route from becoming a simple story in which Kazusa chooses everything or Haruki finally assumes full responsibility. Kazusa sets the limit and Haruki turns it into a secret life; Kazusa decides to leave in the morning, and Haruki later tells Setsuna what happened.

From Setsuna's reaction, Haruki concludes that she had already detected the betrayal and meant to pretend otherwise. Detection differs from hearing the whole timetable. Only after Haruki tells her face to face about the cohabitation, the journey, the sexual relation, and his continuing love for Kazusa can Setsuna question each fact. She does not accept the sentence of self-punishment he has prepared, nor let him determine the whole exchange alone. The secret schedule stops here. Their former life does not return.

After the long telephone call, Setsuna begins eating again and decides for herself to see Haruki. Haruki has also begun to walk outside almost every day, leaving his whereabouts for the woman who still has a key. He does not assume that she will return. He leaves her a way back into his daily life if she does. Medicine and treatment help him, as do his friends' understanding and time; he still has to walk, get sunlight, and leave the house. Without Setsuna he would have kept deteriorating, but Setsuna alone could not restore him.[^after]

When Haruki returns to work, he says that there are still many things he cannot do. At a gathering, his colleagues agree not to press alcohol on him and to let him leave whenever he wants. Family, friends, clinicians, and work hold up a limited recovery and let him stop before his symptoms worsen.

Yet when Setsuna asks whether he cares about Kazusa's concert, whether he wants to see her, and then tries to mention the previous night's call, Haruki closes the subject. He says that he has only begun to recover and cannot bear that level of mental strain. Recovery now has supports beyond the couple, while Kazusa remains a temporarily closed subject.

These small permissions to share a burden, refuse, and reschedule give “returning to the human world” its shape. Friends can help, Setsuna can say that she cannot continue, and Haruki must walk, see clinicians, and resume his job.

Kazusa True tests that point. Haruki ends the engagement in public, resigns, hands over his work, and moves to Vienna. This departure is far more complete than the secret winter. The additional story then shows how distance becomes daily life. Kazusa performs, Haruki acts as her manager, Youko receives treatment, and the agency and home require maintenance. On this return to Japan, staff from the local office meet them; Youko arranges the restoration of the old house and uses her connections to contact the school. Europe also has relatives, work, and doors that must be opened each day. None of this cancels the decision in Kazusa True or supplies the joint renegotiation with Setsuna that has not occurred.

Secrecy cuts one life into two versions, but Setsuna's dinner table, the editorial schedule, Youko's room, and the lovers' train remain connected throughout. The “human world” in this essay is the shared world that supports both versions at once. It is not identical with the original partner, conventional family life, or remaining in Japan. Returning to it means that when people outside the room still support a life with their labor, their questions, refusals, and changes of mind become effective again.

The fire in *Snow Country* leaves Shimamura's answer unmade. The morning in *South of the Border, West of the Sun* leaves a spoken choice that has not become a life. The locked ward in *The Sting of Death* reduces tomorrow to a set of bedding. The Affair route runs two versions of the same world at once. Kazusa moves the relation toward its end; Haruki discloses it; Setsuna answers and carries much of the care; clinicians, family, friends, and coworkers bring life into the next day. No beautiful act pays the whole account by itself.

The road remains beneath the snow. When the snow melts, the ground does not repair itself. Rails, footprints, and forks in the road reappear, enough for people to see where those borrowed days came from and who is still waiting in the same place for an answer.

_Next: one year later, *To You, My Sworn Enemy* has Kazusa and Setsuna call each other at almost the same moment. A telephone lets them interrupt, reproach, and refuse in real time, but it still has only two endpoints, and Haruki never learns what they say. [Essay eight, “After They Meet, the Three Still Have Unfinished Business”](/en/posts/white-album-2-after-they-meet/), asks how direct speech can stop deciding on behalf of the absent third person._
