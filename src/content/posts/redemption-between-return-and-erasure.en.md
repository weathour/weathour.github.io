> **Spoiler warning:** This essay discusses the complete plots of *A World Without Thieves* and the *Infernal Affairs* trilogy. It follows the Hong Kong ending of the first *Infernal Affairs* and its continuation in the third film. The final visit discussed here appears in the extended cut of *Infernal Affairs III*.

## Why a Thief Will Not Disappoint Him

Sha Gen boards the train carrying sixty thousand yuan. He has worked on the plateau for five years to save it, yet he is not afraid to let people know. His workmates urge him to transfer the money. He refuses, even announcing at the station that he is carrying it. He trusts people. Wolves have never harmed him; surely people cannot be worse than wolves.[^thieves]

He is just the sort of person a thief hopes to meet.

But Wang Li has already begun to refuse. She is pregnant, prays before the Buddha, and wants to do some good for her child. What if the consequences of her own wrongdoing fall on the baby? The child is not yet born; Sha Gen is here before her. She wants to take a step away from the trade she has lived by, and that step begins with his sixty thousand yuan. She takes him as a younger brother, protects him, and forbids Wang Bo to steal from him.

Wang Bo is not ready to step back with her. He sees an easy victim. Later, he even faults Sha Gen for not understanding the world: people have to learn about its dangers; surely someone cannot protect him forever. From a thief, this sounds particularly knowing. He offers testimony about the world while preparing to make that testimony come true with his own hands.

Their disagreement involves attachments that a simple story of innocence reforming thieves would miss. In an interview after the film's release, Feng Xiaogang said that Sha Gen's goodness alone had not convinced even him. Pregnancy and the desire to accumulate merit were written into Wang Li's change; love and the child entered Wang Bo's choices.[^feng] She wants to stop; he wants to carry on. The life she no longer wants is the life that has supported them both. Sha Gen gives their unresolved dispute something they must decide now.

Later, Wang Bo stands in the way of other thieves. Of course he knows there are thieves in the world. He is one himself. Yet this time he cannot bear to let Sha Gen find out.

Who believes for whom now runs in two directions. Wang Bo and Wang Li protect Sha Gen's money and his reasons for continuing to trust people. Sha Gen, in turn, preserves something these two worldly people are reluctant to admit they still believe. He does not know what they are, but treats them as good people. They can keep taking advantage of that trust. They can also refuse to take advantage of it again.

Žižek describes a kind of belief sustained through another person's belief: I may no longer be able to believe directly, yet it matters to me that someone still does. I may even change what I do because I cannot bear to disappoint that person.[^belief] Sha Gen comes to matter in this way to Wang Bo and Wang Li. Protecting him does him a good turn and gives them a chance to accomplish one.

This belief does not instantly make them different people. Wang Bo still has his skills as a thief, his competitiveness, and his own attachments. Change begins with a different use of the same hands.

## Sha Gen Sleeps; the Audience Stays Awake

Those hands must eventually return the bag, but the film first puts them through an ordeal. Money is stolen and restored. A plainclothes officer substitutes paper money for the dead and watches where the real cash goes. After the police round up the thieves, Wang Bo, Wang Li, and Uncle Li escape in turn. Uncle Li takes the real money again; Wang Bo goes after it. Some want the money, some want to win, and some want to catch thieves. Their conflicting purposes turn around a single bag before Sha Gen can take his savings home.[^thieves]

The money really does come back. Concealment cannot erase that fact. Five years' wages may be merely a number to someone accustomed to stealing money. To Sha Gen, they are the life he will have when he returns home. Wang Bo and Wang Li allow him to bring five years of work back with him.

Wang Bo's skill also lends the rescue its glamour. An eggshell removed without breaking the membrane, a piece of ice snatched away, a contest over the bag on the roof of the train: we watch skill and then judge who is better. Wang Bo changes what he uses his craft for on this occasion, but the craft retains its brilliance. We wait for him to win, partly because he is still the accomplished man we have enjoyed watching. His appeal accompanies him into the rescue.

Sha Gen sees far less than we do. When the lost money reappears, Wang Li explains it as a magic trick. He sleeps through the final struggle. Wounded, Wang Bo returns the bag to his chest. A message remains unsent on Wang Bo's phone; the officer arrives and presses send for him: “Sha Gen is taken care of. Wait for me.”[^thieves] Wang Li will receive those words. Sha Gen will receive only his money.

To wake him now and point out the theft, the struggle, and the blood might seem cruel. Knowing what the rescue has cost, we too can feel reluctant: let him sleep. People who know the truth may wish to preserve the dream. This is where the film's tenderness takes hold. Any criticism of it has to pass through that tenderness.

In the preface to *Call to Arms*, Lu Xun imagines an iron house. If it cannot be broken open, waking its sleeping inhabitants will only make them suffer as they die. Would the person who wakes them be doing right by them? Jin Xinyi answers that once some people are awake, one cannot rule out the hope of destroying the house. Lu Xun accepts this possibility: “hope belongs to the future.”[^luxun] Whoever wakes others must consider the consequences; whoever declares the situation hopeless cannot decide the future for them either.

Sha Gen is not inside a house that is certainly impossible to break open. He need not lose sixty thousand yuan in order to learn that thieves exist. He could keep the money and also know that someone tried to steal it and someone else protected him. Knowing this would give him a chance to thank his protectors, take care on his next journey, and say what he thinks. Returning the bag to his chest does not require making its journey disappear.

Earlier, Wang Bo speaks too harshly, as though Sha Gen must be hurt before he can grow wiser. Later, he will not let him know, as though knowledge can only hurt. Between these extremes, there is room for another way of treating him. The film leaves both the danger and the rescue with those who know, allowing Sha Gen to keep believing. We can cherish the kindness of this journey and still wonder whom he will meet on the next.

## I Want to Become Good; You Still Have to Come Down with Me

Lau Kin-ming also hopes someone will believe him. On the rooftop, his wish is clear: “I had no choice before. Now I want to be a good person.” Chan Wing-yan answers: “Fine. Tell the judge. See if he'll let you be good.”[^rooftop]

It is easy to hear one man asking to change and another refusing him a chance. But what each has done before reaching this rooftop determines how much those words ask of the other.

Hon Sam planted Lau inside the police. Lau really did attend the academy and become an officer; he really does have a position, access, and colleagues. He passes police information to Sam, then uses Sam's intelligence to advance his police career. His public record grows through secret dealings, and a stronger record gives him access to more secrets. His colleagues can follow his orders without knowing which side he privately thinks he belongs to.

Chan, by contrast, becomes less and less able to live openly as a policeman because he is carrying out police work. He moves with the gang, acquires a history that looks criminal, and can account for himself to very few people. When Superintendent Wong dies, he loses a crucial witness. After Lau kills Sam, Chan briefly thinks he can finally return. In Lau's office, however, he recognizes his own handwriting on Sam's dossier, realizes that this officer is the mole, and leaves. Lau then deletes the undercover file from the police database, keeping the material that can restore Chan's identity on his own computer.[^infernal]

What Chan has done does not disappear. What disappears is the proof that others could recover through the ordinary system. Ten years can be real, while the police temporarily have no record of the man. Lau's secret dealings are real too, yet he can still carry a gun, investigate people, and give orders. Both men have secrets. Those secrets give them very different things.

When Chan says he is a cop on the rooftop, he asks others to acknowledge what he has already done. When Lau says he wants to become good, he asks them to believe in what he will do. Between those statements lie the ten years Chan has given and the position Lau has gained. If Chan lets him go in a moment of mutual understanding, people who never stood on this rooftop or heard this exchange will also be made to vouch for him.

Telling Lau to speak to the judge leaves him his wish and leaves the judgment to others. He can want to change and begin to change, but he has to leave the desk from which he can delete another man's file. Lau hears this as a demand that he die. Wanting a new life remains a long way from being willing to lose the advantages of the old one.

Watching the films together now, Andy Lau's face makes that distance feel intimate. As Wang Bo returned the money bag, we were willing to believe he could turn back. On the rooftop, the same familiar face makes it hard to dismiss Lau Kin-ming's wish. We can hope he changes. Chan cannot promise on everyone's behalf that our hope has already made him good.

Then Billy kills Chan in the elevator and tells Lau that he too is Sam's mole. Lau kills Billy. Chan is dead, and another person who knows Lau's secret is dead. The elevator doors open. Lau holds up his police ID and identifies himself as a cop.[^infernal] Words Chan has just used to establish who he is now come from Lau. The people meeting him must judge the dead and the living before them.

## The Same Voice Reaches Different People

When the two men first meet, things are far less tense. Chan is minding a friend's audio shop; Lau comes to buy equipment. They sit listening to Tsai Chin's *Forgotten Times*, discussing tube amplifiers and sound. Neither recognizes the man he will later be trying to find. A shared pleasure gives them a brief interval in which they can treat each other like ordinary people.

The rest of the trilogy makes this meeting harder to watch so simply. The second film places the song alongside the history of the young Lau and Hon Sam's wife Mary. The third adds a moment in which Chan collects goods for Sam from the audio shop. Returning to the first film after watching these scenes, we bring more secrets into the quiet shop. The men are still listening to the song. We can no longer hear only the song.[^sound]

The sampler CD brings the same equipment into another relationship. We need to return to the period before the rooftop: after Chan discovers who Lau is and Lau deletes the file, Chan has a disc containing Lau's conversations with Sam delivered to Lau's home under the guise of a sound test from the shop. Lau's wife Mary hears it first. When he returns, she tells him someone has adjusted the equipment and left a disc. They still exchange everyday words about breakfast, but she has already heard another voice.[^recording]

Earlier, she has worried about whether a character in her novel is good or bad. Now she again says she cannot finish it; she does not know what kind of person he is. The question suspended in her fiction has entered her life. Lau sits down and plays the disc. His conversation with Sam comes through the speakers at home. Equipment that once allowed two men to listen quietly to a song now tells a wife something about the man she lives with.

The recording does not decide Mary's entire future. It allows her to reconsider Lau from what she has heard for herself. Her eventual departure is also her answer. Treating her merely as one additional listener would miss the decision she can now make within this marriage.

Lau does respond. Before meeting Chan, he leaves Mary a message saying he has chosen to become good and will restore Chan's identity. The file is on his computer; the password is her birthday.[^recording] This hesitation and effort matter. He asks Chan for a chance, but also makes his wife a promise that can be carried out. After the message, however, come the rooftop and Chan's refusal to release him. A wish has to continue becoming action precisely when other people do not answer as one wishes.

The first film brings the recording into the home. The third brings another set of copies into police headquarters. Lau takes a tape from Yeung Kam-wing's safe, believing he has evidence that will expose Yeung, and plays it before his colleagues. What comes out of the speakers is his own voice with Sam's. The officers who followed Lau to investigate another man demand Lau's arrest. By now, he identifies himself as Chan Wing-yan and Yeung as Lau Kin-ming, and shoots Yeung.[^sound] The man who wants to be a good policeman tries to complete what he believes is a righteous arrest while mistaking both himself and the person before him.

The voice has existed for some time. The people who hear it can still multiply. Between accomplices, the secret is a means of holding one another in check. In a wife's hearing, it changes a relationship. Before colleagues, it undermines the right to give orders. Each time, someone must answer what they hear before the recording can escape the control of the people who held it.

Chan has also entrusted his words to Dr. Lee Sum-yee. When he first tells her he is a policeman, she takes it as a joke. Later, wanted by the police, he still comes to her. She invites him upstairs and asks about it again. When she is willing to listen, she does not have the file that could prove his story. Six months after his death, she finds the undercover record among Principal Yip's effects, enabling the police to restore Chan's identity.[^recording]

While Chan lives, she welcomes him. After he dies, she keeps looking. The first gives him somewhere to speak and rest; the second secures public acknowledgment of his name. At last, a file can establish that he was a policeman all along. But he will never again come through Dr. Lee's door.

## Out in the Underworld, Whom Do You Pay Back?

*Infernal Affairs II* repeatedly warns: “Out in the underworld, expect to pay.”[^repayment]

The word *expect* speaks with experience. Before taking this road, you should know that your turn will come. A violent death or prison: when it arrives, you must accept it. Such knowledge does not stop the next killing. Ngai Wing-hau inherits his father's business and grudges; Hon Sam takes Wing-hau's place. One man falls and another carries on, inheriting even the tone of someone who knows how it will end.

But *pay* leaves something unclear. If retribution falls on you, what does someone else receive? Knowing that you will eventually pay a price, undergoing punishment, and returning what you owe sound like ways of settling an account. They are different acts. As the warning repeatedly comes true, it is easy to feel that fate has run its course and the debt is discharged. Yet what was taken does not necessarily come back with the punishment.

Wang Bo finally returns the sixty thousand yuan that belonged to Sha Gen. The return is real and deserves to be remembered. It does not restore the property of people he previously cheated or robbed. If saving Sha Gen is made to stand for answering everyone, people whose names the story has not even preserved are asked to step aside once more for our emotion.

His death makes the return especially difficult to question. The film brings old skills, a new use, and a fatal wound together: a thief saves someone with the thing he does best and dies doing it. The image becomes complete. There will be no next reach into someone else's pocket. We see him turn back at this moment; there is no time to see him turn this change into an ordinary way of living.

Yet Wang Bo has not resolved from the outset to die. Feng Xiaogang explained that he could have backed down and had enough money to compensate Sha Gen himself. The script had to force him, step by step, into the final fight with Uncle Li. Both men also had the pride of professionals unwilling to concede defeat.[^feng] He saves someone and competes for supremacy. He pays with his life, but the film does not require us to imagine that he planned to pay with his life. Keeping these elements visible prevents a good act from disappearing beneath too tidy a martyrdom.

Lau Kin-ming's injuries can likewise bring us to an apparent endpoint: at last he has suffered enough. Yet Chan's ten years have not returned. Superintendent Wong has not returned. Nor has Yeung, whom Lau later shoots. What a body has endured and what remains to be faced after harming others cannot be settled by a single dreadful image.

Remembering Wang Li's wish to accumulate merit makes the question harder. She fears that her wrongdoing will fall on her child and begins with a good deed. This thought helps her stop; it has a force rooted in her life. But if one good act can cancel past harm and a child is treated as someone who can bear another's punishment, good and evil still pass between people who do not owe each other's debts. Her first step should lead toward how she treats people from now on. The child has no debt to assume on her behalf.

Wang Bo's rescue of Sha Gen remains moving. Being moved can change how we see someone; it cannot make everyone else disappear from his past. Who believes for whom now also asks who forgives for whom. Seeing his change has not made us the representatives of all his victims.

## She Still Has to Finish Her Meal

*A World Without Thieves* does not end with Wang Bo fixed in his most tragic pose. Five months later, Wang Li sits eating. The plainclothes officer tells her to stop waiting; Wang Bo is gone. She asks him to wait until she has finished before saying more. Here the film uses a flashback to reveal the final struggle, then returns to the table. She cries and keeps eating.[^ending]

First, how he died; then, how she lives. Placing them together shows the days his sacrifice has not provided for.

Wang Li has never simply been a woman waiting for Wang Bo. She is the first to want to leave their old life and the first to protect Sha Gen. She wants a different beginning for the child; Wang Bo joins that hope later. Now Sha Gen's money is safe, but Wang Bo has not returned. A heroic death has not finished changing the life she originally wanted to change.

*Infernal Affairs III* also leaves us with a woman who comes to visit. Mary tells the badly injured Lau that the child can already say Daddy.[^children] The person who heard the recording and later left the marriage now makes her own decision to bring him news of the child. Lau sits there, unable to answer as he once did. The child is already growing up.

That word declares neither Lau's innocence nor the child's inheritance of his guilt. What a person does changes the lives of his family, but they still have lives of their own. Mary can leave and can also bring news. The child can have attachments without taking on the task of proving anything for the father. The film's punishment cannot close off their futures too.

After watching these films, we can still hope that a person may one day turn back. Sha Gen's belief gave Wang Bo an opportunity to refuse to betray someone's trust. Chan's refusal did not make change impossible for Lau. It required him to begin changing in the company of the people and events he did not want to face. Believing that someone can become better should leave him things to do and leave others things to say.

Wang Li has no such composure at this moment. She asks to finish eating before hearing more, but her tears come first. The child is inside her, and she too must live. She eats, one mouthful after another.
