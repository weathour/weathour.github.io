> **Spoiler warning:** This essay discusses the confession and graduation-day scenes in *WHITE ALBUM 2: Introductory Chapter*, as well as the ending of Natsume Soseki's *Kokoro*. All English renderings of game dialogue are my translations from the Japanese script, not official English lines.[^source]
>
> **Concept note:** By “third term” I mean only a relationship name or promise that remains available for invocation after it has been proposed and accepted. It is neither the concrete third person nor the outsider's position created by an exclusive relation. A third person's later acknowledgment brings the term into the relation among three; that acknowledgment neither creates the original bond nor necessarily endorses it.
>
> **Cover note:** The cover is an original conceptual image. Two envelopes on opposite sides of a doorway and three platforms at different heights evoke unequal access to messages and replies. It is neither a game image, a book cover, a cemetery photograph, nor a reconstruction of any specific scene.

Kazusa already knows from Haruki that he and Setsuna have begun dating. Later, with Haruki absent, she confirms the matter with Setsuna: “This is a conclusion Kitahara and Ogiso reached between them.” She says that she accepts the promise made by her two friends and will wish them well. Then Kazusa asks Setsuna to stop calling her “Touma-san.” From then on, they will call each other “Setsuna” and “Kazusa.”[^conclusion]

A confession between two people becomes a “conclusion” and a “promise” when it reaches a third. Kazusa took no part in the confession, yet her acknowledgment matters. Their use of given names also preserves her friendship with Setsuna. Haruki and Setsuna's exchange does not cancel their older feelings. Instead, it creates obligations against which later conduct can be judged: which actions will hurt a lover, and which will betray a friend.

A conclusion reached by two now constrains someone who had no part in deciding it. The question is not only how that constraint arises. Can the person affected learn the reasons, respond, and change what happens next? The phrase “third party” folds three different levels together here. We need to separate them first.

## Three People, Three Changes of Position

Haruki is absent when Setsuna recruits Kazusa. Setsuna first catches up with Kazusa as she is about to leave. Their conversation moves through dinner, time alone, friendship, and the school festival; Kazusa asks in return how Setsuna feels about Haruki. On Sunday, all three go to the Ogiso home. Haruki leaves first, and only afterward does Kazusa make Setsuna a conditional promise: if Setsuna can persuade her parents, Kazusa will consider joining the club. Kazusa comes out and tells Haruki about the agreement herself. That evening, Setsuna convinces her father, and Kazusa's membership is finally settled.[^recruitment]

At this point, Haruki is simply the third person outside the conversation. The women discuss him, and Kazusa's decision will affect him. No romantic promise has yet formed, so no one has been placed outside an exclusive relation. Kazusa and Setsuna are discussing friendship, a performance, and the condition for joining the club. The presence of someone beyond the pair before us does not by itself mean that a romantic relation already has an outsider.

The positions change after the school festival. Setsuna narrows Haruki's proposed future for the three of them into a future for two, and Haruki eventually kisses her of his own accord. Her request and his acceptance establish their relationship. Haruki tells Kazusa what happened; Kazusa later offers her blessing face to face and acknowledges the promise between her two friends. Only then does Kazusa stand outside the romantic promise between Haruki and Setsuna, while remaining the friend whom Setsuna addresses by her given name.[^confession]

When Haruki recounts the previous night to Kazusa the following evening, he also changes who first asked for the relationship. He says that he confessed to Setsuna and that she accepted. Haruki did initiate the final kiss, and the responsibility for that act remains his. Yet Setsuna had first narrowed the future of three into a request for two, bringing the question to him step by step. “Haruki confessed, and Setsuna accepted” preserves his final action while rearranging the order of request and response.[^retelling]

When Kazusa later speaks to Setsuna, she does not repeat Haruki's retelling. She calls it instead a “conclusion reached by two people.” The phrase cannot tell us who initiated each step of the confession. It does capture the minimum condition under which the relation came into being: one person made a proposal, and the other gave an acceptance that no third person could supply. Kazusa acknowledges a relational fact made by two people, not a unilateral claim of possession.

This does not mean that Kazusa previously had no desire, or that Setsuna confessed in ignorance of the situation. Setsuna had already recognized what was particular about Haruki and Kazusa. She feared that if they became a couple, she would once again be left outside. Her judgment and her earlier experience helped lead her to confess. The order in which feelings arose, one's reading of the situation, and the promises actually made are three different matters. Collapse them into one, and “who loved whom first” soon displaces the more exact question: who asked whom for what, and how did the other respond?[^setsuna]

On graduation day, the pair before us changes to Haruki and Kazusa. Before leaving Japan, Kazusa declares her love. Without Setsuna's knowledge, they recognize their feelings for one another and kiss. Though Setsuna is absent, Kazusa calls herself and Haruki “betrayers,” and she apologizes to the absent Setsuna before crossing the line again. The concrete person outside the scene is now Setsuna, but the outsider's place in the exclusive relation has not shifted simply because the visible pair has changed. Setsuna remains the lover whose proposal Haruki accepted, and Kazusa still remembers what she once acknowledged.[^graduation]

The visible pair changes three times, while the boundary of the romantic relation does not change with it. The third person outside a conversation can change. To know whom an exclusive relation leaves outside, we have to ask how its promise was established and what people later did, not who entered the story first. Something else remains in force across the changing scenes: although one person is absent, the people present can still name a promise in which she took part.

## After a Promise Leaves Its Speakers

Kazusa's word “betrayal” on graduation day has a clear history. Haruki first tells her that he and Setsuna are dating. She then acknowledges their promise in Setsuna's presence. When only Haruki and Kazusa are together, that promise still supplies the standard by which they judge their own actions. Setsuna does not have to enter as an imagined body. Haruki and Kazusa invoke the romantic commitment in which she took part.

I call a relationship name or promise established through one party's proposal and the other's acceptance, then invoked in someone's absence to judge an action, a relation's “third term.” Setsuna's proposal and Haruki's acceptance first establish their relationship. Kazusa's later acknowledgment makes it a fact that all three know and can invoke. “Lovers” names Haruki and Setsuna's relation. “The promise between two friends” is Kazusa's acknowledgment of that same relation from the third person's position. The term is not Setsuna herself, Kazusa's position, or anyone's present desire.

A relation does not acquire this force merely because one person names it. Suppose Haruki had simply announced to Kazusa, “Setsuna is my girlfriend,” though Setsuna had never asked him for a relationship and he had never accepted. That sentence would be Haruki's unilateral claim about the relation. The script first gives us Setsuna's request, then Haruki's kiss, and only afterward Kazusa's knowledge and acknowledgment. By the time “lovers” reaches Kazusa, actions by both parties already support it. The name does not belong to Haruki's version alone.

“Friends” has its own history. Setsuna first asks Kazusa to be her friend. Kazusa does not treat her only as a messenger recruiting on Haruki's behalf. She turns back to ask about Setsuna's feelings, makes the promise to join, and later proposes that they address each other by their given names. Friendship is not exclusive, but concealment and exploitation can still wound a friend, who can question the act face to face. Haruki and Setsuna's romantic promise and Kazusa and Setsuna's friendship therefore overlap in Kazusa. When she later crosses the line, she touches both relations. Neither one cancels the other.

Relational names put obligations on record; they do not illuminate every desire. “Friends” leaves room for probing questions and jealousy about Haruki. “Lovers” does not make Haruki's feelings for Kazusa disappear. When Kazusa calls them “betrayers,” she judges their intimacy against the existing romantic relation. When she apologizes to Setsuna, she records the separate debt created by friendship. These names make people accountable to one another, but they cannot guarantee anyone's sincerity. A unilateral declaration can report one person's desire. It cannot provide a mutually recognized standard for breach.

A promise can support accountability without giving everyone affected a part in the whole decision. Kazusa must face the conclusion reached by Haruki and Setsuna. Whether she can learn its history, object to it, and alter what comes next still depends on how the words travel to her. Natsume Soseki's *Kokoro* makes this route unusually clear. Its most consequential truths are spoken again and again, yet Shizu, who lives with their consequences, is never among their recipients.

## Which Route Does the Truth Take?

When K tells Sensei that he loves the young lady, neither she nor her mother is at home. The young lady is Shizu, who later becomes Sensei's wife; the novel consistently calls her mother the widow. Sensei's first thought on hearing the confession is that K has “beaten him to it.” He first confirms that K has told neither woman. On a later walk, in a separate conversation, K asks Sensei for an impartial judgment. At the crucial moment, Sensei alone can see the two men's desires, K's hesitation, and the young lady's everyday conduct at once.[^k-confession]

K's love is not a third term in the sense used here. The young lady has not heard the declaration or responded to it. A one-sided declaration has not created a relation recognized by two people and available for others to invoke. It does, however, give Sensei information he can act on. [Essay VI, “Who Lives the Days After a Choice?”](/en/posts/white-album-2-who-lives-after-choice/) examined how Sensei turns K's moral maxim against him, then bypasses K and the young lady to propose through the widow. K asks a friend for judgment; because the friend hears the secret first, a rival gains time to act first.

The secret circulates between men, but the widow and Shizu continue to act. The widow initially opposes K's moving in, then yields to Sensei's persuasion. The mother and daughter's daily contact with K brings him into the household in practice. When Sensei abruptly proposes marriage, the widow assures him on her daughter's behalf that she will not object, turning a secret move made in advance into an engagement. She does not know about K's confession, because when she asks whether K has said anything, Sensei says no. Shizu keeps asking questions after the marriage, yet both K's confession and Sensei's later retrospective confession bypass her. The widow and Shizu can both act; that is exactly why Sensei's decisions have consequences. He decides whom to tell and whom to keep uninformed.[^widow]

Years later, Sensei chooses another recipient. In a long letter, he recounts how he met K, heard the confession, proposed first, and then lived the rest of his life in guilt after K's death. He sends the letter only to the young narrator, “I.” Sensei tells him that as long as Shizu is alive, she must never learn the past recorded there. He arranges delivery near the death he expects, leaving the recipient a confession that can scarcely be questioned before its writer is gone.[^long-letter]

K's confession travels first to Sensei; Sensei's retrospective confession later travels to the young narrator. What the young man reads is a version Sensei selected, ordered, and explained decades afterward. K's tone, the young lady's actions, and the widow's judgments reach him largely through Sensei's retelling. Sensei admits his guilt voluntarily, while still dictating how the young man should hear him and what Shizu must not hear.

The novel does not finish the aftermath on the reader's behalf. By the time the young man reads the letter to its end, he has left his ailing father and boarded a train to Tokyo. The novel returns to neither Sensei nor the father to depict either death. The retrospective narration in the first part has already established that Sensei later dies; only the fate of the young man's father remains unstated. Nor does the book tell us whether the young man obeys the order of secrecy after reaching Tokyo. The visible consequence is that the letter changes the order of his actions.[^ending]

Shizu is never chosen as a recipient. She lived in the same house as K, married Sensei after K's death, and directly endured her husband's gloom and distance. Yet the truth connecting K's confession, Sensei's preemptive proposal, and K's death never travels to her. She can only infer causes from the changes in her husband over many years.

Speaking a truth does not ensure that everyone affected will hear it. Choosing whom to tell first distributes the ability to judge and act first. Others can only infer from results. Sensei uses K's secret to secure the engagement before K can act, then leaves his own explanation to a male inheritor. Knowing more because he has read the long letter does not give the young man the right to speak for Shizu.

The long letter is delivered once. The graveyard returns the same past to Sensei's marriage every month. When the novel reaches the grave, however, it gives two conflicting versions of whether Shizu ever went there with him.

## One Grave, Two Accounts

In the first part of *Kokoro*, the young man follows Sensei to the cemetery at Zoshigaya. After noticing him, Sensei asks whether Shizu has told him the name of the person in the grave. The young man says no. At this point, he does not even know who is buried there; he knows only that Sensei visits every month. Later, when he asks to accompany Sensei again, Sensei says that he has never brought even his wife.[^cemetery]

The long letter tells a different story. Sensei writes that after their marriage, Shizu herself proposed that they visit K's grave together. In this version, they did go once. Sensei simply decided never to take her again.[^shared-visit]

“I have never brought her” and “we went together once after our marriage” cannot both be literally true. Sensei may have lied to the young man at the time. He may have revised the story years later, or his memory may be mistaken. The novel supplies no second witness to decide the question. Both the claim in the first part and the version in the letter ultimately come from Sensei.

The two versions arise in very different speaking situations. When the young man asks to come again, Sensei invokes the fact that he has supposedly never brought even his wife to show how private the place is. Earlier, at the grave, he has already checked whether Shizu revealed the dead man's name. In the long letter, Sensei decides to entrust his life story to the young man while commanding him to keep it from Shizu. His earlier reply limits what the young man may know then. The letter regulates whom he may tell later. Though the versions conflict, Sensei controls the direction in which each one travels.

The contradiction also changes the particulars of Shizu's position. If we follow the first version, she never took part in her husband's regular mourning at all. If we follow the letter, she proposed a visit and went once, only to be excluded afterward. The first stresses that she never entered even the cemetery. The second makes a different point sharper: standing before the grave does not mean receiving the past.

Shizu continues to act. She knows that her husband's disposition changed after K's death. She asks him directly why he is so gloomy and why he has withdrawn from her, and she urges him to stop drinking. In the letter's version, she is also the one who proposes the visit to the grave. Her questions and her footsteps already reach toward her husband's past. What she still lacks is the causal explanation connecting confession, proposal, and death.[^shizu]

Even if Shizu did accompany him once, shared mourning did not produce a shared explanation. Sensei understands concealment as protection and wants to preserve Shizu's “white” memory. The care is sincere. It is also bound up with his control over what she should know.

K continues to shape the marriage after death because Sensei repeats a set of actions. The dead man cannot schedule a monthly ritual, select the recipient of a letter, or order that Shizu remain uninformed for life. Sensei goes to the same grave each month, sustains his distance at home, keeps the secret from his wife, and decades later arranges his past for the young narrator. These acts keep K within the marriage. Every question Shizu asks still meets a husband who will not explain its cause.

Shizu participates in the marriage and may have participated in its mourning. Yet the past that keeps shaping the marriage never becomes something she and her husband can judge together. The grave, the ritual, and the recipient maintain and transmit that past. They do not thereby become the relation's third term.

## Who Has Not Had a Chance to Answer?

K in *Kokoro* is dead. Kazusa in *WHITE ALBUM 2* can still act. Their fates cannot be mapped onto each other character by character. The works can nevertheless be placed together to ask how the living arrange another person's absence, and whether those affected retain the conditions needed to change what comes next.

After acknowledging Haruki and Setsuna's promise, Kazusa is not fixed once and for all as a symbol by their conclusion. She can still conceal her plan to leave Japan, refuse Haruki's pursuit, confess her love over the phone, and apologize to Setsuna before crossing the line again. These acts are not necessarily fair, nor do they all arise from one motive. Each changes what the three can do next. A concrete third person always retains possibilities for action that exceed the position assigned by a relation.

Shizu's room for action is narrower. She can ask questions, but she never receives the causal facts controlled by Sensei, nor can she question him face to face before he acts. To write her as a voiceless wife would repeat Sensei's treatment of her: disregarding what she has already said, then deciding on her behalf what kind of purity her life requires.

Return to Kazusa's opening sentence. Setsuna's proposal and Haruki's acceptance first establish their relationship. Kazusa's knowledge and acknowledgment then make it a fact all three can invoke. A conclusion reached by two can therefore constrain action even when one person is absent. Accountability is only the beginning. If the arrangement is to involve all three, those who bear its consequences must know the decisive facts, have a chance to question them, and be able to change the next step through their own response. Someone may be living under a conclusion while still having to guess why it was reached.

Once one version is written down, preserved, and carried into the years ahead, who gets to decide what kind of common past it becomes? [The next essay, “Who Makes a Shared Past Count?”](/en/posts/white-album-2-who-writes-the-past/) begins there.
