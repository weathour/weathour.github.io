> Call on the void to yield something; knock on silence and seek a sound.
>
> — Lu Ji, *Wen Fu*[^titles]

This essay was finished once before.

At the time, it was called “Writing Happens While Thought Is Unfinished.” It began with six parallel notes and discussed how materials connect, how sections are ordered, how sentences clarify relations, and finally how a writer revises in response to a trial reading. Each part had its reasons and addressed a real difficulty in writing. Yet the question most worth asking in that title remained insufficiently developed: amid all these arrangements, what actually happens to thought?

A set of materials is hard to understand; rearranging it makes it easier. A judgment is crude; in writing it out, the author discovers that the very thing they want to say has changed. These are two changes that may affect each other. The old essay spent considerable space explaining the first. The second often passed by in a phrase such as “content continues to emerge.”

The essay had taken shape, but the understanding had not been developed to match it. Patching its transitions would make it look more like a finished piece. Rereading its already fluent sentences called instead for a moment of hesitation: what did they make clear, and what did they allow to go unquestioned?

## The Mind Roams with Things — Wenxin Diaolong, “Imaginative Thought”

We do not come to writing empty-handed.

We bring books we have read, things we have lived through, words we have heard, or an attraction or unease whose cause we cannot yet name. Some of what we know is reliable and ready to use; some has only an outline. We may dislike a passage without knowing which word needs changing. We may grasp something about a situation without having distinguished the relations within it. This understanding, still short of a full account, gives us a reason to keep writing.

In “Imaginative Thought,” a chapter of *Wenxin Diaolong*, Liu Xie describes literary thought opening onto vast distances: it “reaches across a thousand years” and “sees across ten thousand li.” He also writes that “the mind roams with things.” Thought travels with what we have seen and heard; things enter literary thought through the senses and through language. At the moment of writing, however, he describes a different experience: “As we take up the brush, our spirit exceeds our words; when the piece is finished, it falls halfway short of the heart’s first intent.” What seemed abundant in anticipation has not quite survived into the finished work.[^shensi]

This shortfall may mean that expressive skill cannot keep up with an existing understanding. It may also reveal that things which seemed connected have not actually been joined. When we imagine an essay, many paragraphs seem to exist at once. Once we write a sentence, we must decide what it says, whether the reasons before it support it, and whether the next sentence can follow from it.

Liu Xie attends to both the passage and the obstruction between intention, thought, and words. He also draws on the making of cloth from hemp, with loom and shuttle, to describe the work of giving writing form. The material is already there; what it becomes also depends on its making. In our own writing, choices of wording leave traces we can return to. An “always” that seemed entirely right yesterday may have to be reconsidered today in the light of another piece of evidence.

The thing itself has not necessarily changed. What changes is how the writer understands it and how the reasons within that understanding relate to one another. A theorem already proved may keep its conclusion throughout an explanation. Yet explaining why a particular assumption is necessary may change the writer’s understanding of the proof’s structure. They knew the result before; now they can also explain why the argument fails without that condition. Established knowledge provides a basis for further understanding.

Writing can therefore begin with different degrees of certainty. We may bring known facts, or a question worth clarifying. Words give some part of this material a shape that the next act of thought can encounter. Once a sentence is on the page, its author may notice a distinction: when I call this person “weak,” do I mean that they dare not act, or that they refuse to do what I expect? A single word had covered willingness, ability, and the expectations of the person passing judgment. Now those relations begin to separate.

Such distinctions can change the choice of materials. Actions previously placed in one category need to be compared again. An instance of persistence that was left out may start to matter. The word has supplied no facts on its own, but it has helped the writer see how they were organizing the facts. The new understanding must find support in the materials and then return to the sentence.

There is also pleasure in this movement. A metaphor lets two distant things illuminate each other. A way of naming gives scattered experiences something in common that can be discussed. A description gives a particular color to a feeling previously called only “sadness.” The discovery need not always be a refutation. Sometimes it is here that something worth writing about first appears.

## Turn Back to Examine Yourself — Mencius, “Li Lou I”

Lu Xun’s “A Small Incident” gives a brief and searching account of a change in judgment.[^small]

The story’s narrator leaves home early to earn his living and hires a rickshaw. The strong north wind slackens, the dust clears, and the puller runs faster. Near the destination, an old woman crosses in front of the rickshaw. Her ragged cotton waistcoat catches on a shaft, and she slowly falls to the ground.

The narrator already has a judgment. She fell slowly, so he supposes she is unhurt. Nobody else saw it, so they might as well carry on. The puller stops, and the narrator resents his making trouble, inviting a dispute, and delaying the journey.

“Making trouble,” 多事, has already assigned the incident its meaning. The puller’s job is to deliver his passenger; stopping adds an unnecessary complication. The woman’s condition is quickly decided, and “besides, nobody else had seen” becomes another reason to leave. The road, the vehicle, the journey, and the person on the ground all take their relative importance from the narrator’s need to get on his way.

So he says, “It’s nothing. Keep going!”

The puller does not continue the journey. He lowers the rickshaw, helps the woman up, and asks what has happened. “I’ve hurt myself,” she answers. The narrator thinks she is putting on an act and that the puller is asking for trouble. The puller continues to support her as they walk toward the door of the police station.

The story does not establish that the narrator was right about her being unhurt. Nor does it give the puller a moral speech. One person has reached a judgment; another continues to act according to a different set of relations. By asking the woman, the puller brings her own answer into the incident. The narrator’s judgment had seemed easy to close. The action before him prevents that closure.

Then the dust-covered back of the departing man seems to grow taller. The narrator has to look up at it. It becomes an oppressive presence, as if it would “squeeze out the ‘smallness’ hidden beneath my fur robe.”

At the beginning there is a “small incident.” Here, “smallness” appears beneath the robe. The first concerns the incident’s weight among great affairs; the second concerns the person accustomed to judging others. The puller keeps walking, but the narrator’s way of looking at him has changed. The man who measured others by the needs of his own journey and decided their interests for them now feels that the incident has exposed something in himself.

If the story ended here, we could certainly say that the puller’s conduct had made the narrator ashamed. It goes a step further.

A policeman comes out and tells him to hire another rickshaw: this puller cannot take him any farther. The narrator takes out a large handful of copper coins and asks the policeman to pass them on. After leaving, he asks himself what the money means. “Was I rewarding him? Could I still sit in judgment on the puller?”

These two questions give further meaning to the earlier tallness and smallness. The narrator can praise the puller and give him money. Yet either gesture may leave him occupying the position of someone entitled to assess and reward another person. The puller has made him ashamed, but responding has not thereby become simple. His original judgment is in trouble; even the stance from which he might make his next judgment has become a question.

The money’s meaning remains unsettled. It is handed to the policeman; the story does not say whether the puller eventually receives it or how he accepts it. The narrator has acted, but the action cannot easily settle his questions about himself. The reader encounters something beyond a reversal from disapproval to praise.

At first, the narrator thinks he knows what the puller should do. Then he sees the puller do something else. Later, he begins to ask what made him so certain in the first place. What he is trying to understand comes to include his own position as the person who judges. Something has happened to him, changing how he looks back on what happened.

The change continues into the present of the telling. The ending speaks of shame and self-renewal, but also of courage and hope. Unanswered questions have not deprived the incident of meaning. The very things that resist an easy answer keep it worth remembering.

What we are reading is the story’s finished prose. It makes a change in understanding available for close attention. How Lu Xun actually conceived, drafted, and revised it would require other materials. What the finished work offers is already rich enough: an apparently settled judgment encounters an action, exposes its own grounds, and brings the person judging back into question.

## Words Must Fit Reality, and Artistry Comes Hard — Wenxin Diaolong, “Imaginative Thought”

“Helping an old woman, the puller makes his passenger ashamed” preserves a line of action and a direction of moral judgment. Yet the relations still unfolding in “A Small Incident” fall away from that sentence. We learn whom to admire without necessarily seeing where the narrator’s understanding runs into difficulty.

Take “besides, nobody else had seen.”

It follows the narrator’s supposition that the woman is unhurt. Whether she is hurt concerns her body; whether anybody saw concerns the attention of others. The narrator puts the two together and decides there is no need to stay. The small Chinese word 又, “besides,” exposes the different kinds of reason supporting one judgment. The scope of his concern has entered the syntax.

When the puller faces the passenger’s urging, the original leaves a pause: “taking no notice—or perhaps not having heard—he lowered the rickshaw.” “Taking no notice” seems already to identify an attitude. The intervening 或者, “perhaps,” preserves another possibility. We can establish that he lowers the rickshaw and helps the woman up. We cannot thereby establish that he heard his employer and deliberately refused him.

That distinction matters to the relation between the characters. “The puller refused me with contempt” would add a psychological declaration the story never supplies. Condensing everything to “the puller stopped to help” would lose this hesitation in the narrator’s judgment of another person. The original gives the action force while preserving what the narrator has not been able to establish.

“Was I rewarding him? Could I still sit in judgment on the puller?” also has an order of its own. The first question begins with the handful of money just given away. The second follows the relation implicit in “rewarding” to ask who has the right to judge. The Chinese 还, “still,” joins past and present: a standing that was once taken for granted can no longer simply be exercised as before.

“I realized I had no right to judge the puller” would state a conclusion more directly. It would also turn the moment of being unable to answer oneself into an understanding already achieved. The difference is more than one of detail. A question and a statement establish different degrees of certainty, presenting a different version of the narrator to the reader.

The work of wording takes place in such choices. “Besides” puts reasons together; “perhaps” loosens a claim about someone else’s intention; “still” brings an earlier entitlement into question across time. These are ordinary words. The distinctions they make cannot be supplied by a few grander nouns.

The tallness of the departing figure and the “smallness” beneath the robe have another force. They let judgment happen through sight and bodily feeling. The narrator is still seated in the rickshaw as the puller helps the woman away. The distance grows, yet the figure seems larger. The position from which the narrator had looked down on another person changes as he finds himself looking up and feeling overborne. Language gives the relation a perceptible direction.

This expression can be explained, and deserves explanation. It also does its work in the reading itself. Seeing the departing figure and then encountering the “smallness” beneath the robe is a different experience from first accepting a proposition about self-examination. Calling both simply “content” risks overlooking this part of the experience that form helps constitute.

Choosing words and making sentences therefore cannot always be assigned a place after the work of thought. Comparing two formulations also means comparing two understandings. Which identifies the action? Which decides the motive in advance? Which leaves room for an answer not yet given? Which turns a difficult self-questioning into a conclusion accepted too easily? Such comparisons can give content to an understanding that had not yet been fully distinguished.

Precision in writing thus has a wider measure. Some passages need a full account of cause and consequence; others need to preserve what is unknown accurately. An argument cannot use vagueness to avoid explanation, and a narrator should not settle an unfinished relation on everybody’s behalf. The exacting work concerns what the words are being asked to bear.

## Beginning and End Sustain Each Other — Wenxin Diaolong, “Joining the Parts”

Words and sentences make local relations visible. The whole work lets the same word acquire a different weight later on.

The opening of “A Small Incident” already tells us that the incident changed the narrator and remains unforgettable. We know that a change is coming. Knowing the outcome, however, is not the same as understanding the change. We still need to read how he assumes the woman is unhurt, resents the puller’s interference, sees the departing figure, and takes out the coins without being able to answer himself.

The story’s order therefore serves more than suspense. It gives readers enough relations to understand the effect announced at the beginning. The “small incident” first stands beside great national affairs, then enters the passenger’s calculation of what matters, later exposes the “smallness” beneath the robe, and finally endures in memory. These instances of large and small concern different things. They cannot be treated as repetitions of a single meaning; within the work, they illuminate one another.

The time of the event also meets the time of its telling. The narrator then did not answer himself; the narrator telling the story later is still moved by it. The ending preserves a continuing relation: remembering may bring greater clarity and still requires him to think about himself. Without this dimension, the incident could easily become an education already completed. Without courage and hope, it would become a story of shame alone.

A work’s wholeness consists in more than collecting its sentences. It brings its parts into relation and shapes how we return to them. After the episode with the coins, “Keep going!” raises a further question about the speaker’s certainty. No event has been added to what the reader knows. The events already known have entered new relations.

A writer may also find the organization of a piece through this movement back and forth. A paragraph already written makes a detail matter. To make that detail intelligible, an omitted circumstance needs to be supplied earlier. Writing that circumstance may in turn change the paragraph’s original judgment. The shape of the whole becomes clearer, giving the writer a new basis for deciding whether a part belongs.

There is a difficulty here that requires patience: the writer must choose the parts in light of a whole that is not yet finished. What is available is a gradually forming understanding of the whole. It may begin as the direction of a feeling, a contradiction needing explanation, or a passage already written toward which the rest must work. The writer uses this understanding to make choices while allowing new passages to change it. An outline helps the work along; the completed draft may demand a different outline.

A theoretical essay may introduce a concept and give it fuller content through the materials that follow. Another essay may place several situations side by side, letting readers gradually discover the question between them. A recollection may follow time’s sequence or let an old object bring a distant moment back. These organizations have different reasons. Readers need to be able to recognize important relations, but every section need not supply a logical premise for the next.

Organization and the formation of thought meet here. Put a general verdict on a person before their actions, and what follows can readily be read as illustration. Put it after several conflicting actions, and readers can already bring those differences to bear on it. If the writer finds that the sentence cannot accommodate the preceding material, they may revise its scope or reconsider what it is actually judging. Through these revisions, sequence participates in understanding. Merely moving a sentence cannot supply the grounds it lacks.

The reasons for an essay’s organization should be sought in its subject. How does it make its object gradually discernible? How does it let an understanding withstand rereading? Where is it worth lingering, and where has enough been said? These questions come closer to the craft than manufacturing a reason why no two sections can change places. A good continuation makes us want to read on while allowing what we have already read to yield further meaning.

## Follow the Current to Its Source — Lu Ji, Wen Fu

Once writing has established some relations, those relations influence how the writer looks for more material.

Someone preparing to argue that a person always gives way will readily notice scenes of yielding. The more they find, the more reliable the judgment appears. Yet a crucial scene may show that the person yields where others expect firmness while standing resolutely by something those others scarcely value. The writer must then distinguish a claim about the frequency of an action from a verdict on character already carried by the word “yielding.”

The first calls for further comparison of scope and exceptions. The second also calls for asking what this person is defending. Materials previously grouped as examples of the same thing may fall into different relations. Writing changes the question: how do these actions belong to one person, and why do others give one kind more weight?

Theory helps us establish such relations. A concept makes scattered materials comparable, saving us from naming everything afresh. It must also confront what it has not yet handled adequately. Some details require only a further qualification; others make the classification itself unworkable. The difference has to be established from particular grounds. Calling everything “complex” will not distinguish the two.

In his discussion of experience in the Introduction to the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, Hegel brings knowledge, its object, and the criterion of examination into the inquiry together. The examination may reach what was previously taken to be essential, as well as the standard used to examine it.[^hegel] This question sharpens our view of the essay’s own work: does examination in writing test an answer, or does it also test why we asked the question in that way?

The old version of this essay chiefly understood “writing well” as establishing relations among materials and enabling readers to acquire those relations in sequence. By that measure, revision naturally concentrated on supplying premises, adjusting order, and making each section take up what the previous one had left it. This preserved important demands, but it did not adequately explain how thought takes shape. The shortfall can be found between the essay’s own title and its body.

If the difficulty continues to be called “poor transitions,” revision will keep working by the same measure. Trying harder to explain how thought forms may then produce only more instructions about what should come next. What needs to change includes the essay’s understanding of its object: writing also helps questions, concepts, and relations of judgment acquire content. Techniques of organization need a place within that account; they cannot supply the whole explanation.

The new criterion must be tested too. If rewriting merely adds philosophical names while leaving the relations unexplained, a larger theoretical vocabulary cannot establish greater depth. If it helps us distinguish how materials change a concept, how a sentence anticipates a judgment, or how a reader’s objection reaches the way a question is framed, something has been gained. Accuracy and coherence, preserved by the old criterion, remain useful. They help these insights become a readable essay.

Such revision has a direction, but it need not always begin in failure. Sometimes writing actively tries an unfamiliar organization through which experiences acquire new connections. A description places things previously kept apart in one paragraph, letting the reader see how they happen together. A new question takes shape in the making and is then tested against the materials.

Research and composition work on each other in this way. We read an original passage and find a sentence we cannot explain. We write our explanation and find another scene that does not fit it. After the concept is revised, a previously unremarkable detail begins to matter. At each turn, something in the judgment becomes more definite. What we have read does not automatically become our understanding; what we have written must also be able to face the materials again.

This contact with the materials gives imagination and abstraction somewhere to work. An author can travel far, make comparisons, and offer an interpretation nobody has stated before. The new understanding must bring more of the object’s relations into view and give the inferences more grounds to stand on. That is how it gains the force to sustain further writing.

## Together We Examine Doubtful Meanings — Tao Yuanming, “Moving House,” I

When we write for someone else, we have to consider where they will enter.

Some background must be explained; some terms can become intelligible through use. Sometimes an early conclusion tells readers why the essay is worth their time. Sometimes an experience needs room to unfold before a conclusion can avoid flattening it. The writer’s arrangements help readers acquire what they need to judge.

But readers also have their own experience, interests, and questions. An explanation of a situation may meet a different example from their lives. A distinction the writer considers minor may be precisely what they most need to understand. As an essay enters a reading, it enters relations it could not have exhausted in advance.

These responses let the writer test the prose. If someone misidentifies the speaker, the reference needs clarification. If they cannot find the grounds for an inference, the reasons need to be supplied. A reader may also understand the entire essay accurately and point out that its author has compared only two cases, leaving out a third that could change the conclusion. At that point, the reader has brought the essay new material.

A deeper response asks why this particular criterion should govern the judgment. The coins in “A Small Incident” leave just such a question in the text. If one reader takes them simply as a reward and another presses the question that follows “Was I rewarding him?”, the discussion can move from whether the sum was enough to how the giver understands himself. This objection can be tested against the words on the page. The discussion need not remain a contest between private impressions.

Differences in understanding therefore deserve investigation, but their existence does not decide in advance who needs to change. They may expose an expressive difficulty, a factual error, a missing inference, or a real disagreement about values. The author must make their reasons visible, and the reader’s reasons must have a chance to enter. If “you have not understood” can account for every disagreement, the essay has lost a means of learning from others.

This calls for taking readers seriously. Accessible language can put complex relations within more people’s reach. Once the explanation is theirs to use, they can bring it to their own circumstances and discover where it does not apply. When someone begins using a formulation, more has happened than memorizing its conclusion. They combine, try out, and retell it, returning with new questions.

There is also a responsibility in guiding understanding. The more powerful an explanation, the more it may affect how people understand themselves and others. If a writer calls a person’s difficulty a defect of character, readers may use that account to blame themselves. If the relations within the difficulty are explained, the range of possible actions may change. The essay must answer for the grounds and scope of these judgments and acknowledge that the people concerned may bring knowledge the author lacks.

An essay that seeks agreement by giving reasons has already made its judgment available for examination. If a reader brings relevant material, the author needs to explain whether it changes the original judgment. If the reader advances a different demand about what ought to matter, both sides need to identify the reasons on which their disagreement rests. This gives the reader’s standing to respond a basis. The author may hold to a well-grounded conclusion; what they uphold should be able to withstand the same questioning.

Writers and readers need not have equal knowledge. In an explanation, the writer may know the key to a proof while the reader is learning it. In criticism, the writer may know more versions of a work and more of its details. The difference makes explanation necessary. Setting out the reasons also lets the reader gradually examine them, perhaps eventually taking a new question beyond the original explanation. Expertise can continue to matter through such use.

What writing offers includes the possibility of judging further. Accurate restatement is one achievement. Recognizing the original relations in another situation and knowing where fresh evidence is needed takes the understanding further. If a long essay leaves its reader with only a few beautiful conclusions and no way of approaching what it explains, the power of its words has not been fully passed on.

An essay can therefore leave something unfinished. That opening needs to make clear what remains unresolved and why it is worth pursuing. If a crucial reason has been omitted, readers can only guess what the writer meant. Once the relevant relations have been sufficiently developed, they may be able to take up the question with materials and judgments of their own.

## Those Who Read After Us — Wang Xizhi, “Preface to the Orchid Pavilion Collection”

What is written can leave the present moment.

A judgment made today can be found again tomorrow. Someone quoting it can point to particular words. An experience takes shape in a telling, giving later readers a place to enter. A concept becomes familiar through repeated use, providing a starting point for new observations. The persistence of writing allows understanding to accumulate, so that those who come later need not begin from a blank page.

This stability can also begin to organize the writer’s activity. Yesterday’s provisional explanation enters an outline and may become today’s premise for selecting material. A rule that once helped revise an essay goes into a set of working instructions and shapes decisions about emphasis in the next. Earlier achievements become present conditions.

These conditions have histories. One rule was devised for an essay whose structure was loose; one omission suited readers already familiar with the background. In another essay, they may still help, or they may conceal a new demand. Their earlier effectiveness has to find its reasons again in the work now being done.

Writing therefore includes rereading what it leaves behind. We return to a judgment to ask both whether its wording is accurate and what it originally answered, then what it was later used to do. A sentence describing a limited relation takes on a different task when excerpted as a universal conclusion. Writers can revise, explain, and supply its history; readers can ask them to do so.

When writing with AI, this continuation deserves particular attention. If the same explanation enters the outline, draft, and subsequent evaluation, those texts can readily agree with one another. Their agreement shows that they have worked with similar assumptions and definitions. We still need to return to the works, sources, and actual questions to test those assumptions and definitions themselves. Collaboration can help find materials, compare formulations, and identify counterexamples. These activities need to keep the object’s relations entering the prose.

For a writer, owning a passage matters too. “I think” takes responsibility for a judgment. “We all know” also invokes shared knowledge or a shared position. Who belongs to that “we,” and who can disagree, may become clearer only when other people take up the essay. The speaker’s position takes on content within these relations as well.

The narrator of “A Small Incident” never settles securely into a final judge’s seat. He has seen the puller’s action and has handed over money; he still has himself to face. The question has not deprived him of all judgment. It has given judgment another object and given a possible later change a reason. The ending’s courage and hope coexist with shame.

A writer need not be able to explain their entire history before beginning. Each particular act of expression can first make clear a judgment worth taking responsibility for. Through materials and responses, the writer may then recognize assumptions and distinctions they had not previously seen. Some changes concern a single phrase; others reach the task of the whole essay. Their weight has to be understood through the relations they actually change.

Finishing an essay is, in this light, a positive achievement. It gives what has been understood a sufficiently definite shape, so that the reader need not supply the writer’s missing grounds. Judgments that can be made are made; distinctions that need preserving are preserved; relations still unresolved have a discernible place. The writing fulfills its task here.

Later reading may still bring discoveries. New material may warrant a revision of the original judgment; rereading may also deepen our understanding of something already written correctly. The definite form left by completion gives these activities somewhere to begin.

A sentence on paper can be taken up again. We can ask what it refers to and why it says what it does, and also what it enables to appear. The writer first puts the understanding they have reached into others’ hands, and readers continue from there. Writing happens while thought is unfinished. It also leaves words through which thought may happen again.
