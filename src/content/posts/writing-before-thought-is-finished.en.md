Writing is saying clearly what you want to say.

The sentence sounds right. It makes writing seem like a delivery problem: the author already possesses complete content, then finds words that will carry it accurately to a reader. When the prose stalls, expression appears to be the problem. Revision becomes the search for better words for a meaning already settled.

When I began this article, I wrote six notes:

```text
Content: What is the article actually trying to say?
Materials: How should materials be selected, removed, and tested for the support they provide one another?
Reader: The author can see things at once; the reader can take them in only sentence by sentence.
Language: Diction, syntax, pause, rhythm, and rhetoric.
Medium: Title, notes, images, page, and links.
Revision: How can a trial reading locate what really went wrong?
```

Every line works, and every line could become a section. They can also be rearranged at will. Language could come before the reader without damaging the section on materials; medium could disappear while the other five remain intact. Such an article would tell its reader six things about writing without showing why Section 3 needs the first two. The notes are clear. The article has not yet appeared.

The familiar opening sentence now leaves a question behind. If what the author “wants to say” does not wait intact before writing begins, what exactly is being made clear?

## 1. Before the First Sentence, No Article Is Waiting

The materials, experience, and even a preliminary conclusion already exist. They have not yet become the particular selections, relations, and weights of this article.

When a completed and checked mathematical proof is rewritten as a long explanation for undergraduates, the writer still rearranges definitions, examples, and derivations. The theorem and the proof’s core relations can remain unchanged. My subject is therefore prose in which an author must still form a contestable claim from heterogeneous materials and take responsibility for it.

The six notes placed content first, as though the other five merely executed its instructions. Yet an article for novices must first make the notes’ structural failure visible. For readers familiar with argument theory, the difficulty may instead lie in turning relations among materials into a reading sequence. Once the intended reader becomes specific, the author has to answer again what this article needs to explain.

I had already begun to remove material when I tried three working titles. “Six Dimensions of Writing” preserves the parallel list. “How to Write an Article Clearly” confines the problem to expression. By choosing the present title, I owe the reader an actual change in the claim.

Linda Flower and John Hayes rejected a fixed sequence of prewriting, writing, and rewriting. In their account, planning, producing prose, and reviewing can be embedded in one another. When writers reread a sentence they have just written, they may generate subordinate goals or revise a higher-level goal. Once a sentence exists on the page, the conditions governing the next act of writing have changed.[^flower]

Carl Bereiter and Marlene Scardamalia make the change more specific through their distinction between knowledge telling and knowledge transforming. A writer may follow topic and genre cues and write out suitable knowledge in sequence. The writer may also discover, while trying to decide how to say something, that what they believe remains unsettled. The rhetorical problem then returns to the content problem, and the revised claim invalidates the old organization.[^bereiter]

I had originally placed “the reader” after content, where it would introduce ways to reduce difficulty. At this point, imagining where a reader would ask for another explanation exposed the mathematical proof as a boundary case. Section 1 therefore leaves a provisional claim: when evidence must still determine how far an author can go, writing participates in forming the claim. The next difficulty is that a folder of relevant materials does not identify the relations among them.

## 2. Materials Come with No Natural Order

Thirty papers, ten cases, and a folder of quotations constitute a set of materials. Their presence together shows only that the author put them there. The author still has to decide whether they support, limit, or conflict with one another.

The mathematical-proof example in Section 1 supplies a limiting relation: it narrows the central claim from writing in general to long-form prose in which an author must still form a contestable judgment from heterogeneous materials. Treated merely as “another case of writing,” it would lose that function. Materials acquire order because one changes the scope, grounds, or consequences of another claim, not because they share a topic.

The introduction establishes one fact: six individually clear notes can still be rearranged at will. Section order comes from a relay of premises: readers acquire one premise before they can test or revise an answer later. A manual arranged for lookup can keep its entries parallel, marking the boundary of this inference.

Stephen Toulmin separated two questions that often collapse into one: “What grounds do you have?” and “How do you get from those grounds to this claim?” More citations can add grounds while leaving the warrant empty.[^toulmin] I keep that check in the material ledger: what does an item establish directly, which step belongs to the author, and what would force the conclusion to change?

Relations among materials also include limitation, conflict, cause, and return. Calling relevance support lets citations hide a missing inference. Calling succession causation lets story order replace conditions, action, and consequence. Once the relations are named, absences become more visible than abundance. Some paragraphs need an inferential bridge; others have enough facts and need the author to take responsibility for the claim.

The author now possesses a field of directed and sometimes broken relations. Many nodes are visible at once, and several routes remain possible. The prose can take a reader through only one of them.

## 3. The Author Sees a Field; the Reader Can Follow Only a Path

Consider a paragraph that is easy to produce:

> Article writing includes content, materials, reader, language, medium, and revision. Content determines the center, materials provide support, awareness of the reader affects expression, language concerns clarity and beauty, medium changes presentation, and revision improves quality. These six dimensions are interconnected and together form a complete writing process.

Nothing is obviously false, and the sequence looks orderly. At the end, however, we still possess the original six notes with predicates attached. “Interconnected” has occupied the place where the connections should have been shown; “complete” announces completion before a relation has been established. The second and fifth clauses can trade places with almost no loss.

To test sequence by itself, the material must remain fixed. The following passages use exactly the same five sentences and change only their order.

> **A:** This article began with six parallel topics. Any two could switch places without affecting the rest. The local clarity of each topic had established no dependencies among them. The author first needed to identify relations among the topics. Only then could a reader follow those relations in a particular sequence.

> **B:** The author first needed to identify relations among the topics. Only then could a reader follow those relations in a particular sequence. This article began with six parallel topics. Any two could switch places without affecting the rest. The local clarity of each topic had established no dependencies among them.

A lets the failure occur before it introduces a remedy. That route suits a reader who already has a stack of notes and does not yet know where the problem lies. B presents the remedy before revealing its problem; it need not be wrong in a workshop already titled “How to Arrange Materials.” Relations rule out arbitrary disorder without determining one unique article. The author still chooses among viable routes according to where readers enter and what they presently lack.

The author can scan backward and forward through an outline. A first-time reader has no such overview. The reader meets a title, then a first sentence, and at any moment can use only what has arrived together with prior knowledge. A later qualification may revise an expectation invited earlier; it cannot withdraw the stretch of prose the reader has already crossed.

Stanley Fish’s early reader-response work traced how readers form expectations as words arrive and revise them when later words intervene. I borrow one point: a new order for the same material can alter the process through which a reader forms a judgment. Fish’s *informed reader* is a theoretical position; the note states its limits.[^fish]

An outline should therefore record how readers understand the problem on entering a section and which part of that understanding has changed when they leave. I choose A because this article addresses readers who already have a stack of notes and have not yet located its structural failure.

The gray nodes record material the author has set aside; omission does not make it disappear. Relations with inadequate evidence should be removed, while sound material that leads toward another question can wait for another article. Fragmentary, montage-based, and polyphonic prose also creates reader paths: an object or voice returns, and its meaning changes because of where each return occurs.

The order an author selects in an outline must finally be carried by individual sentences. A relation field can record “the counterexample limits the conclusion.” A sentence has to identify who presents the counterexample, what it limits, and where the conclusion now stops. The writer gives abstract order its agents, degree, and voice.

## 4. Thought Acquires Weight in a Sentence

Return to the opening: Writing is saying clearly what you want to say.

It is easy to read and remember. In the Chinese original, “写作，就是把想说的话说清楚” (*Xiězuò, jiùshì bǎ xiǎng shuō de huà shuō qīngchu*), “写作” names the topic, “就是” promises a definition, and the *bǎ* construction brings the object affected by the action forward. “想说的话,” what one wants to say, appears already in hand. The two instances of “说,” say, echo each other, and “清楚,” clearly, closes the cadence. The sound completes its work before the claim has earned the same completion.

A more precise version might read: “The content of analytical long-form prose continues to emerge through the selection of materials, the identification of relations, the arrangement of sequence, the formation of sentences, entry into a page, and the reception of feedback.” The sentence carries more information while reproducing the six-item list inside a longer container. Its parallel nouns occupy attention while the agent and the changing object retreat behind them. The vocabulary is more exact; the action is harder to follow.

Try again: “Authors also revise what they meant to say as they arrange materials for readers.” The agents are visible, and materials, order, and the central claim now have a direction. The sentence limits itself to one checkable inference; later sentences can unfold the roles of language, page, and feedback.

A sentence distributes action and responsibility. Active and passive constructions decide who stands at the front. Nominalization compresses a process into something that seems already to exist, while adverbs calibrate the strength an author is prepared to claim. If the author cannot say who changed what, more synonyms will merely smooth an unresolved causal relation.

The Chinese sentence can be heard in groups: *xiězuò / jiùshì / bǎ / xiǎng shuō de / huà / shuō qīngchu*. The one-syllable *bǎ* and *huà* interrupt a sequence of longer groups, while the repeated *shuō* ties the sentence together. Replacing “想说的话” with “尚未完成的判断,” an unfinished judgment, brings the semantics closer to this article at the cost of the earlier echo and conversational speed. Word length has no aesthetic rank independent of syntax and use. Revision must ask what this beat lifts and what it makes heavy.

The neat cadence of the earlier line “The notes are clear. The article has not yet appeared” is supported by the attempted rearrangement: the six notes have formed no dependencies. Reading aloud can find a misplaced modifier or too many turns in one breath; the rearrangement establishes the relation behind the sentence.

The opening presents a familiar account of writing and gives the article a stable point to revise. Its voice closes before its evidence; the title, encountered first, places it in the position of an object awaiting examination.

## 5. After the Article Leaves the Author

Under the working title “Six Dimensions of Writing,” the opening would sound like a course description. The present title lets “thought is unfinished” press against “what you want to say” and requires the body to show a claim changing. The sentence stays fixed while the reader enters through a new tension.

Gérard Genette described titles, covers, and notes as thresholds that arrange how a work enters public reading. A site title, sharing image, and footnote provide digital analogues, although Genette’s own subject remained print.[^genette]

The cover and the figure in Section 3 are original conceptual art for this site. The cover provides an entrance through marks that have not entirely closed; the figure separates a simultaneously visible relation field from a sequential path. The body identifies the gray nodes and explains why an author has set one aside.

Footnotes open the bibliography, page numbers, and methods for inspection, while the body states what a source does in the present inference. On a phone, the caption, footnote return, and paragraph order still have to appear. Only then can another person walk the route the author imagined. The first place that reader takes another direction becomes the starting point for revision.

## 6. Revision Begins at the First Divergence

The old outline turned “content, materials, reader, language, medium, revision” directly into six sections. The first test reader could restate every topic but could not explain why Section 3 had to follow Section 2. Any two sections could trade places while the rest still held. The first divergence had appeared before the opening paragraph.

Revision removed the six-part catalogue. The mathematical proof first limits the domain; relations among materials then establish dependencies; the A/B comparison selects a reader path through the same relation field; sentences and title assign weight and entry along that path. The object of revision changed from “how to introduce six kinds of writing knowledge” to “how readers acquire a judgment still taking shape.”

I call the starting point for a trial reading the “first divergence.” Before writing a section, the author records how readers are expected to understand the problem on entry and which part of that understanding should change on exit. A test reader restates what they understood and identifies the first place they mistook the object or could no longer explain why a sentence had appeared. The first point where those two paths separate becomes the candidate for revision.

The candidate still has to be traced upstream. If a reader cannot understand the figure in Section 3, the author first checks whether the caption names the object. If it does, the author returns to the A/B passages and asks whether they established the distinction between a relation field and a reader path. If that distinction remains empty, revision moves back to Section 2 and checks whether the alleged relations are merely new labels.

Once the upstream gap is located, the author changes the smallest unit that can repair it and then checks later sentences that have lost a premise. “Smallest” refers to responsibility: the unit may be one word or an entire section. Sentence-level polish can make a structural failure more fluent, while a complete rewrite can erase work that already holds. Locating the earliest failure determines the size of the repair.

Return now to the six notes in the introduction. They did not become six separate bodies of knowledge. Structural review changed their relations; those relations changed the section order and what the opening sentence and title could mean. “What you want to say” now names a claim taking shape through materials, sentences, and readings. “Clearly” asks whether readers can recover why it holds and where it may change. The grammar still treats those words as an object held in the hand, while the article has recorded how that object changed shape during writing.

Writing is saying clearly what you want to say.
