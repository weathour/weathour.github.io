The door chain was still undone.

After the Affair / Kazusa Normal route, Setsuna continued to enter Haruki's apartment with her own key. Haruki was on leave from work and visited a psychosomatic medicine clinic once a week. He told Setsuna to stop coming and leave him alone, then admitted that he was lying. If she stopped appearing altogether, he would not know what to do.

Haruki pointed out the contradiction himself. He could have fastened the chain or taken back the key if he had meant to keep her out. He had done neither. His words sent her away while his actions kept the entrance open.

[The previous essay, "The Third Person Returns Through Work"](/en/posts/white-album-2-third-person-returns/) ended with Coda's four in-game conclusions. Haruki can continue toward marriage with Setsuna while keeping his feelings for Kazusa private. He can spend a winter with Kazusa, collapse afterward, and leave the two women to manage what remains. He can break up with Setsuna in public, resign, and leave Japan with Kazusa. He can also marry Setsuna while Kazusa remains their friend and musical collaborator. The buttons determine the shape of each relationship. Life continues after the credits.

The three additional stories give those shapes different futures. *不倶戴天の君へ* (working translation: *To You, My Sworn Enemy*) follows the Affair / Kazusa Normal route. *幸せへと戻る道* (*The Road Back to Happiness*) follows Kazusa True, while *幸せへと進む道* (*The Road Toward Happiness*) follows Setsuna True. Coda Normal already has a one-year-later endpoint in the game but no separate after story on the same scale. These stories also belong to mutually exclusive timelines. A quarrel, homecoming, wedding, or pregnancy in one route cannot fill the blank left by another.[^versions]

Work goes wrong. People who left Japan return to an old house. Setsuna, who once promised that she would save Haruki, reaches the point where she can no longer sustain that promise. What follows depends on who speaks first, who acts, and who can answer or alter the arrangement already in place.

## The Door Chain Remains Undone, and Staying Put Is Already a Choice

On an April weekend, Setsuna does Haruki's laundry, cooks for him, and holds him as usual. Haruki admits that the care satisfies him. Setsuna admits that being needed satisfies her too. Her work keeps him fed and his apartment running. It also spares both of them from reopening the relationship for the moment: one waits for the door to open, while the other inhabits the role of the only person who can save him.

After spending the night, Setsuna decides that her presence has made Haruki's condition worse and leaves while he is asleep. She later tells someone at work that the two of them had a terrible fight. Haruki's breakdown, leave of absence, and treatment are compressed for the outside world into an ordinary quarrel between lovers.

Haruki then asks Takeya what to do about work, Setsuna, and the rest of his life. He says that he has never decided anything and has only drifted to this point. Takeya refuses to choose for him. Haruki knew other paths existed and remained where he was; Takeya tells him that this, too, was a decision.[^door]

That answer exposes the way Haruki has described the relationship. He has begun going out in stages as his doctor advised, and he has resumed written contact with Setsuna. Their arrangement remains unchanged: she still has the key and still insists on visiting. His body's limited capacity and his refusal to state his place in the relationship are separate matters. Setsuna is moving as well. She discusses his return to work and her visits with him, though she still cannot call. Her language shifts from wanting to support Haruki to having to support him, and she lies to her family, saying that the two of them have always loved each other without trouble.

In IC, Haruki promised never to cut Setsuna off unless she asked him to. CC had already shown how he could keep the words of that promise while withdrawing from conversation, kisses, and the ordinary life of lovers for three years. *To You, My Sworn Enemy* places the same practice at a door. Haruki neither retrieves the key nor ends the relationship in words. He asks Setsuna to stop visiting and leaves the act of departure to the person most afraid of being abandoned.

Setsuna's body gives out before the language of duty does.

On the way to one visit, she begins sweating heavily at the station. Her legs lock, and nausea keeps her from moving. When she finally reaches Haruki's apartment, she blames the delay on work and forces herself through the familiar routine of chores, conversation, and care. She wants to look after him. She also fears that stopping will cost her place in the relationship. Several desires carry her toward the door while her body refuses to continue.

Haruki's later demand that she stay away differs from the one at the opening. His own illness has taught him to recognize that she is collapsing too. He wants to keep her from following him down, and he is still waiting for her to resist what he says. Both wishes can be true. The scene offers no purer private intention hidden beneath them.

Setsuna returns to work but remains unable to reach Haruki's apartment on weekends. A television preview then announces Kazusa's Paris performance. The broadcast shows a pianist advancing through her European career. Outside that frame, Kazusa is dealing with Youko's illness, the pressure of the performance, and years of isolation while waiting for someone to reassure her. Television crosses the national border with ease and separates public success from private distress.

When neither Haruki nor Setsuna can move their relationship further, Kazusa is the one who makes the next call.

## Once the Call Connects, Why Speaking Plainly Is Still Not Enough

Setsuna had also been preparing to contact the European agency. Both women wanted to reach the other. Kazusa calls first, dialing from memory after deleting the number from her phone.

They begin with polite conversation about performances, work, and Takahiro. They avoid Haruki and their own distress. Setsuna knows she is unwell but says that she has merely been busy. Kazusa leaves out Youko's illness, the crisis surrounding her performance, and her isolation. Setsuna gives an account of herself that she knows is false; Kazusa mostly withholds. Both protect a facade, but they do so in different ways. The safe subjects keep the call alive a little longer.

Kazusa says good night and almost ends the call. Then she stops Setsuna and gets only as far as "I..." Setsuna names the failure first. She apologizes, admits that she may no longer be able to save "our person," and says that she has failed the task Kazusa once entrusted to her. Kazusa asks how matters reached this point. Her shock turns into accusation only afterward. Setsuna does not explicitly cry "help me" until that attack is already under way.[^call]

*The telephone lets Kazusa and Setsuna sustain an unusually long conflict without Haruki present. This frame shows only Kazusa's endpoint; the order in which they speak still has to be established from the voices and script. PS3 added-story gameplay frame: ©2012 AQUAPLUS / via 次郎JIRO, Bilibili.*

Setsuna first places her failure before Kazusa. After pressing her for an explanation, Kazusa invokes the way she once cried and withdrew, then begins attacking her. Setsuna only then says outright that she wants Kazusa's help. Kazusa must face another fact as well. Entrusting Haruki to the person she trusted and envied most did not end her own responsibility. Neither woman can keep using the other's existence as proof that she has already done her part.

The quarrel soon turns ugly. Kazusa demands that Setsuna give Haruki back. Setsuna refuses. Youko's illness enters the conversation at this point. Setsuna is willing to help Youko and Kazusa in other ways, even to care for them in Paris or Vienna, but she will not "return" Haruki. Her own language remains one of taking and refusing to surrender possession. Kazusa understands that Setsuna's request for help does not amount to a complete withdrawal. Setsuna wants someone else to share the part that has crushed her while she remains beside Haruki herself.

The narration does not polish this direct speech into a victory for honesty. Self-justification, insults, feigned deafness, sobbing, and attempts to pass on responsibility all enter a quarrel that lasts four or five hours. The women say what they want and still use "for Haruki's sake" as a weapon against each other.

Kazusa finally says that Haruki must decide. The sentence cuts across the idea that the women can transfer him between them. Setsuna then says that she will not stop acting even if Haruki abandons her one day. His choice cannot end the other two women's love, hostility, or lives on their behalf. Kazusa and Setsuna claim the decision as "our decision," then part as eternal enemies who love each other intensely. Hostility does not consume the love, and love does not reduce the hostility to a misunderstanding.

The next day, Setsuna says that she is hungry. She starts cooking, packs the food into a lunchbox, and prepares to eat it with Haruki. She tells her mother that she spent the night fighting with a friend on the phone, then states openly that she still loves Haruki as he was, as he is, and as he will be. She resumes her visits and is prepared to move out if her family continues to stand in their way. Returning to Haruki is now a desire she acknowledges, not only a duty that "no one else can perform."

Haruki has already begun going out in stages when Setsuna finds him. In his own retrospective account, medication, treatment, time, and the understanding and help of several people have all contributed to his improvement. Setsuna's care matters, but it is not the only cause. After she enters again, Haruki admits that the notes he left each day recorded his life and waited for her. The story places the call beside Setsuna's renewed action without making the quarrel the cause of Haruki's recovery.[^aftermath]

Neither calls the day a cure. Setsuna is prepared to spend her life on what follows, and Haruki knows that his symptoms may return. They begin moving again without abandoning his treatment or their existing pattern of visits. The after story gives recovery a direction and leaves its endpoint open.

Later, Haruki and Setsuna watch Kazusa's Paris performance and interview together. On the public screen, Kazusa leaves them with "Serves you right." Haruki is certain only that the remark is not addressed to him alone. Setsuna hears a declaration that the two women will remain enemies forever. Haruki hears a farewell carrying love within it. Each reading belongs to a character; the script does not select a single recipient for the audience. Television brings Kazusa into the room, where Haruki and Setsuna can listen but cannot answer her.

*To You, My Sworn Enemy* gives Kazusa and Setsuna a sustained argument without Haruki interpreting on the spot. They can accuse, refuse, ask for help, and admit their interests. Yet the quarrel keeps returning to who should have Haruki. Before the call, Haruki remains beside Setsuna in practice while describing himself as adrift, unwilling to describe remaining there as a choice he has made. Only after Setsuna returns does he admit that he has been hoping for her to come back. The three still do not discuss their future together.

One conflict can break a stalemate. Keeping an ending alive for three years also requires housing, income, medical information, family, friends, and a way to respond after something fails.

## Choices Enter Daily Life: Homes, Family, Secrets, and Broken Promises

Both True after stories open inside relationships that already exist. In *The Road Back to Happiness*, Haruki and Kazusa have been legally married and living together in Vienna for about three years. In *The Road Toward Happiness*, Haruki and Setsuna are married and both work in Tokyo. Neither story gives them another route button. Earlier choices now govern housework, housing, business travel, overtime, and family contact.

### Homes and Families Make the Choice Habitable

Kazusa True appears to make the most complete break. Haruki resigns, leaves his friends and the Ogiso family, and moves to Vienna with Kazusa. By the after story, he is her husband, manager, agency employee, and daily caregiver. Kazusa remains connected to the outside world through her performances, the agency, Youko, and Miyoko. Haruki handles much of the travel, spending, and daily care within those relationships, giving him both the largest burden and considerable control.

Three years of marriage have not dissolved Kazusa's fear of being left. During their first night back in Japan, the two still bring up divorce in an argument. After they sleep in separate rooms for a short time, Kazusa fears that Haruki may truly abandon her. Life in Vienna works, but their dependence is now fixed around Haruki's combined roles as husband, manager, and caregiver.

Continued treatment has left Youko's leukemia stable for the time being. She again draws on the agency, her property, and her personal connections to prepare the couple's return. She restores the Touma house, negotiates with the school, and repeatedly visits another family. On the fifth visit, the family finally lets her through the door, and "that family's daughter" begins helping her persuade them.

The script does not yet name the person who opens this door. It does show the work required for the homecoming. Youko repairs the house, contacts the school, returns to the other family's door, and waits until someone there will respond.[^kazusaafter]

The old house is restored to match their memories, though important objects do not return to their former places. Kazusa's original piano remains in Vienna, and the Japanese house receives a new one. Haruki picks up a guitar, then replaces it on the stand when Kazusa enters. He believes that she understands why he no longer plays in front of her. The couple can return to the house and school. The IC relationship formed by piano, guitar, and voice does not reappear with the setting.

Youko and Haruki also arrange a New Year's Eve wedding in Houjou University Attached High School's Second Music Room, and Haruki prepares a ring. By the time Kazusa puts on the dress, she and Haruki have been legally married for years and have lived together for about three. The ceremony supplies formal ritual and witnesses, leaving them with an image of the marriage that can be kept. The story does not continue far enough to show how Japanese society at large receives them.

Youko keeps the old house as another place to belong. The couple can return for concerts, holidays, visits with friends, or bouts of homesickness. Vienna remains the center of their work and daily life. Japan becomes somewhere they can revisit and hold a ceremony. Permanent exile gives way to a limited return.

When Kazusa goes out on the streets of Japan, she first wears glasses and a hat and uses the alias Kazumi Higashino. She later kisses Haruki where passing drivers and pedestrians can see them. They can show intimacy as a married couple in public space, while her name remains concealed. Their return no longer confines them to a hotel room, but it has not become full public recognition.

The housing change in Setsuna True looks more ordinary. Haruki and Setsuna both work, save money, and prepare to move into a 2LDK apartment. Haruki takes charge of laundry and cleaning. Setsuna treats cooking as the last shred of dignity left to her as a wife. They continue to negotiate housework against their working hours and their ideas of themselves. The Ogiso family helps with the move, and Setsuna's mother has begun attending plays and sharing meals with Haruki's mother. Family contact once severed is being restored, though the story does not offer a completed reconciliation between Haruki and his mother.

Help from the Ogisos still makes Haruki uneasy. From his perspective, the family barges into entanglements he has always avoided and goes about things noisily and inefficiently. He has begun to trust that even when they scold him, they will not expel him from the family.

Takeya and Io remember the three years Setsuna missed, and Setsuna can now ask them to finish telling those stories. Their later disagreement about income, promotion, and parental leave concerns their own future. It is not a childcare plan Haruki and Setsuna have already adopted. These friends can discuss old injuries and also carry unresolved conflicts between work and care into the room.

Youko's repairs, negotiations with the school, and visits to the other family give the couple usable housing, a ceremony, and a limited way back into social life. Haruki and Kazusa know that people and events from their past await them and still agree to return. Setsuna's route sustains its marriage through shared chores and renewed family contact. Both homes also draw a line around who can enter directly and who still arrives as a voice at a wedding or a message in the post.

### Well-Meant Secrets Can Still Decide for Someone Else

Time has not removed lies and concealment from either marriage.

Kazusa avoids serious discussion of Youko's illness. Haruki insists on keeping himself informed and includes Youko's eventual death, when to have children, and the transfer of care in his long-term plans. Youko has discussed her treatment in Kazusa's presence, and the script never says that she and Haruki fabricate a diagnosis. The disparity lies elsewhere: Kazusa evades the subject while Haruki and Youko plan around childbirth and future care without her participation. Protection carries the risk of arranging her future for her.

The surprise wedding is also a secret. The marriage already exists; the surprise changes its ceremony, not its legal status. Kazusa objects first, then states that she will answer the vow. Only then does Haruki contact the friends waiting outside. Avoidance around illness, long-term planning, and a wedding surprise are all tied to efforts at protection, yet they do not carry the same ethical weight.

Setsuna's route has not become a life of total transparency either. Her mother has already begun spending time with Haruki's mother. Setsuna says that she knows nothing about it, while Haruki reads her mischievous expression as evidence that she has known all along. That prior knowledge remains Haruki's inference. Haruki also keeps matters he believes will not alter their immediate arrangements to himself.

Whether a secret removes the other person's power to decide depends on what remains possible after it surfaces: can that person answer and revise the plan? A broken Christmas Eve promise makes the distinction concrete.

### After a Promise Breaks, How Do Two People Meet Again?

Kazusa's after story offers one kind of vow. Before the wedding formally begins, Haruki promises once more to stay with her forever. Kazusa replies that she has made the same vow a million times already and can make it for the million-and-first time. The delayed ceremony is about to bring their established marriage before friends, and they confirm it again in the Second Music Room. The story gives them no comparable breach, so it cannot show what they would do after this vow fails.

Setsuna's after story lets a promise fail once. Haruki and Setsuna solemnly agree that both will be home before ten on Christmas Eve. They know how much history such a holiday promise carries. Then a cabinet minister's resignation produces breaking news for Haruki. A defect in a product released by Setsuna's company the day before triggers a full recall, and she is sent to apologize at shops across Tokyo. Work holds both of them away from home, and both break the promise.

According to Setsuna's account on the midnight phone call, she has not been waiting for Haruki alone at home. Once her own emergency began, she also abandoned her morning demand that he hand off even an exclusive story to his superior. After midnight, Haruki calls to apologize and explain where his work stands. Setsuna then tells him that she cannot go home either. They arrange another way to meet after work.

Setsuna eventually waits outside Haruki's office. They talk again about the considerate lies and absolute vows of their past. They do not decide that maturity means giving up promises. They still say that they will keep their next promise, knowing that they may fail again. This time they explain why they cannot return, change the meeting place, and meet. A breach still leaves another action available.[^setsunaafter]

Repair costs them overtime, a taxi, a hotel, hours of waiting, and a night without sleep. Both remain in contact, describe their circumstances, allow the other to be disappointed, and decide what to do next.

In *To You, My Sworn Enemy*, Haruki describes his refusal to choose as drifting, while Setsuna describes her visits as a necessity. The two sustain themselves through signals and sheer bodily effort. The promise in the Setsuna True after story is no weaker, but failure need not lead at once to self-punishment or silence. Kazusa's after story submits its vow to delayed witnesses. Setsuna's after story shows a vow missing its appointed hour. The capacities belong to different marriages and cannot complete each other.

None of the three stories tests a working procedure for exit. Kazusa can object to the wedding, but the room is already prepared and the guests are waiting outside. Haruki and Setsuna can reschedule a meeting; they never reach the question of leaving the marriage. The existing texts leave open who can depart and how care or contact would be arranged afterward.

The ways partners continue with each other do not tell us how someone cut off from the couple regains an address, a voice, or a reply. Both True after stories bring the third person back by different indirect routes.

### How the Third Person Returns

Kazusa True brings the person who was cut off close again without naming her. An unnamed woman visited six months earlier. Kazusa says that she once loved, admired, and betrayed that woman, and that the woman has forgiven her. When Youko visits the other family, "that family's daughter" helps her. Reading these figures as Setsuna is a strong plot inference, but the script never lets either one speak under that name.

The wedding closes with the voices of "Friend 1," "Friend 2," and "Friend 3." A player may readily think of Takeya, Io, and Setsuna. The text confirms none of those names and gives no basis for assigning each numbered voice to one person. Kazusa says that "she" has forgiven her, while Youko reports that both Haruki and Kazusa have been forgiven. The harmed person never states under her own name what she forgave or what she retains. Kazusa also admits that even if "she" has forgiven her, she still cannot approach that person or forgive herself.

Setsuna True gives Kazusa a different place. As Haruki and Setsuna leave their old apartment, a neighbor hands them a postcard addressed to the adjacent unit and therefore delivered next door. Setsuna reads that Kazusa's Vienna performance succeeded and that she will return to Japan after New Year. Haruki guesses that Kazusa knew the correct apartment number, wrote the adjacent one on purpose, and timed the card for moving day. The intention remains his conjecture. The couple can read her news together without hiding the card.

The couple can now discuss Kazusa openly. She still reaches the household from outside, through reports of a performance and a postcard. The three can work together, and the card preserves the possibility of another meeting. Daily life and the future obligations of raising a child belong to Haruki and Setsuna.

The child sharpens that boundary. Haruki fears becoming like his father and says that he wants a child while still doubting his capacity to be a good father. Only then does Setsuna tell him that she has known the result since the previous day: a test was positive, and the hospital has confirmed it. Haruki states the wish before knowing the result. Setsuna then tells him that the future he has imagined has already begun. The brief difference in knowledge exists, but the scene does not use it to force his answer.

The third person named at the ending is the child. The postcard and baby appear in the same closing sequence, creating two forms of presence. Haruki and Setsuna can remember Kazusa together, speak about her, and know that she will return. The child will enter a household with a home, relatives, and future obligations of care.

## One Blank in the Text, Three Histories That Cannot Be Combined

Read side by side, the after stories tempt us to build a better route out of their parts: take the direct Kazusa-Setsuna conflict from *To You, My Sworn Enemy*, Haruki's firm choice and life in Vienna from Kazusa's after story, then add the repair after a shared breach from Setsuna's. The result appears more complete than any ending the game provides.

Players can carry knowledge across routes. The characters cannot. Each capacity grows from a history that excludes the other two.

*To You, My Sworn Enemy* lets Kazusa and Setsuna speak, while Haruki remains in a relationship he does not admit that he needs until Setsuna returns. Kazusa's after story preserves Haruki's most decisive choice between them, but the harmed person returns only through reports and anonymous voices. Setsuna's after story shows a married couple meeting again after both break a promise, while Kazusa remains outside the household. Each route develops one capacity and preserves the cost at which it was acquired.

Coda Normal ends one year later in the game but has no separate after story of comparable length. Haruki and Setsuna are preparing for a wedding six months away and tell each other "I love you" every day. His feeling for Kazusa, which he believes may last for life, remains in his internal monologue. The work does not show whether or when that feeling enters their conversation. The blank records an unobserved future. It says nothing about whether the author approves of the route.

A fourth route imagined beyond the work would have to begin with all three speaking while the choice is formed. Haruki would state his decision. Kazusa and Setsuna would make demands while retaining the right to refuse and leave. Illness, housing, work, care, public disclosure, and the terms of contact could no longer be arranged by one person alone. These are conditions inferred from the three routes' gaps. They might produce a new common relationship, or they might lead to separation after everyone has spoken plainly.

Those conditions are harder than choosing one woman. The people affected must still be able to answer a decision, and someone must explain what happened when a promise breaks. Good intentions cannot justify making decisions for someone else indefinitely. The after stories reach this point without putting exit itself to a real test.

The first essay began with a voice that had no name and asked how the triangle formed. The second followed three years without a piano and watched absence continue to organize the relationship. The third brought Kazusa back through Haruki's work and examined how four endings distributed choice and cost. This final essay crosses the credits. It asks whether those choices can endure in time, and who performs the repair when they fail. The next volume will test the conditions no single route realizes against analogous structures in Japanese literature.

The door chain, old house, missed appointment, and postcard all carry the past forward. The three continue living inside the different histories they chose.
