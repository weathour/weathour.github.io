The concert had not ended when Haruki told Setsuna what had happened that winter.

He told her about the trip north, about having sex with Kazusa, and about Kazusa's decision never to see him again. Setsuna did not begin by asking how he meant to atone. She asked something smaller: while Kazusa held him, had he thought of her even once?

Haruki's inward answer was clear. Setsuna's voice had reached him more than once while he was alone and intimate with Kazusa. He could not separate the two women even in his own mind, so he had closed his heart on purpose and tried to break himself. What he said aloud was, "I forgot you completely."[^affair]

The lie intensifies Haruki's guilt by making the betrayal sound more complete. If he admits that Setsuna came to mind, he still seems to be reaching for her help, and their relationship may appear to retain some opening. By claiming to have forgotten her, he can turn that winter into absolute betrayal and fix himself in the worst possible place.

He apologizes, then tells Setsuna that she need neither forgive nor accept him. The apology is only self-satisfaction, he says. He will bear the punishment for the rest of his life, even if the weight of responsibility breaks him again. Setsuna understands, but she neither forgives him on his terms nor carries out the sentence he has devised. She stops him several times. At last she says that time is up and asks him to hear Kazusa's final piece first.

The first four essays followed the ways these three people became connected, preserved the absent third person, and passed initiative, closure, and repair among themselves across four routes. [The previous essay, "History Remains After the Endings"](/en/posts/white-album-2-history-after-endings/) reached the year after the Affair route, where Setsuna, medication, treatment, help from others, and time together restored Haruki's life. The second volume begins with that unsettled account. What does a person assume when he declares himself the worst of all? The more crushing his self-judgment becomes, the more readily everyone else is confined to three roles: forgive him, punish him, or save him. Under those names, someone still pays, someone tends the sick, someone stops the harm, and someone goes to work the next morning.

Two works by Osamu Dazai approach this problem from opposite directions: *No Longer Human* (*人間失格*) lets a man write his life as a history of his own disqualification, while *Villon's Wife* (*ヴィヨンの妻*) makes a wife convert a poet's anguish into liquor bills, a sick child, and wages. Neither work selects the correct *WHITE ALBUM 2* route. They turn "Who is most guilty?" into a harder set of questions: who speaks, who pays, who has the right to answer, and who must go on living afterward.[^comparison]

## Turning a Life into One Sentence of Guilt

*No Longer Human* begins with three photographs.

The first shows a child with clenched fists and a forced smile. The second shows a handsome student whose face resembles a blank sheet. In the third, a man sits beside a brazier in a ruined room, his face leaving no impression on the viewer. The outer narrator cannot see how the three faces belong to one person. Only the first notebook gathers them into a single "life filled with shame."

The photographs are not evidence free of interpretation. The outer narrator sees the child's smile as an ape's grimace and the student's expression as something manufactured. By the third picture, he cannot even find a protagonist whose face could be described. He has judged Yozo before Yozo begins to speak. The novel therefore declines to rank objective photographs above subjective notebooks. It first gives us a viewer repulsed by the man, then a narrator repulsed by himself. Each gaze catches something. Each may also confine the person to its own verdict.

Yozo says early on that clowning is his "last courtship" of humanity. He fears people but cannot give them up, so he keeps producing a version of himself that will make his family, classmates, and servants laugh. The mask deceives them, and it is also how he survives. Calling him false overlooks the contact that clowning makes possible. Calling it mere self-protection overlooks the people he uses and the things he hides from them.

This helps explain his dread of argument. Yozo hears a rebuke as an inviolable human truth, as if answering back would open a breach between himself and the other person that could never be repaired. Near the end of the notebooks, he attributes his misery to his inability to refuse. Clowning preserves contact while leaving no room for open disagreement. If others laugh, he is safe for the moment. If they grow angry, he can only sentence himself more harshly in private.

"Taking off the mask" cannot by itself repair such a relation. If Yozo suddenly tells someone all his fears, the listener does not thereby gain a chance to respond; his confession may still turn the whole exchange toward his suffering. The practical question begins after the mask is seen through. Can two people argue, can either refuse, and can a statement be corrected later?

Haruki likewise begins by placing himself in a proper role: correcting mistakes, filling a vacancy at work, treating Kazusa's injured foot, arranging his girlfriend's schedule. The care is real, but he often waits until something "must be done" before admitting that he wants to be near someone. In the concert hall, he casts himself instead as the bad man who must withdraw from Setsuna's life. Whether he performs every task flawlessly or banishes himself, he saves himself from waiting for her answer.

Yozo's notebooks draw a boundary even around their most complete confession. Addiction, debt, and despair drive him to write his father a letter giving the "whole truth." The next parenthesis immediately makes an exception: he still cannot write about the women. What he calls the whole truth still depends on his decision about which people and injuries belong in the letter.

The parenthesis is brief, but its work exceeds that of the long letter. It does not deny that Yozo wants help, or make everything else in the letter false. It exposes the edge that the speaker has cut around "everything." The longer a confession runs, the easier it is for the listener to mistake omission for nonexistence. Yozo admits addiction and despair while leaving outside the letter the women and debts entangled with them.

Haruki says more in the concert hall. He names the journey, the sexual relationship, and the separation. Setsuna is no longer left inside the fiction of an intact engagement. His later self-punishment does not erase the weight of that disclosure. Yet he also says that he forgot Setsuna and, within the same exchange, declares that she need not forgive him, that he must be punished, and that they should not remain together. He gives Setsuna the facts, then tries to settle their meaning and the end of their relationship as well.

*No Longer Human* carries this closure to its limit. After he enters the hospital, Yozo hands himself the total verdict "no longer human." He no longer distinguishes the injuries of childhood, what he did to Tsuneko and Shizuko, what happened to Yoshiko's body, or the ways liquor and drugs shifted costs onto others. Events with different origins and weights are sewn into one identity: he has ceased to qualify as human.

The verdict is so heavy that it leaves little room for appeal. Yozo hears other people's reproaches as the truth of humanity and can scarcely answer them. His own judgment adopts the same voice. A particular act can be stopped, repaid, or done differently. Disqualification judges an entire life's eligibility. The more final the judgment sounds, the harder it becomes to ask item by item: whom did you harm, what does that person say, and what can still be done today?

The novel does not give this verdict the ending to itself. In the postscript, the bar madam first says that a person is finished once he reaches Yozo's state. She then blames his father, and finally describes the Yozo she knew as gentle and considerate, "a good child" even when he drank. These judgments do not combine into a final ruling. The madam does not know all of Yozo's life, and the postscript never makes her an omniscient judge. Her presence only keeps the three notebooks from speaking the last word for everyone who knew him.

The notebooks and photographs also reach the reader by chance. A package without a name or address arrives at the bar in Kyobashi and survives the air raids among miscellaneous possessions. Years later, the madam finishes reading it and hands it to the outer narrator. Yozo tries to close the account of his life, but the paper on which he depends falls into another person's shop, another person's wartime memory, and another person's act of transmission. The postscript need not possess the whole truth to prove something material: once a person has written himself to an end, people outside his control still preserve and interpret the text.

The madam's testimony neither erases the injuries in the notebooks nor establishes the father as the single cause. It returns the Yozo who existed outside his notebooks to the ending. The reader must therefore hold several irreducible figures together: a person who suffered harm, a person who harmed others, someone who courted people through performance, and someone who could be gentle and attentive in another person's life. No one name contains the whole of him.

At the end of IC, Haruki and Setsuna already compete to claim the blame. He says that he betrayed his girlfriend; she says that she first seized the chance to confess. In Kazusa True, Kazusa later assigns the renewed betrayal to her own weakness. Each accusation contains a basis in fact, but they cannot be collapsed into "everyone was guilty." Haruki had made a promise to his girlfriend. Kazusa crossed the boundary with him while knowing the promise remained. Setsuna's earlier confession did not choose the later acts for either of them. Their bids to take everything under their own names flatten the differences between those moments.[^ic]

Whether we explain everything through Yozo's inborn disqualification or pronounce everyone equally guilty, particular kinds and degrees of harm disappear. The postscript to *No Longer Human* leaves a narrower possibility. Yozo need not be expelled from humanity forever, while the acts recorded in his notebooks remain unchanged by one woman's memory of a "good child."

The total verdict has begun to loosen. Money, work, and the child are still outside the center of the frame. *Villon's Wife* sends a creditor to the door at night and converts "Why does this poet suffer?" into "Who pays tomorrow?"

## One Lie, Several Real Accounts

At the beginning of *Villon's Wife*, Otani flees his house at night with a knife. The couple who own the Tsubakiya tavern have followed him there. They do not start with his genius, despair, or character. The husband sits in the ruined room and begins with Otani's first drink three years earlier. He explains how the couple came to the city for work, saved enough to open a small tavern, and watched war, air raids, and the black market change the price of liquor. Aki paid for Otani when she first brought him in. A married woman sometimes left money as well. The rest of his drinking accumulated in the tavern's ledger. His wife learns for the first time that her husband's name travels through newspapers and bars while the bill stays with two shopkeepers.

The tavern keeper's long account has its own interests. He needs to prove the loss to Otani's wife and explain how long the couple have endured it; along the way, he describes the customer who kept winning exceptions from him as a kind of demon. The wife has not received an objective file on her husband. She has acquired another way of measuring him: who paid for each drink, who filled the gap with operating capital, which woman lost clothing and livelihood because of him, why the shopkeepers finally came to the house, and why Otani pulled a knife and ran.

Judgments of character continue to waver, but the ledger makes every relation leave a mark. Otani can be a famous poet, a familiar customer who moves the proprietor to pity, and a man who has failed to pay for years. These identities need not combine into the "real Otani." His wife has to deal with the cash shortage that one of them has already imposed on someone else.

The next day, she has neither raised the money nor devised a plan. She carries the child to the Tsubakis and tells the wife on sight that someone will deliver the money that night or the following day. She offers to remain as a hostage and work in the tavern. The narration says plainly that the promise is a lie she herself had not anticipated.

The woman proprietor is delighted, though doubt has not quite left her face. When her husband returns with provisions at noon, he warns that a promise counts for nothing until they hold the money. Otani's wife asks him to wait one more day, borrows an apron from her, and greets a customer who has just entered. The lie buys her a day and gives her a place to work. She no longer has to wait in the ruined house for her husband. She can bargain with the proprietors, serve customers, and begin to earn money. Soon the customers call her Sacchan.

She has no finished scheme hidden behind the words when they come so readily to her. She first speaks herself into the role of a debtor with a guarantor. The owners' hesitation and the arrival of customers slowly make the role practicable. She puts down the child, fetches the proprietors' rationed goods, ties on an apron, and begins serving. These small acts fill the empty promise with a future in the world.

The work also gives her a life beyond being "Otani's wife." She fixes her hair, buys cosmetics, enjoys the tavern's bustle, and takes pleasure in having tip money in her own hands. Sacchan is a name for the person who works there; the story never reveals her birth name. She and Otani have not registered their marriage, and the child has no legal father. The new name enlarges her life without producing property, marital status, or safety for her body.[^villon]

The child is more than another entry in the account. Almost four, he is smaller than other people's two-year-olds, unsteady on his feet, and often ill with diarrhea and fever. His mother cannot afford a doctor and has to carry him whenever she goes out. At the tavern, the woman proprietor takes him into her arms, watches him while his mother collects the rations, and gives him an empty tin to use as a toy. The wife gets out of the house through more than her own resourcefulness. She brings the child into the shop, where another woman shares the work of watching him for a while.

None of this action creates safety out of nothing. The proprietors first assume the risk of delaying a police report. By placing herself and her child in the tavern, the wife also puts her body under the customers' eyes and jokes. If the promised money never arrives, the consequences fall first on the owners, the child, and her. The lie changes reality; whether it is defensible still depends on who receives those consequences.

The money arrives by accident. That day Otani bursts into a bar in Kyobashi and tells its madam everything: the stolen money, his flight, and the events of the previous night. Fearing police involvement, she advances five thousand yen on his behalf. The husband tells the truth. Another woman still pays.

The old account at Tsubakiya far exceeds five thousand yen. The owner reduces it to twenty thousand, and the wife asks on the spot to keep working until her wages repay it. From then on, she carries the child to work every day. Otani continues to drink there, and she continues to settle the bill. His confession returns five thousand yen to the tavern. Her later wages pay the older debt.

Several women stand between five thousand and twenty thousand yen without being written as rivals. Aki has paid Otani's liquor bill before. The married woman who visits in secret has sometimes left money. The Kyobashi madam supplies cash this time, and his wife assumes the old debt. Their payments keep the tavern afloat and repeatedly spare Otani from meeting the demand himself. The women do not fight one another in public, yet Otani remains the center of this circle of cleanup. Because each woman deals with one consequence, the man who keeps running a tab can continue passing through all their lives.

Otani's confession returns five thousand yen to Tsubakiya while shifting the advance to the Kyobashi madam. His wife's lie secures time and work while exposing the owners, the child, and herself to the first risk. A statement has to be judged by what the speaker knows, where the statement sends events, and who fulfills it days later.

Its status can also change over time. "Someone will bring the money" lacks a basis when spoken and later comes true by accident. "I will pay it all" is sincere when spoken, but the wife's daily work becomes the means of fulfillment. Truth and falsehood tell us whether a sentence matches what its speaker knows at that moment. Responsibility also follows the delayed police report, the child carried to work, and the debts that keep accumulating.

Haruki's "I forgot you completely" calls for the same reading. He knows that he has not forgotten Setsuna. Telling the truth would expose his continuing division between the two women and might let him cling to Setsuna's love. By saying the opposite, he hurts her and pushes himself into the place of the complete betrayer. The answer gives Setsuna no new fact. It protects the punishment Haruki has chosen for himself.

No true face waits behind the lie to resolve this division. Haruki remains attached to Setsuna inwardly and tries to sever that attachment in speech. Both movements are his. One keeps him from forgetting; the other lets him continue punishing himself. The inner narration does not cancel the lie, and the lie does not erase his attachment. The two opposing statements change Setsuna's position together.

Nor does Otani's wife need to be reduced to one purer motive. She does not know where the money will come from. She still wants to save her husband and protect the proprietors, and she wants to escape the life of waiting at home. The work pleases her and also leaves her paying Otani's bills. These wishes support one another in action. Her later pleasure does not prove that she began with a plan, and the old debt does not make that pleasure false.

Setsuna keeps pressing for an answer. Haruki says first that nothing he says can change the betrayal. She concedes that this may be true and asks again: "What is your answer?" She wants to know whether she vanished entirely from that winter. The answer cannot change what happened, but it changes how she understands the relationship. Before lying, Haruki has already decided for her that the question has no meaning.

On the journey back before the concert and in the parting that follows, Kazusa makes another decision. She decides to end the relationship with Haruki and orders him to return to Setsuna. The game intercuts this memory with her last performance. The piano still sounds in the hall while, in another time, Kazusa entrusts Haruki to Setsuna and even says that only Setsuna can save him. She ends her own part in the transgression and assigns the care of Haruki to the woman she envies most. Stopping the harm and handling its aftermath now belong to different people.

Truth is only one entry in the account of responsibility. Otani tells the truth and the Kyobashi woman pays. His wife lies and enters the tavern with her child to work. Haruki discloses the affair, then uses another lie to prescribe his own sentence. Telling what happened, stopping the harm, bearing costs over time, and receiving a chance to answer remain distinct tasks.

## Care Cannot Consent on the Caregiver's Behalf

Kazusa means it when she says at their parting that "only Setsuna can save him." She knows that if she remains, Haruki will continue sinking into that winter. She also knows that his dependence on Setsuna has not disappeared. Kazusa leaves and entrusts to Setsuna what she cannot do herself.

One year later, *To You, My Sworn Enemy* takes apart the word "only." Haruki admits that without Setsuna he would have remained broken, and that Setsuna alone might still have left him broken. Her support, departure, and return worked alongside medication, treatment, help from others, and time. He puts the limit plainly: Setsuna is not omnipotent.[^aftermath]

The collision between these statements shows how the Affair route assigns the days afterward. Kazusa leaves, Haruki falls, and together they occupy the loudest moment of the tragedy. Setsuna is designated the sole rescuer, while medicine, treatment, and other people wait offstage. The after story restores the everyday division of labor hidden by the tragic line. Love can give someone a reason to return, but it cannot substitute for treatment, a return to work, or time itself.

Kazusa has the right to leave and can end her part in the affair. Whether Setsuna returns and how long she provides care lie outside Kazusa's authority. "Only Setsuna can save him" thus holds two impulses at once. It expresses Kazusa's knowledge of Haruki, and it reaches beyond her right by arranging Setsuna's future. She envies Setsuna most and admits in the same breath that she cannot save Haruki. Leaving limits the damage, yet Kazusa also describes the departure as part of saving him. Love, jealousy, and self-punishment pull against one another inside her sentence.

Setsuna is not following an instruction when she handles the aftermath. She loves Haruki, refuses to yield him entirely to Kazusa, and wants to approach him again when he needs her. These desires give her strength to care and make it easier for her to exhaust herself. Supporting him, leaving, and returning are decisions she makes at different moments. The point at which her body gives out reveals a person's limit. Becoming a caregiver later does not erase the injury she suffered as a party to the affair or take away her right to answer it.

Women in Dazai's two stories keep supplying what the men need to live. *No Longer Human* contains income, housing, housework, medicine, and institutional shelter. *Villon's Wife* contains advances, a tavern, childcare, and service work. The men's anguish sounds loudly on the page, while this money and labor are often recast as love, kindness, or women's natural impulse to care. Once the question narrows to whether the men repent sincerely, the people who supply these things recede into witnesses to the depth of that repentance.

Shizuko lets Yozo live in the apartment she shares with her daughter, negotiates with his family and friends, and brings his cartoons to a magazine. Publication and income enable him to buy liquor and cigarettes. Yozo calls the arrangement something close to life as a kept man and reads his dependence as shame. Shizuko's job, editorial contacts, and errands retain their own weight. She finds work for a man with nowhere to go and bears the effects when he spends the income on drink.

The pharmacist's widow offers another form of care. She walks with a crutch and has a gravely ill relative at home, yet she dispenses medicine to Yozo, explains its use, and urges him to stop drinking. She later treats morphine as a less harmful substitute. Yozo uses intimacy, pleading, and unpaid bills to obtain increasing amounts. Her attention helps him through an immediate crisis. Her bad judgment and his exploitation of it expand the danger into addiction and debt. Care is not immune to error; concern, misjudgment, power, and exploitation can inhabit the same practice.

Shizuko has a job and editorial connections. The pharmacist controls access to drugs. Yoshiko does the housework but cannot read the foreign lettering on the medicine boxes, while the bar madam preserves the notebooks. Their resources differ, as does each woman's room to refuse Yozo. The notebooks often compress those differences into a claim that women always want to do something for him, turning their actions into signs of his charm and misfortune. Once we return to housing, payment, medicine, and housework, the women separate again.

The scene of Yoshiko's assault makes this narrative tilt especially severe. Horiki discovers what is happening and calls Yozo to look. Yozo stands on the stairs, forgets to rescue her, and then flees alone to the roof. Yoshiko begins to say, "He said he wouldn't do anything," but Yozo stops her. His long inward passage turns at once toward his own fear, whether trust itself is a sin, and the destruction of his last faith in human beings. The text states that Yoshiko was assaulted and that she thereafter watched Yozo with a frightened expression. Her experience receives only the beginning of a sentence.

After witnessing the assault, Yozo collapses into drinking and suspicion and eventually survives a suicide attempt by overdose. The narration soon uses Yoshiko's body as material for his judgment of his relation to humanity. Even forgiveness enters first as something the husband imagines. Yoshiko never receives equal space to say what happened to her or what she wants afterward. The fuller one person's self-trauma becomes, the more readily another person's injury remains only as proof of his collapse.

*Villon's Wife* lets the wife narrate, yet it still does not give her a full account of what happens on the rainy night. She refuses a young factory worker's offer to escort her, and he follows her anyway. Late that night he returns and asks to sleep only in the entryway until the first train. She permits that one thing. The story omits the process in between and uses a brief passive phrase, that she "fell into the man's hands," for the assault before dawn. She carries the child to work the next day and outwardly continues as usual. That tells us the day must go on. Her limited permission to sleep in the entryway never included what followed.

When she reaches the tavern in the morning, she sees sunlight falling on Otani's glass and finds it beautiful. The story provides no clear disclosure or reckoning. Sunlight, the glass, and the opening of business carry the rainy night past the point where it can be spoken. The words "outwardly as usual" preserve the break inside the routine: the shop must open, the child must be carried, and the husband is still drinking. Continuing to work is one of the consequences. Ordinary time has not pronounced the wound over.

At the end of the story, Otani reads a newspaper calling him a monster. He defends the theft of five thousand yen as an attempt to give his wife and child a good New Year. Precisely because he is not a monster, he says, he committed it. His wife neither rejoices nor joins the newspaper and Otani in debating whether he qualifies as human. She says only, "A monster is fine. All we need is to stay alive."[^wife-end]

The line refuses a verdict on personhood without canceling Otani's debts. His wife still works at the tavern and carries the child back and forth. Neither her legal status nor the boundary of her body has gained protection from the words "stay alive." Life sets a floor beneath which a person is not expelled from humanity. It is not a receipt for unlimited tolerance. Otani need not be a monster for the theft, unpaid bills, long absences, and his wife's experience to retain their separate weights.

The game does not stop at "Setsuna saved Haruki" either. At the end of the Affair route, Setsuna spends one week crying enough tears for a year, then returns to Haruki's apartment and accompanies him through a full year of recovery. Haruki calls her the most foolish and strongest woman he knows. He says that she protects him and that without her he would not know where his life was heading. This is Haruki's view of Setsuna, not a complete account of the recovery.

The return-to-work scene brings the rest of that process into view. Haruki returns to the editorial office, ready to begin again, and promises to repay everyone someday. A coworker interrupts before his first day back turns into a vow to spend his life settling debts. Someone has a deadline, someone else must travel. The welcome party goes ahead, no one presses him to drink, and he may leave early for home before the work resumes. Oolong tea, a fixed departure time, and that day's assignments give him a day he can inhabit. Haruki can then say that he has returned to home ground.

Medication, treatment, and the conditions placed on his return to work do something love cannot replace. They do not require Haruki to prove that he deserves love, or Setsuna to forgive him first. Limits on drinking, permission to leave early, and the day's work depend on his health rather than on whether the couple have reconciled. Under those conditions, Setsuna reaching her own limit no longer means that she has abandoned a man who can survive through her alone. Care can pause, be shared, and be resumed instead of becoming a romantic mission from which the person who accepts it may never withdraw.

Caregivers can gain something too. Otani's wife enjoys her new life as Sacchan. Setsuna wants Haruki to need her, and his coworkers are glad to welcome back someone they know. Enjoying the tavern does not clear Otani's old account. Setsuna's return does not give retrospective consent to Kazusa's assignment. A caregiver may love, need, reach her limit, and continue to reject the people and claims that placed the aftermath in her hands. Her consent remains hers to give, regardless of the care she provides.

## How Guilt Enters the Days Ahead

In Kazusa True, Haruki finally carries his spoken choice into work, engagement, and housing. He does not remain in the concert hall waiting for Setsuna's forgiveness, or wait for Kazusa to stop him. He ends the engagement himself and begins rearranging his job and home. Self-condemnation has not replaced action here. It drives him to execute a decision in the world.

Haruki first submits his resignation to the editor in chief, then tells his direct supervisor that he will finish the work already in his hands. The company has invested in his training, and his coworkers expect him to remain. The resignation imposes a concrete loss on them. Haruki promises to finish his assignments but gives only personal reasons for leaving. He never tells them why.

At the invitation of Setsuna's parents, he also visits the Ogiso home while Tomo has taken Setsuna out. Setsuna's mother arranged the absence so the parents could speak to Haruki without her. He withdraws his proposal, admits that he loves someone else, says that Setsuna has done nothing wrong, and announces his plan to live in Vienna beginning in April. He does not name Kazusa. Takahiro hears part of the exchange from outside the living room. Haruki brings the long-delayed rupture into the family and faces the parents' disappointment. Inwardly, he urges them to punish him quickly and settle the account. Takahiro hears some of the conversation, then answers with his fist.

Ending a proposal he can no longer honor stops a false engagement. The resignation and preparations to move reorganize his working and domestic life around his decision. These acts belong to Haruki. Self-punishment appears elsewhere, when he begins to treat the loss of his job, being beaten, and severing most of his old relationships as proof of responsibility. If he suffers enough, he seems to believe, he can determine the form of repayment for Setsuna.

These acts also reach different degrees of completion. The work does proceed to a handover. Haruki finishes proofreading an entire special issue and transfers his remaining responsibilities to Matsuoka. Matsuoka prepares to take over that week and says outright that his workload will double. Resignation has not made the burden vanish; it has placed it with a named coworker. Vienna remains a plan beginning in April. The Ogiso family receives enough of an explanation to understand the broken engagement, but Setsuna does not answer in that living room. Kazusa True takes Haruki's choice beyond his private thoughts without bringing everyone affected into one negotiation.

The resignation costs Haruki work he loves and costs the editorial office a trainee in whom it has invested. The broken engagement ends preparation for a false future and confronts Setsuna with the simultaneous loss of a five-year relationship, a new engagement, and her family's trust. These costs belong to different people and have no automatic exchange rate. Takahiro's blows cannot give Setsuna the missing conversation. The things Haruki loses do not amount by themselves to the answer she wants.

Kazusa then tries to price her own guilt. She tells Setsuna that she will give up the piano, even the career that is almost identical with her life, as payment for betraying her twice. Setsuna says immediately that the sacrifice is meaningless. Kazusa admits that it would satisfy only herself. Haruki later stops her again and rejects the belief that gaining one precious thing requires throwing away another. If the piano still makes her happy, he says, she should keep playing. Whether she eventually leaves a pianist's career can be decided later.[^true]

This exchange comes closer to responsibility than the thought that a sacrifice has not yet grown painful enough. Kazusa offers what would hurt her most, and Setsuna retains the right to say that this is not the restitution she wants. Someone who caused harm may assume a cost but cannot determine alone what equals the life the injured person has lost. The more magnificent the self-punishment, the harder it may become for that person to refuse it.

"Self-satisfaction" appears twice in these routes. Haruki uses it in the Affair route for his apology and lifelong punishment. Kazusa uses it in her True route for abandoning the piano. Both recognize that they are designing closure for someone else, and neither stops at once. The term does not turn the apology, resignation, or proposed sacrifice into fraud. It locates the problem in the right to set the price. The person who pays may propose a cost, the injured person may refuse it, and the effect of that cost remains for later life to test.

Setsuna refuses both assigned positions. In the concert hall, she will not become the forgiver or executioner Haruki requests. Facing Kazusa, she will not accept the piano because the sacrifice is large enough. Her refusals do not judge whether the others' pain is true. They preserve something that already belongs to her: the way she answers harm cannot be fixed by the offender's judgment of himself.

Placed together, the later accounts in these three works look roughly like this:

| Event | Main actors | Reality already changed | What this step has not answered |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Disclosure of the winter relationship and apology | Haruki | Setsuna learns the journey, sexual relationship, and decision to separate | The contradiction in "I forgot you completely"; how Setsuna understands it and whether she stays |
| Ending the winter affair at parting and completing the performance | Kazusa | The secret relationship stops, and she later leaves Japan | Whether Setsuna agrees to take charge of Haruki's recovery; how Kazusa will bear responsibility later |
| Refusal to let Haruki close the matter through self-punishment | Setsuna | The confession is not immediately sealed by forgiveness or punishment, and all three hear the performance through | How much care Setsuna will later provide, and whether she can withdraw |
| Support, medication and treatment, return to work | Setsuna, Haruki, clinicians, and other people | Symptoms ease over time; ordinary life and work resume | Whether love equals forgiveness; whether one recovery prevents relapse |
| Resignation, withdrawal of the proposal, and preparations to move | Haruki in Kazusa True | The work is handed over; the engagement and family relations begin to change | Full disclosure, Setsuna's independent answer, and long-term arrangements after the move |
| Refusal to use the piano as payment for guilt | Setsuna, and later Haruki | Kazusa cannot let the heaviest self-punishment set the price for the injured person | How the three will handle their shared history and different relations over time |

The table cannot tell anyone whom to choose. It breaks "I will take responsibility" into events that can be checked: whether the harm has stopped, what the people involved know, who receives time to answer, where costs to money, work, and the body fall, and whether treatment and daily life can continue through more than one person.

Responsibility takes shape in particular consequences, not in the intensity of remorse. Remorse can drive someone to end an affair, resign, or say goodbye. Once it hardens into "I am a bad person," it can also let the person who caused harm keep the last word. Someone may offer a cost. The people affected may refuse it or answer differently the next day.

"All we need is to stay alive" leaves this account with a floor. Yozo, Otani, Haruki, and Kazusa need not be banished forever because they have failed to remain pure. What Yoshiko, Otani's wife, Setsuna, the child, the tavern keepers, and the coworkers did and bore still has to be recorded separately. A person can remain among other people while every act retains its own account.

In the concert hall, Haruki tries to set the term of his punishment all at once. Setsuna neither acquits him nor takes up the gavel. She asks him to remain and hear Kazusa finish the last piece. Kazusa has already decided to end the relation, and the script says only that their hands finally part. Setsuna later returns; medication and treatment continue; the coworkers stop pressing alcohol on Haruki at the welcome party. Different people accomplish these acts, and none substitutes for the others. The days afterward no longer belong only to the guilty person's notebook.

This account can now see who paid, cared, and stopped the harm. Setsuna's question, which Haruki had dismissed as meaningless, still waits where she asked it.
