The concert had not ended when Haruki told Setsuna what had happened that winter.

He told her about the trip north, about having sex with Kazusa, and about Kazusa's decision never to see him again. Setsuna did not begin by asking how he meant to atone. She asked something smaller: while Kazusa held him, had he thought of her even once?

Haruki's inward answer was clear. Setsuna's voice had reached him more than once while he was alone and intimate with Kazusa. He could not separate the two women even in his own mind, so he had closed his heart on purpose and tried to break himself. What he said aloud was, "I forgot you completely."[^affair]

The lie intensifies Haruki's guilt by making the betrayal sound more complete. If he admits that Setsuna came to mind, he still seems to be reaching for her help, and their relationship may appear to retain some opening. By claiming to have forgotten her, he can turn that winter into absolute betrayal and fix himself in the worst possible place.

He apologizes, then tells Setsuna that she need neither forgive nor accept him. The apology is only self-satisfaction, he says. He will bear the punishment for the rest of his life, even if the weight of responsibility breaks him again. Setsuna understands, but she neither forgives him on his terms nor carries out the sentence he has devised. She stops him several times. At last she says that time is up and asks him to hear Kazusa's final piece first.

The first four essays followed the ways these three people became connected, preserved the absent third person, and passed initiative, closure, and repair among themselves across four routes. [The previous essay, "History Remains After the Endings"](/en/posts/white-album-2-history-after-endings/) reached the year after the Affair route, where Setsuna, medication, treatment, help from others, and time together restored Haruki's life. The second volume begins with that unsettled account. What does a person assume when he declares himself the worst of all? The more crushing his self-judgment becomes, the more readily everyone else is confined to three roles: forgive him, punish him, or save him. Under those names, someone still pays, someone tends the sick, someone stops the harm, and someone goes to work the next morning.

Two works by Osamu Dazai approach this problem from opposite directions: *No Longer Human* (*人間失格*) lets a man write his life as a history of his own disqualification, while *Villon's Wife* (*ヴィヨンの妻*) makes a wife convert a poet's anguish into liquor bills, a sick child, and wages. Neither work selects the correct *WHITE ALBUM 2* route. They turn "Who is most guilty?" into a harder set of questions: who speaks, who pays, who has the right to answer, and who must go on living afterward.[^comparison]

## Why Clowning Is the Last Courtship

*No Longer Human* opens with three photographs. The first shows a child with clenched fists and a forced smile; the second, a handsome student; the third, a man seated beside a brazier in a ruined room, his face no longer leaving the viewer any impression. The outer narrator cannot see how the three faces could belong to one person. Yozo's first notebook gathers them under the phrase "a life filled with shame."

The photographs already carry the viewer's judgment. He sees an ape's grimace in the child's smile and something manufactured in the student's, then cannot find even a protagonist to describe in the third face. Before Yozo speaks, a decline from forced laughter to a death mask has been laid out. Yozo later uses his strange grin in a childhood group photograph as proof that he was always a clown. The viewer's disgust and Yozo's self-disgust corroborate each other until the three photographs resemble a case history completed in advance.

The first notebook gives that history a harder beginning. Yozo says that he does not know what hunger is. Asked whether he is hungry, he supplies the expected answer and swallows a few sweet beans; when his family eat in silence according to rank, he experiences three meals a day as an incomprehensible rite. Bodily need, family routine, and what other people call happiness do not join up naturally for him. Nor does he know what to say in conversation.

He fears people and cannot bear to give them up, so he "invents clowning." Yozo calls it his last courtship of humanity: a smile held on his face while he sweats inwardly, frantic service keeping him at the edge of the group. As long as people laugh, he knows that he has not provoked their anger or been cast out for failing to understand ordinary life. Their laughter gives him a surer response than trying to read their minds.

The arrangement appears in full when his father asks the children what gifts they want. Yozo would rather have books than the lion-dance mask his father mentions, but cannot say so. His silence plainly disappoints his father. That night Yozo slips into the drawing room and adds "lion dance" to his father's notebook. When his father finds the entry, he laughs, believing that the boy was too shy to admit how much he wanted it. Yozo cannot ask for the books he wants; he can alter his father's notebook in the dark.

At school he learns to arrange laughter with precision. He writes a deliberately comic essay in a solemn style, follows the teacher after class, and feels satisfied only when he sees the man laugh in the corridor. Good marks might win respect, and respect carries the threat that some all-seeing person will eventually expose him. A reputation as a mischievous boy feels safer. Clowning has become a craft with a measurable effect: he composes the material, then follows the audience to check whether it worked.[^yozo-course]

The notebook next recounts sexual abuse by servants in his childhood. Yozo had already learned to clown. The abuse makes him trust parents, police, and government even less to understand a complaint, and drives his existing refusal to seek help deeper. Trauma intensifies the course; it does not by itself explain its beginning.

Takeichi is the first person to break this system of safety. Yozo falls deliberately from the horizontal bar, and the class erupts in laughter. Behind him, Takeichi murmurs, "*Waza. Waza.*"—a trick, a trick. He has seen the act being made. Yozo feels as though the world has caught fire. He continues entertaining the class as before, but secretly watches Takeichi and tries to persuade him that the comedy comes naturally.

He takes Takeichi home out of the rain and cleans the pus from his ear. The care begins as an attempt to win him over; Yozo cleans the ear carefully and soothes him until the pain really does ease. Later Takeichi calls Van Gogh's self-portrait a "monster picture." Yozo paints his own grim face and shows it to Takeichi alone. Outside the public smile there is now a secret image, still dependent on the gaze of the one person who saw through him.

"Taking off the mask" therefore does not describe Yozo's problem well enough. Clowning has delighted other people and kept him among them. Its price is that it cannot conduct a refusal, sustain an argument, or state a desire directly. When laughter no longer answers him, he turns to proxies, alcohol, or departure. Takeichi has exposed the construction, yet the clowning continues. Only the helpers change.

## From Drowning and Overdose to "No Longer Human"

When Yozo moves to Tokyo to study, taking a train, entering a restaurant, or paying a bill alone can still terrify him, so he hands his wallet to Horiki. Horiki bargains, chooses a car, pays, and fills the silence with talk; an occasional laugh is enough from Yozo. Alcohol loosens his body for a while. In his account, sex workers offer him a night of shelter in which he need not stay on guard and can finally sleep. Work that clowning once performed alone is now divided among drink, Horiki, and these women.

Yozo is also drawn into a covert left-wing study group. Materialism seems broadly correct to him but does not explain the human heart he fears; what attracts him is the scent of illegality. The lawful world is cold and unreadable, while the name "criminal" at least gives him a place. He keeps making people laugh, carries out errands accurately, and soon moves from the comrade who lightens the room to the leader of an action squad. Meanwhile, his father sells the Tokyo house and replaces his support with a fixed allowance. Debt, missed classes, organizational duties, and the fear that his family will discover him converge. Clowning still works—so well that it has made him a dependable operative. The refuge has become another society demanding sustained obligation. Unable to pay and unable to carry the duties, Yozo runs and decides to die.

This period is often compressed into "decadence," as though Yozo adopted a decadent creed and followed it downward. The novel is more exact. Monster pictures and fantasies of the artist first give failure a shape; an illegal identity gives him a place; alcohol and sex then suppress fear for a while. Family money, women's clothes, and their care pay for these devices. Decadence here is a supported way of life that can run for a time. Once the money fails, clowning can still make Yozo useful, but it cannot pay his bills or bear continuing obligations for him.

Tsuneko briefly offers another kind of relation. They can sit in silence, and Yozo need not keep making her laugh. Because the night feels almost happy, he releases another joke the next morning, hurrying to put distance between them. Later, after he has fled the organization and meets her again, Tsuneko is the first to speak of death. Yozo still hears an element of play until they buy milk in Asakusa and he opens his purse to find three copper coins. Her harmless "Is that all?" lets the rich man's son touch poverty and shame for the first time. Before entering the water, Tsuneko removes a sash borrowed from a friend at the café and folds it on a rock. The debt must be returned to its owner; the two people enter the sea that night. Tsuneko dies, and Yozo survives.

The attempt immediately becomes news, a criminal case, and a family affair. His father's prominence adds to its news value. Relatives arrive to clean up, police and prosecutors question him, and Flatfish assumes guardianship. Yozo feels unexpectedly safe in protective custody: "criminal" is a stable identity, and being bound is easier than continuing to guess at others. He still performs, adding two false coughs; the prosecutor's calm "Really?" sees through him again. The law does not end the clowning. It supplies a new audience.

The novel later gives Yozo two ordinary lives. Shizuko takes him in, connects him with a magazine, and enables him to draw cartoons, care for her daughter, and earn money from his work. Shigeko calls him Father, then says that she wants a "real father," and he at once hears the child's wish as hostility. As his drinking increases, he steals Shizuko's clothes to pawn them. One night he watches mother and daughter chase a white rabbit on the other side of a door, decides that he will inevitably ruin them, and never returns, in the name of "making them happy." The wish to protect them may be genuine. He still decides the farewell for both of them alone.

Yoshiko's trust assembles daily life once more. After they marry, Yozo gives up alcohol for a time, draws seriously, sees films with her, buys flowers, and even wonders whether he might gradually become human. Horiki reappears with news of Shizuko; old shame returns with him, and Yozo chooses to drink again. A home, work, a child, and marriage have all made his life function. Decline is not written into his nature. Particular relations and resources build these intervals of ordinary life, while old ties, poverty, drinking, and Yozo's flight from them dismantle the intervals again.

When Yoshiko is assaulted, Yozo freezes on the stairs and then flees alone to the roof. She begins, "He said he wouldn't do anything," and Yozo tells her to stop. The notebook spends pages asking whether trust itself is a sin and turns her assault into the collapse of his last belief. Yoshiko begins to fear him and address him formally; no amount of clowning makes her laugh. Afraid to ask her directly, Yozo turns to suspicion, drinking, and control. Clowning has lost the ability to repair a shared reality.[^violence]

When Yozo finds a box of DIAL with the label scraped away, he decides without asking that Yoshiko means to kill herself and cannot read the remaining Roman letters. Inwardly he pronounces her innocent, then swallows the whole box alone. After waking, he decides again that she has understood the overdose as his attempt to die for her; from then on she fears him more. The drowning with Tsuneko was an escape spoken by two people. With DIAL, Yozo takes an intention he has only imputed to Yoshiko, transfers it to himself, and recovers the power to end things through a solitary act.

Morphine succeeds alcohol. The pharmacist's widow wants him to stop drinking and treats morphine as the less dangerous substitute. The first injections do remove anxiety and shame, make him talkative, and let him draw cartoons again. Once the dose rises from one injection to four, he cannot work without it. Credit, pleading, and intimacy buy the drug while debt and dependence deepen.

A plea for help promises the "whole truth," then admits in parentheses that the women still cannot be written. No answer comes. Yozo makes a plan for that night: ten injections followed by a plunge into the Sumida River. Flatfish and Horiki arrive that afternoon, interrupting it before he acts.

Across these three approaches to death, the first speaker, the act, and the people left to deal with it all differ. Tsuneko enters the water with Yozo, yet only she dies. Yozo swallows the DIAL alone after appropriating a possible intention of Yoshiko's. Ten morphine injections and the river form a concrete plan, but his guardian and friend stop it before action. Calling all three "repeated suicide attempts" loses those differences and turns the final plan into an act that never occurred.

Flatfish and Horiki say only that Yozo must enter a hospital, and he assumes they mean a sanatorium. A doctor gently asks him to stay. Before leaving, Yoshiko hands back the injection kit and remaining drug. Yozo answers naturally, "I don't need them." It is a rare refusal in the notebooks, and it comes just before the ward is locked. Only when the lock falls does he understand that he has entered a psychiatric hospital. "Those inside are mad; those outside are normal" forms a loop he cannot dispute. He follows it with the verdict: "No longer human."

Disqualification is therefore more than an elegant name Yozo invents for himself. Addiction, the approaches to death, and danger to mind and body are real. Flatfish and Horiki have stopped the ten-injection-and-river plan, and the hospital then places him in a locked ward. Once the door closes, he enters the circular proof that whoever is inside is mad and whoever remains outside is normal. He also imagines society branding him a human wreck after discharge. External confinement and his anticipation of other people's judgment join into a sentence for the whole life, erasing the capacity to refuse that appeared moments earlier.

After discharge, his family installs him in an old house by the sea with an older maid named Tetsu. During the next three years his lung disease waxes and wanes, he coughs blood, and Tetsu sexually assaults him several times. Nothing here can be named lightly as recovery or care. One day she buys a laxative in place of his sleeping pills. Yozo starts to say the drug's name and suddenly laughs, remarking that "human wreck" seems to be a comic noun. Assault, illness, near-confinement, and laughter occupy the same short passage. The laughter also jars against the deathly face in the third photograph. The line of decline prepared at the opening breaks once more at the notebook's end.[^yozo-course]

The postscript loosens the verdict further. The bar madam first says that a person is finished once he reaches Yozo's state, then blames his father, and finally describes the Yozo she knew as docile and attentive, like a good child even when he drank. Her claims contradict one another, and she does not know his whole life. She merely returns a Yozo from outside the notebooks to the ending, keeping "no longer human" from speaking the last word for everyone who knew him.

Ten years earlier, a package with no sender's name or return address had reached the Kyobashi bar. The madam is sure it came from Yozo. Inside are three notebooks and three photographs: he gives a life over to be read while preventing questions and replies. This, too, may be the final shape of his courtship by performance. The madam carries the package through the air raids and years later passes it to the outer narrator; after reading, he decides to add nothing and submit it to a magazine as it stands. Yozo can write his final judgment. He cannot control how other people preserve and retell it.

### How Haruki Makes Himself Useful

Haruki's method is more respectable: he makes himself useful. He excels at school, argues from principle, fills gaps in other people's work, and clears up their messes. Kazusa says that he is always "playing the model student," and Haruki admits that he once worked to please his teachers. In one conversation with Kazusa, he tells her that his father left and that he and his mother barely concern themselves with each other. In another scene, while persuading Setsuna's parents, he explains his bravado by saying that he is the only man left in his family, then admits inwardly that this family history is also a personal appeal deployed after the sound argument.[^haruki-role]

Haruki really does solve problems. He organizes the last performance of his high-school years and later performs well at university and at work. Recruiting a pianist, caring for an injured foot, and arranging his girlfriend's schedule also give desires he finds hard to state a set of tasks that can be carried out in public. Each task is useful, and each saves him from first saying whom he wants to approach. Yozo remains among people by making them laugh; Haruki enters relations by being needed. Both turn "I want" into something another person needs him to do.

During the three years of Closing Chapter, this method is turned against Haruki himself. He changes departments, moves house, and packs his days with courses and part-time jobs until almost no private time remains. His own narration then strips away the reasons supplied after the fact: he is fleeing Setsuna. He even hopes that she will finally despise him, after which he can allow himself to sink completely. Setsuna neither hates him nor comes closer nor chooses to withdraw, so overwork never completes a breakup on their behalf. Alcohol and drugs steadily cost Yozo his social functioning; Haruki overloads the work and study through which his functioning appears strongest. Isolated from the world, he can still feel happy simply because the editorial office needs him. Their movements run in opposite directions, while the role in each case postpones an answer to desire.

When the role can no longer hold, Haruki sentences himself as "the worst." At the airport, after seeing Kazusa off, he has promised to return to Setsuna but cannot make his feet move. Since he has committed the worst betrayal, he decides to remain the worst person from then on. That sentence sets the course of three years of busyness and self-punishment. "I forgot you completely" fixes him once more as the complete betrayer, allowing him to decide in advance that Setsuna need not forgive him.

The resemblance between Haruki and Yozo ends with the service role and self-condemnation. Yozo's abuse, addiction, two attempts to die, and a further plan form a history of their own. Haruki remains able to work; in Kazusa True he can end an engagement, resign, complete a handover, and prepare to move. A branching visual novel also lets the same past open into different courses, and its script gives Haruki no suicidal act.

At the end of IC, Haruki and Setsuna already compete to claim the blame. He says that he betrayed his girlfriend; she says that she first seized the chance to confess. In Kazusa True, Kazusa later assigns the renewed betrayal to her own weakness. Each accusation has a basis in fact, but they cannot be collapsed into "everyone was guilty." Haruki had made a promise. Kazusa crossed the boundary with him while knowing it remained. Setsuna's earlier confession did not choose the later acts for either of them. When each person takes the whole event under one name, the distinctions between the steps disappear.[^ic]

The "I am the worst" of *No Longer Human* may be the final sentence left after several ways of relating have failed. It records genuine pain and also stitches different events into one identity, leaving the speaker in control of how the story ends. The bar madam does not acquit Yozo. She leaves a narrower exit: he need not be expelled from humanity forever, while nothing written in the notebooks disappears because she calls him a good child.

Yozo's total verdict has loosened, but money, work, and the child remain away from the center of the frame. *Villon's Wife* sends creditors to the door at night and converts "Why does this poet suffer?" into "Who pays tomorrow?"

## One Lie, Several Real Accounts

At the beginning of *Villon's Wife*, Otani flees his house at night with a knife. The couple who own the Tsubakiya tavern have followed him there. They do not start with his genius, despair, or character. The husband sits in the ruined room and begins with Otani's first drink three years earlier. He explains how the couple came to the city for work, saved enough to open a small tavern, and watched war, air raids, and the black market change the price of liquor. Aki paid for Otani when she first brought him in. A married woman sometimes left money as well. The rest of his drinking accumulated in the tavern's ledger. His wife learns for the first time that her husband's name travels through newspapers and bars while the bill stays with two shopkeepers.

The tavern keeper's long account has its own interests. He needs to prove the loss to Otani's wife and explain how long the couple have endured it; along the way, he describes the customer who kept winning exceptions from him as a kind of demon. The wife has not received an objective file on her husband. She has acquired another way of measuring him: who paid for each drink, who filled the gap with operating capital, which woman lost clothing and livelihood because of him, why the shopkeepers finally came to the house, and why Otani pulled a knife and ran.

Judgments of character continue to waver, but the ledger makes every relation leave a mark. Otani can be a famous poet, a familiar customer who moves the proprietor to pity, and a man who has failed to pay for years. These identities need not combine into the "real Otani." His wife has to deal with the cash shortage that one of them has already imposed on someone else.

The next day, she has neither raised the money nor devised a plan. She carries the child to the Tsubakis and tells the wife on sight that someone will deliver the money that night or the following day. She offers to remain as a hostage and work in the tavern. The narration says plainly that the promise is a lie she herself had not anticipated.

The woman proprietor is delighted, though doubt has not quite left her face. When her husband returns with provisions at noon, he warns that a promise counts for nothing until they hold the money. Otani's wife asks him to wait one more day, borrows an apron from her, and greets a customer who has just entered. The lie buys her a day and gives her a place to work. She no longer has to wait in the ruined house for her husband. She can bargain with the proprietors, serve customers, and begin to earn money. Soon the customers call her Sacchan.

She has no finished scheme hidden behind the words when they come so readily to her. She first speaks herself into the role of a debtor with a guarantor. The owners' hesitation and the arrival of customers slowly make the role practicable. She puts down the child, fetches the proprietors' rationed goods, ties on an apron, and begins serving. These small acts fill the empty promise with a future in the world.

The work also gives her a life beyond being "Otani's wife." She fixes her hair, buys cosmetics, enjoys the tavern's bustle, and takes pleasure in having tip money in her own hands. Sacchan is a name for the person who works there; the story never reveals her birth name. She and Otani have not registered their marriage, and the child has no legal father. The new name enlarges her life without producing property, marital status, or safety for her body.[^villon]

The child is more than another entry in the account. Almost four, he is smaller than other people's two-year-olds, unsteady on his feet, and often ill with diarrhea and fever. His mother cannot afford a doctor and has to carry him whenever she goes out. At the tavern, the woman proprietor takes him into her arms, watches him while his mother collects the rations, and gives him an empty tin to use as a toy. The wife gets out of the house through more than her own resourcefulness. She brings the child into the shop, where another woman shares the work of watching him for a while.

None of this action creates safety out of nothing. The proprietors first assume the risk of delaying a police report. By placing herself and her child in the tavern, the wife also puts her body under the customers' eyes and jokes. If the promised money never arrives, the consequences fall first on the owners, the child, and her. The lie changes reality; whether it is defensible still depends on who receives those consequences.

The money arrives by accident. That day Otani bursts into a bar in Kyobashi and tells its madam everything: the stolen money, his flight, and the events of the previous night. Fearing police involvement, she advances five thousand yen on his behalf. The husband tells the truth. Another woman still pays.

The old account at Tsubakiya far exceeds five thousand yen. The owner reduces it to twenty thousand, and the wife asks on the spot to keep working until her wages repay it. From then on, she carries the child to work every day. Otani continues to drink there, and she continues to settle the bill. His confession returns five thousand yen to the tavern. Her later wages pay the older debt.

Several women stand between five thousand and twenty thousand yen without being written as rivals. Aki has paid Otani's liquor bill before. The married woman who visits in secret has sometimes left money. The Kyobashi madam supplies cash this time, and his wife assumes the old debt. Their payments keep the tavern afloat and repeatedly spare Otani from meeting the demand himself. The women do not fight one another in public, yet Otani remains the center of this circle of cleanup. Because each woman deals with one consequence, the man who keeps running a tab can continue passing through all their lives.

The status of a statement can also change over time. "Someone will bring the money" lacks a basis when spoken and later comes true by accident. "I will pay it all" is sincere when spoken, but the wife's daily work becomes the means of fulfillment. Truth and falsehood tell us whether a sentence matches what its speaker knows at that moment. Responsibility also follows the delayed police report, the child carried to work, and the debts that keep accumulating.

Haruki's "I forgot you completely" belongs in the same account. He knows that he has not forgotten Setsuna. Telling the truth would expose his continuing division between the two women and might let him keep hold of Setsuna's love. The opposite answer hurts her and preserves the punishment he has chosen for himself. The Haruki attached to Setsuna and the Haruki trying to sever that attachment are both present; no true face waits behind the lie to settle the division.

Nor does Otani's wife need to be reduced to one purer motive. She does not know where the money will come from. She still wants to save her husband and protect the proprietors, and she wants to escape the life of waiting at home. The work pleases her and also leaves her paying Otani's bills. These wishes support one another in action. Her later pleasure does not prove that she began with a plan, and the old debt does not make that pleasure false.

Setsuna keeps pressing for an answer. Haruki says first that nothing he says can change the betrayal. She concedes that this may be true and asks again: "What is your answer?" She wants to know whether she vanished entirely from that winter. The answer cannot change what happened, but it changes how she understands the relationship. Before lying, Haruki has already decided for her that the question has no meaning.

On the journey back before the concert and in the parting that follows, Kazusa makes another decision. She decides to end the relationship with Haruki and orders him to return to Setsuna. The game intercuts this memory with her last performance. The piano still sounds in the hall while, in another time, Kazusa entrusts Haruki to Setsuna and even says that only Setsuna can save him. She ends her own part in the transgression and assigns the care of Haruki to the woman she envies most. Stopping the harm and handling its aftermath now belong to different people.

Truth is only one entry in the account of responsibility. Otani tells the truth and the Kyobashi woman pays. His wife lies and enters the tavern with her child to work. Haruki discloses the affair, then uses another lie to prescribe his own sentence. Telling what happened, stopping the harm, bearing costs over time, and receiving a chance to answer remain distinct tasks.

## Care Cannot Consent on the Caregiver's Behalf

At their parting, Kazusa genuinely believes that "only Setsuna can save him." She knows that if she stays, Haruki will keep sinking into that winter, and that his dependence on Setsuna has not disappeared. She leaves and entrusts to Setsuna what she cannot do. One year later, *To You, My Sworn Enemy* takes apart the word "only": Haruki admits that without Setsuna he would have remained broken, and that Setsuna alone might still have left him broken. Her support, medication, treatment, other people's care, and time together compose the recovery. Haruki puts the limit plainly: Setsuna is not omnipotent.[^aftermath]

Kazusa has the right to leave and can end her own part in the affair. Whether Setsuna returns and how long she provides care lie outside Kazusa's decision. Setsuna later approaches Haruki because she loves him, will not yield him entirely to Kazusa, and wants to be needed by him. Those wishes give her strength and can also exhaust her. She supports him, leaves, and chooses to return. Becoming a caregiver does not erase the injury she suffered or transfer her right to answer it to Kazusa.

Women in Dazai's two stories keep supplying what the men need to live. *No Longer Human* contains income, housing, housework, and medicine. *Villon's Wife* contains advances, a tavern, childcare, and service work. The men's anguish sounds loudly on the page, while this money and labor are often recast as love, kindness, or women's natural impulse to care. Once the question narrows to whether the men repent sincerely, the people who supply these things recede into witnesses to the depth of that repentance.

Shizuko lets Yozo live in her apartment, negotiates with his family back home, and takes his cartoons to a magazine. The pharmacist's widow walks with a crutch and has a sick relative at home, yet still dispenses medicine on credit and urges him to stop drinking. One woman controls editorial contacts and income; the other controls access to drugs. Their room to give or refuse is not the same. Shizuko's errands give Yozo an occupation; the widow's mistaken judgment and Yozo's exploitation of it turn relief into addiction. Once we return to fees, medicine, clothes, and housework, the women no longer dissolve into the notebook's claim that women always want to do something for him.

Yoshiko's assault makes this narrative tilt especially severe. Yozo freezes on the stairs and then escapes to the roof; when Yoshiko tries to explain, he stops her after the first fragment. The notebook turns to a long inquiry into whether trust itself is a sin, while Yoshiko's fear, her formal speech to him, and what she wants afterward receive no comparable space. The DIAL overdose discussed above is also set in motion by Yozo's guess at Yoshiko's intention. The fuller one person's self-trauma becomes, the more readily another person's injury remains only as proof of his collapse.

*Villon's Wife* lets the wife narrate, yet it still does not give her a full account of what happens on the rainy night. She refuses a young factory worker's offer to escort her, and he follows her anyway. Late that night he returns and asks to sleep only in the entryway until the first train. She permits that one thing. The story omits the process in between and uses a brief passive phrase, that she "fell into the man's hands," for the assault before dawn. She carries the child to work the next day and outwardly continues as usual. That tells us the day must go on. Her limited permission to sleep in the entryway never included what followed.

When she reaches the tavern in the morning, she sees sunlight falling on Otani's glass and finds it beautiful. The story provides no clear disclosure or reckoning. Sunlight, the glass, and the opening of business carry the rainy night past the point where it can be spoken. The words "outwardly as usual" preserve the break inside the routine: the shop must open, the child must be carried, and the husband is still drinking. Continuing to work is one of the consequences. Ordinary time has not pronounced the wound over.

At the end of the story, Otani reads a newspaper calling him a monster. He defends the theft of five thousand yen as an attempt to give his wife and child a good New Year. Precisely because he is not a monster, he says, he committed it. His wife neither rejoices nor joins the newspaper and Otani in debating whether he qualifies as human. She says only, "A monster is fine. All we need is to stay alive."[^wife-end]

The line refuses a verdict on personhood without canceling Otani's debts. His wife still works at the tavern and carries the child back and forth. Neither her legal status nor the boundary of her body has gained protection from the words "stay alive." Otani need not be a monster for the theft, unpaid bills, long absences, and his wife's experience to retain their separate weights.

The game follows "Setsuna saved Haruki" through the next year. At the end of the Affair route, Setsuna spends one week crying enough tears for a year, then returns to Haruki's apartment and accompanies him through a full year of recovery. Haruki calls her the most foolish and strongest woman he knows. He says that she protects him and that without her he would not know where his life was heading. This is Haruki's view of Setsuna, not a complete account of the recovery.

The return-to-work scene brings the rest of that process into view. Haruki returns to the editorial office, ready to begin again, and promises to repay everyone someday. A coworker interrupts before his first day back turns into a vow to spend his life settling debts. Someone has a deadline, someone else must travel. The welcome party goes ahead, no one presses him to drink, and he may leave early for home before the work resumes. Oolong tea, a fixed departure time, and that day's assignments give him a day he can inhabit. Haruki can then say that he has returned to home ground.

Medication, treatment, and the conditions placed on his return to work do something love cannot replace. They do not require Haruki to prove that he deserves love, or Setsuna to forgive him first. Limits on drinking, permission to leave early, and the day's work depend on his health rather than on whether the couple have reconciled. Under those conditions, care can pause, be shared, and be resumed. Setsuna reaching her own limit no longer means abandoning a man whose survival depends on her alone.

Otani's wife enjoys her new life as Sacchan, and Setsuna wants Haruki to need her. What they gain does not clear Otani's old account or give Kazusa the right to arrange Setsuna's future. Care begins in their choices, and its limits remain theirs to set.

## How Guilt Enters the Days Ahead

In Kazusa True, Haruki carries his spoken choice into work, engagement, and housing. He ends the engagement himself, resigns, and prepares to move. Self-condemnation does not replace action here; it drives him to execute the choice in the world.

Haruki first submits his resignation to the editor in chief, then tells his direct supervisor that he will finish the work already in his hands. The company has invested in his training, and his coworkers expect him to remain. The resignation imposes a concrete loss on them. Haruki promises to finish his assignments but gives only personal reasons for leaving. He never tells them why.

At the invitation of Setsuna's parents, he also visits the Ogiso home while Tomo has taken Setsuna out. Setsuna's mother arranged the absence so the parents could speak to Haruki without her. He withdraws his proposal, admits that he loves someone else, says that Setsuna has done nothing wrong, and announces his plan to live in Vienna beginning in April. He does not name Kazusa. Takahiro hears part of the exchange from outside the living room. Haruki brings the long-delayed rupture into the family and faces the parents' disappointment. Inwardly, he urges them to punish him quickly and settle the account. Takahiro hears some of the conversation, then answers with his fist.

Ending a proposal he can no longer honor stops a false engagement. The resignation and preparations to move reorganize his working and domestic life around his decision. These acts belong to Haruki. Self-punishment appears elsewhere, when he begins to treat the loss of his job, being beaten, and severing most of his old relationships as proof of responsibility. If he suffers enough, he seems to believe, he can determine the form of repayment for Setsuna.

These acts also reach different degrees of completion. The work does proceed to a handover. Haruki finishes proofreading an entire special issue and transfers his remaining responsibilities to Matsuoka. Matsuoka prepares to take over that week and says outright that his workload will double. Resignation has not made the burden vanish; it has placed it with a named coworker. Vienna remains a plan beginning in April. The Ogiso family receives enough of an explanation to understand the broken engagement, but Setsuna does not answer in that living room. Kazusa True takes Haruki's choice beyond his private thoughts without bringing everyone affected into one negotiation.

The resignation costs Haruki work he loves and costs the editorial office a trainee in whom it has invested. The broken engagement ends preparation for a false future and confronts Setsuna with the simultaneous loss of a five-year relationship, a new engagement, and her family's trust. These costs belong to different people and have no automatic exchange rate. Takahiro's blows cannot give Setsuna the missing conversation. The things Haruki loses do not amount by themselves to the answer she wants.

Kazusa then tries to price her own guilt. She tells Setsuna that she will give up the piano, even the career that is almost identical with her life, as payment for betraying her twice. Setsuna says immediately that the sacrifice is meaningless. Kazusa admits that it would satisfy only herself. Haruki later stops her again and rejects the belief that gaining one precious thing requires throwing away another. If the piano still makes her happy, he says, she should keep playing. Whether she eventually leaves a pianist's career can be decided later.[^true]

This exchange comes closer to responsibility than the thought that a sacrifice has not yet grown painful enough. Kazusa offers what would hurt her most, and Setsuna retains the right to say that this is not the restitution she wants. Someone who caused harm may assume a cost but cannot determine alone what equals the life the injured person has lost. The more magnificent the self-punishment, the harder it may become for that person to refuse it.

"Self-satisfaction" appears twice in these routes. Haruki uses it in the Affair route for his apology and lifelong punishment. Kazusa uses it in her True route for abandoning the piano. Both recognize that they are designing closure for someone else, and neither stops at once. The term does not turn the apology, resignation, or proposed sacrifice into fraud. It locates the problem in the right to set the price. The person who pays may propose a cost, the injured person may refuse it, and the effect of that cost remains for later life to test.

Setsuna refuses both assigned positions. In the concert hall, she will not become the forgiver or executioner Haruki requests. Facing Kazusa, she will not accept the piano merely because the sacrifice is immense. She preserves her own right to answer: the offender's judgment of himself cannot decide how she responds to the harm.

Back in *WHITE ALBUM 2*, the later account looks roughly like this:

| Event | Main actors | Reality already changed | What this step has not answered |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Disclosure of the winter relationship and apology | Haruki | Setsuna learns the journey, sexual relationship, and decision to separate | The contradiction in "I forgot you completely"; how Setsuna understands it and whether she stays |
| Ending the winter affair at parting and completing the performance | Kazusa | The secret relationship stops, and she later leaves Japan | Whether Setsuna agrees to take charge of Haruki's recovery; how Kazusa will bear responsibility later |
| Refusal to let Haruki close the matter through self-punishment | Setsuna | The confession is not immediately sealed by forgiveness or punishment; Haruki and Setsuna stay as Kazusa completes the performance | How much care Setsuna will later provide, and whether she can withdraw |
| Support, medication and treatment, return to work | Setsuna, Haruki, clinicians, and other people | Symptoms ease over time; ordinary life and work resume | Whether love equals forgiveness; whether one recovery prevents relapse |
| Resignation, withdrawal of the proposal, and preparations to move | Haruki in Kazusa True | The work is handed over; the engagement and family relations begin to change | Full disclosure, Setsuna's independent answer, and long-term arrangements after the move |
| Refusal to use the piano as payment for guilt | Setsuna, and later Haruki | Kazusa cannot let the heaviest self-punishment set the price for the injured person | How the three will handle their shared history and different relations over time |

The table breaks "I will take responsibility" into events that can be checked: whether the harm has stopped, how much the people involved know, who receives time to answer, where costs to money, work, and the body fall, and whether treatment and daily life can be continued by more than one person.

Responsibility must settle in particular consequences; the intensity of remorse is no measure. Remorse may drive someone to end an affair, resign, or say goodbye. Once it hardens into "I am a bad person," it can also let the person who caused harm keep the last word. "All we need is to stay alive" supplies a floor, not a cleared account: no one need be expelled forever for failing to remain pure, while the money paid, the injuries borne, and the care still continuing must be recorded one by one.

In the concert hall, Haruki tries to set the term of his punishment all at once. Setsuna neither acquits him nor takes up the gavel. She asks him to remain and hear Kazusa finish the last piece. Kazusa has already decided to end the relation, and the script says only that their hands finally part. Setsuna later returns; medication and treatment continue; the coworkers stop pressing alcohol on Haruki at the welcome party. Different people accomplish these acts, and none substitutes for the others. The days afterward no longer belong only to the guilty person's notebook.

This account can now see who paid, cared, and stopped the harm. Setsuna's question, which Haruki had dismissed as meaningless, still waits where she asked it.
