It also addresses domestic violence, forced medication, suicide, and a locked psychiatric ward.

In *The Sting of Death*, the husband's mistress edges toward the exit inside the house. Miho orders him to grab her. He blocks the way and holds her; when Miho tells him to strike, he slaps the woman twice. The mistress later breaks free into the yard. At Miho's command, the husband locks the front gate and continues to help restrain her. The mistress asks why he is merely watching. The next line, “Are you going to watch two women die?”, belongs to Miho. The husband writes that he cannot answer.

A girl staying in a detached room has already told them to stop. Police officers then arrive outside the locked front gate. The husband unlocks it to let them in; when the officers order an end to the confrontation, he tells Miho to release the mistress. For the question pursued here, the police perform one limited but decisive act. They interrupt the restraint and violence still in progress. The people inside the yard can no longer keep someone else there.[^doors]

Near the end of the novel, the husband and Miho enter a psychiatric ward together. Once they are inside, a nurse closes and locks the ward entrance. The window of their assigned room bears a coarse wooden lattice, explicitly meant to prevent escape. A door has closed again. This time someone outside the marriage turns the lock in the name of care and protection.

The two doors hold the question that this volume has yet to answer. [The previous essay](/en/posts/white-album-2-who-writes-the-past/) argued that people can retain conflicting accounts of a shared past while remaining bound by facts that no one may erase at will. Once those facts have been preserved, how does an objection alter what happens next? If “no” produces only further persuasion, or “I am leaving” still awaits a lover's approval, neither has yet changed what happens next.

A common world might begin here. It does not require people who cannot reconcile to share one account of the past. It requires something more modest and more material: no story of love may cancel another person's refusal, safety, or place from which to go on living.

## Money and Documents Do Not Guarantee an Exit

Chikamatsu Monzaemon's *The Love Suicides at Amijima* usually draws attention to the deaths of the paper merchant Jihei and the courtesan Koharu. Before they move toward death, however, Jihei's wife Osan has already imagined a way for Koharu to live.

Osan has four hundred *momme* from an Iwakuni-paper settlement. Fifteen pieces of clothing in the household remain unpawned and are valued at another three hundred and fifty. Together they make the required seven hundred and fifty *momme*. Osan means to use it to keep Koharu from being redeemed by someone else. Koharu could enter the household, while Osan nurses the children, cooks, or simply moves aside. The proposal humiliates Osan, yet it mobilizes actual money and housing. It is far more concrete than a wish that everyone somehow live together.[^amijima]

Koharu never receives this new proposal. She cannot accept it, reject it, or say how the seven hundred and fifty *momme* should be used. The three hundred and fifty remains the estimated value of the clothes, not cash already in hand, and those clothes had sustained the wife and children. When Osan's father sees the wardrobe emptied, he immediately takes his daughter away. Jihei later redirects the business money to old pleasure-quarter accounts, expenses, and alms. The money came close to supporting life. Another person still decided where it would go.

This is more precise than a general failure to communicate. The plan exists, and so does Osan's goodwill. What is missing is Koharu's chance to answer, along with a pool of resources that does not depend on stripping the wife and children of what they need. An exit prepared for someone can still leave her outside the decision.

Tanizaki's *Quicksand* reverses the arrangement. Sonoko and Watanuki have no shortage of agreements. They even seal one with blood. The oath regulates Watanuki's marriage to Mitsuko and any future pregnancy. It also forbids either signatory from eloping or dying with Mitsuko without the other's consent. Only Sonoko and Watanuki sign. Mitsuko and a possible child appear in its clauses; Kotaro and both households will bear its consequences. None can answer within the document. While the oath is sealed in blood, someone holds Sonoko's wrist. The more violent the ritual, the easier it becomes to absorb that small inability to pull away into the ceremony itself.[^manji]

Later in the novel, control passes to Mitsuko. She feeds Sonoko and Kotaro sleeping pills and water, lays them on their backs, checks their eyelids, breathing, and pulse, then changes the dosage and restricts their food. They can still speak, but their bodies become progressively less able to carry a refusal through. Only Sonoko survives the ending, and she cannot tell why she was left alive.

The blood oath does not directly cause the later drugging, and the drugs do not enforce its clauses. Power changes hands between the two scenes. Sonoko and Watanuki first arrange Mitsuko's future without her answer; Mitsuko later controls when the two signatories sleep, what they eat, and how much medication they receive. Private rules acquire a new owner, but neither arrangement protects bodily refusal.

One plan fails to reach the person meant to answer it. One agreement reaches beyond its signatories and arranges other people's bodies and futures. They move in opposite directions and leave the same test behind. Resources must reach those affected, and an agreement must allow them to answer. A person must also be able to withdraw a hand, refuse a drug, or actually leave. Otherwise goodwill, proximity, money, and writing can circulate within the relationship until whoever retains the greatest freedom to act makes the final decision.

## The Front Gate Opens; the Ward Is Locked

[Essay VII](/en/posts/white-album-2-beyond-snow-country/) read *The Sting of Death* through concealment, demands for truth, and domestic routine. Here I return only to the final scenes and ask four narrower questions: who stops the immediate danger, who takes over care, who gains power to restrict another body, and when and by whom that restriction is reviewed.

Miho has been injured and has reason to demand the truth. Yet the husband himself blocks the exit, grabs the mistress, and slaps her twice. Those actions cannot be assigned to Miho alone. After the police arrive, Miho lets the woman go and the immediate restraint and assault end. The arguments over who first concealed what, and who turned questions into a test of loyalty, can continue. The officers first stop the danger underway.

Different people take over what follows. An uncle accompanies the couple to a doctor and helps secure an earlier admission date. K-ko cares for the children and plans to take them back to the island. The hospital admits Miho. The police interrupt one scene; relatives arrange treatment and care for the children; the hospital assumes a clinical task. Each act has its own agent and its own boundary.[^hospital]

The hospital's care brings another constraint. After the couple enters the psychiatric ward, the nurse locks its entrance; the coarse wooden lattice over the room's window prevents Miho from leaving on her own. Who may impose this restriction, what danger justifies it, how long it can last, who reviews it, and where the confined person can appeal do not disappear because the door belongs to a hospital. The novel shows confinement and the prevention of escape. It does not supply a complete clinical procedure, so its second locked door remains a question rather than an answer.

Shimao leaves the contradiction unresolved. At the end, the husband leaves the hospital to collect the bedding he will need while staying there. He wraps it, boards a tram, and hurries back. The text ends before he arrives. We never see how he will provide care, how Miho will live, or when the ward entrance will open again. Common life is suspended between a man still in transit and a locked door.

Violence brings the problem to its sharpest point. It may also tempt us to hand every relational dispute to an institution. The recording studio in Setsuna True is much gentler, which makes it a useful test of how far the argument can travel.

## A Song Can Stop; the Three Have No Shared Moment to Answer

The recording does not allow any of the three to halt the session at will. Haruki does not complete a mistake-free run until his fourteenth practice attempt. During rehearsal, the director identifies problems and makes them start again until he approves the performance. Before the formal recording begins, Setsuna suddenly starts to cry. Kazusa calls a thirty-minute break. Only fifteen minutes remain when they resume, and no one can afford another error. The script never shows a completed take being discarded and rerecorded. It shows practice restarting, rehearsal being corrected, and a pause before the formal take.[^recording]

Setsuna has also represented Kazusa in meetings with the staff. Kazusa can veto the selection of songs and the cover design, while the staff work through the night on post-production after the session. Everyone knows who may point out a problem, who may call a halt, who decides whether a performance passes, and when the deadline closes. When the subject turns to their relationship after midnight, those roles no longer help.

Kazusa and Haruki remain inside first, while Setsuna waits outside. Kazusa refuses to leave as a group of three and tells Haruki to find Setsuna. She concedes that he can no longer treat both women equally, wishes the couple happiness, and asks to remain near them as a friend. She sends Haruki toward Setsuna without withdrawing her own love.

When Haruki goes outside, the scene becomes one between him and Setsuna; Kazusa is absent. Setsuna first proposes that they keep waiting for Kazusa. Haruki refuses to wait for the three of them to leave together that night, then proposes marriage and repeatedly presses for an answer now. Setsuna asks why he did not leave “Kazusa and me” more time to work things through. She names herself and Kazusa. She does not request a meeting of all three. Her question establishes that they have had no shared moment in which to answer, but it cannot prove that they ought to have been seated at the same table.[^answer]

Setsuna does not sit there compliantly. She refuses to answer, slaps Haruki, sends the replies she had kept back, and pours out anger and resentment before setting a limit of five minutes. At the beginning of those five minutes, she already makes a conditional promise: when the time is over, she will return to Haruki's room and be the home he returns to. The physical gesture of acceptance comes after the five minutes. It is not the moment at which she first decides. Haruki's narration says that she has severed the tangle of feeling, but this remains his judgment about her. It does not prove that nothing unresolved persists within Setsuna.

The five minutes let Setsuna voice the anger and resentment she had held back and give her own answer. But the interval ends on terms already set: once the time is up, the proposal will be settled rather than left open for revision. Her agency and the asymmetry of the timing exist together. Erase either one, and Setsuna once again becomes a figure inside someone else's answer.

A stronger reading remains possible. Kazusa herself rejects a three-person departure and leaves the next decision to Setsuna. Setsuna herself marks out five minutes and makes a conditional promise. Kazusa's refusal changes who leaves with whom that night; Setsuna accepts the proposal on terms she states. Perhaps the two consecutive dyadic scenes are precisely the boundaries the women choose. The absence of all three from one conversation does not by itself mark a failure, much less justify forcing them to keep negotiating.

The recording-studio analogy ends there. A song is a shared object and can be paused before a defined production stage. Love produces no finished object for three people to approve, and no director can declare it ready. The studio leaves us one useful question: does an objection change who is involved, when the next step occurs, or what anyone does, or does everything proceed as before once the words have been spoken?

Kazusa still asks to remain nearby as a friend and does not withdraw her love. Haruki and Setsuna have made a decision that takes effect, but all three may still live with its consequences. The question now is which of those consequences still require a shared answer.

## Not Every Dispute Needs the Same Procedure

The materials so far present three situations that cannot share one procedure. In the case from *In a Grove* discussed in the previous essay, the evidence still leaves gaps. Koharu in *The Love Suicides at Amijima* never even receives the new proposal. The immediate tasks are to preserve the evidence, distinguish observation from report and inference, and ensure that the people affected are informed and able to answer. Splitting the difference for the sake of relational calm only buries what remains unknown.

Setsuna True presents another situation. The central events are broadly known. Kazusa and Setsuna each make a decision, yet the futures they want remain incompatible. Such disagreements may still require decisions about money, housing, work, care, necessary records, or the terms of contact. Those decisions need a defined scope, an end point, and practical effect, but they have no authority to dictate how anyone must feel afterward. If separation leaves no shared property, care obligations, safety risks, disputed records, or effects on third parties, separation itself is enough. A “common world” provides no reason to prolong a relationship that has ended.

The mistress in *The Sting of Death* has already been blocked at the exit. At that point, negotiation must wait. The first tasks are to stop the violence, secure somewhere safe to stay, define who must not approach and which actions must cease, then assign medical, protective, and legal responsibilities to the actors able to carry them. Danger cannot be redescribed as one more failure of communication.

All three situations can occur within one relationship. They must still be handled separately. Here, “common” refers only to consequences that continue to fall on more than one person: who preserves the evidence, controls property, cares for children, provides housing, or stops a danger. Once those consequences end with separation, the people involved need no further shared process.

## Let Someone Leave the Room First

Japan's Cabinet Office lists several duties for Spousal Violence Counseling and Support Centers. They receive consultations, introduce other agencies, provide psychological support, secure safety and arrange temporary protection in emergencies, and offer information about independent living, housing, and protection orders. A victim need not first agree with the abuser on a shared version of events before seeking somewhere to stay.[^support]

Courts perform a different task when they issue protection orders. Proceedings begin when a victim applies to the court for a protection order. Covered relationships include spouses, de facto spouses, and dating partners who meet the statutory conditions of cohabitation; cases in which violence continues after such a relationship ends may also qualify. A court may prohibit the respondent from approaching or contacting the applicant, or order the respondent to vacate a shared residence; violation carries criminal consequences. The statutory thresholds differ among orders. Broadly, they require prior physical violence or specified threats together with a high risk of further serious harm; the conditions for a removal order are narrower. Ordinary romantic conflict falls outside this framework, and the pain among the three people in *WHITE ALBUM 2* does not belong within its scope. The six forms of order, their durations, and penalties appear in the note below.[^orders]

Counseling, temporary protection, and court orders can connect, but they do not form a sequence that every case must follow. Applying for a protection order does not require prior admission to a temporary shelter. A support center hears the victim's account and arranges immediate safety; a court separately decides whether the statutory threshold is met and, if so, imposes boundaries on approach, contact, or residence. A victim does not have to persuade the abuser before leaving.

The official pages establish which responsibilities the law assigns to which bodies. They cannot, on their own, tell us whether every locality has enough beds, how long an application takes, or whether every order is obeyed. This limit returns us to the ward in *The Sting of Death*. Public power needs defined tasks, durations, and responsible actors, and it must remain open to review. Coming from outside the relationship does not make every lock reasonable.

A common world is no great hall in which everyone finally reconciles. It persists in hard questions: whether someone can get through a door, where she will sleep tonight, who looks after the children, who controls the money, whether a record can be erased, and whether the other person may still approach after being told no. These matters do not answer whom anyone loves. They decide whether a person still has somewhere from which to continue living. While shared consequences remain, no one person's account of love may monopolize them. Once the consequences end, people may go their separate ways.

After the door opens, the question of love remains unanswered. The mistress first gains the possibility of leaving; the later lock on the ward still demands an account of the danger it addresses, how long the restraint lasts, and who reviews it. At minimum, a common world begins when leaving does not await the other person's approval and restraint cannot hide behind the name of protection.
